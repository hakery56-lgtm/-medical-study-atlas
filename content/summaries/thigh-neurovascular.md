# Neurovascular Structures of the Thigh — Exam Night Review

Anatomy lecture (Dr. Ahmed Al-Baghdadi, Anatomy of the Thigh, Part II): **overview of the thigh's nerves, arteries, anastomoses and veins; the obturator, femoral (with the saphenous) and sciatic nerves; the femoral, profunda femoris, popliteal and obturator arteries; the four anastomoses; the popliteal and femoral veins; femoral shaft fractures with compartment syndrome; and the natural tendency of the patella to dislocate.** After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

## 1. Overview and the obturator nerve

- ⭐ **3 nerves:** **obturator** (medial compartment), **femoral** (anterior), **sciatic** (posterior); the proximal compartment gets branches from the lumbar plexus and the femoral nerve.
- **2 arteries:** **femoral** (all compartments) and **obturator** (shares the medial compartment). **4 anastomoses:** trochanteric, cruciate, longitudinal (back of thigh), genicular. **Veins:** femoral and popliteal.
- **Obturator nerve:** chief nerve of the adductor compartment; **ventral divisions of ventral rami L2, 3, 4**; emerges on the **medial border of psoas**, descends on the lateral pelvic wall with the obturator vessels (**behind the ovary** in females), enters the thigh through the **obturator canal**, divides into **anterior and posterior divisions**.

| Division | Course | Branches |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior** | Behind pectineus and adductor longus, **in front of adductor brevis** | **Articular (hip)**; **muscular: pectineus, adductor longus, adductor brevis, gracilis**; **vascular** (femoral artery); **cutaneous** → **subsartorial plexus** (medial cutaneous nerve of thigh + saphenous + anterior obturator) supplying fascia lata and skin |
| **Posterior** | **Pierces the anterior part of obturator externus**; **behind adductor brevis, in front of adductor magnus** | **Muscular: obturator externus, adductor magnus**; **articular: genicular branch to the knee** |

- **Clinical:** vulnerable in **pelvic surgery** (obturator lymph node dissection, prolapse repair), hip surgery and trauma → **weak thigh adduction**, gait instability, **medial thigh numbness/tingling**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 3 nerves (obturator, femoral, sciatic) and 2 arteries (femoral, obturator); 4 anastomoses.
- Obturator n. L2–L4: anterior division → pectineus, adductor longus/brevis, gracilis; posterior → obturator externus, magnus, knee.
- Injury: weak adduction + medial thigh numbness.

</div>

</details>

## 2. Femoral nerve and saphenous nerve

- **Femoral nerve:** **dorsal divisions of ventral rami L2, 3, 4**; appears at the **lateral border of psoas** between it and iliacus; enters the femoral triangle **deep to the inguinal ligament, outside the femoral sheath, lateral to the femoral artery**; **~2 cm below the inguinal ligament** it divides into anterior and posterior divisions.
- **Branches:** **muscular:** sartorius, 4 heads of quadriceps, pectineus (also obturator), iliacus (in the iliac fossa); **articular:** nerve to rectus femoris → **hip**, nerve to vastus medialis → **knee**; **cutaneous:** **medial and intermediate cutaneous nerves of the thigh** and the **saphenous nerve**.
- ⭐ **Saphenous nerve:** **longest cutaneous nerve in the body**; lateral to the femoral artery in the triangle, **crosses superficial to it in the adductor canal** to lie medial; pierces the deep fascia above the knee (**infrapatellar branch** to the skin over the patella); runs on the **medial leg and foot with the long saphenous vein to the first metatarsophalangeal joint**.
- **Femoral nerve injury:** poor **hip flexion**, **loss of knee extension**, **loss of the quadriceps reflex**, loss of skin sensation (**anterior thigh; medial leg and foot to the base of the big toe**), **atrophy of quadriceps**. Causes: **hip bone fracture**, compression between iliacus and psoas by a **psoas abscess**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Femoral n. L2–L4, outside the femoral sheath, lateral to the artery; divides 2 cm below the inguinal ligament.
- Saphenous n. = longest cutaneous nerve; crosses the artery in the adductor canal; infrapatellar branch.
- Injury: no knee extension, no quadriceps reflex, anterior thigh/medial leg numbness.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the course and relations of the femoral nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** From L2–L4 dorsal divisions; appears at the lateral border of psoas between psoas and iliacus; enters the femoral triangle deep to the inguinal ligament, outside the femoral sheath and lateral to the femoral artery; divides about 2 cm below the ligament.

**Why:** Slide "Femoral nerve".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the branches of the femoral nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** Muscular (sartorius, quadriceps heads, pectineus, iliacus), articular (hip via rectus femoris nerve, knee via vastus medialis nerve) and cutaneous (medial and intermediate cutaneous nerves of the thigh, saphenous nerve).

**Why:** Slide "Branches of the femoral nerve".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the saphenous nerve and the effects of femoral nerve injury.</summary>

**Answer:** The longest cutaneous nerve: crosses superficial to the femoral artery in the adductor canal, gives the infrapatellar branch above the knee and runs with the long saphenous vein to the first MTP joint. Femoral nerve injury: weak hip flexion, loss of knee extension and quadriceps reflex, sensory loss on the anterior thigh and medial leg/foot, quadriceps atrophy.

**Why:** Slides "Saphenous nerve", "Injury of femoral nerve".
</details>

## 3. Sciatic nerve

- ⭐ **Largest nerve in the body, 2 cm wide; L4–S3.**
- **Motor:** **directly** the posterior thigh muscles (biceps femoris, semimembranosus, semitendinosus) and the **hamstring part of adductor magnus**; **indirectly (terminal branches)** all muscles of the leg and foot; **no muscle in the gluteal region**. **Sensory:** terminal branches supply the skin of the **lateral leg, heel and both surfaces of the foot**.
- **Course:** emerges **inferior to piriformis**, descends inferolaterally, crosses the **lateral rotators** in the gluteal region, enters the posterior thigh **deep to the long head of biceps femoris**, and at the apex of the popliteal fossa divides into the **tibial and common peroneal nerves**.
- **Injury:** weak knee flexion, **foot drop**, calf wasting, **steppage gait**, **loss of the Achilles reflex**, sensory loss below the knee (the slide's "except?" is left incomplete; standard teaching is the medial leg/foot via the saphenous nerve), possible **trophic ulcer**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sciatic n.: largest nerve, L4–S3, below piriformis, deep to the long head of biceps; no gluteal muscle supply.
- Divides at the popliteal fossa apex into tibial + common peroneal.
- Injury: foot drop, steppage gait, Achilles reflex lost.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the motor and sensory distribution of the sciatic nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** Directly supplies the hamstrings and the hamstring part of adductor magnus; via its terminal branches all leg and foot muscles; none in the gluteal region. Sensory: lateral leg, heel, dorsal and plantar surfaces of the foot.

**Why:** Slide "Sciatic nerve".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the course of the sciatic nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** Emerges below piriformis, crosses the lateral rotators, enters the posterior thigh deep to the long head of biceps femoris and divides at the apex of the popliteal fossa into the tibial and common peroneal nerves.

**Why:** Slide "Sciatic nerve" (course).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the features of sciatic nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Weak knee flexion, foot drop, calf wasting, steppage gait, loss of the Achilles reflex, sensory loss below the knee and possible trophic ulcers.

**Why:** Slide "Sciatic nerve injury".
</details>

## 4. Femoral, profunda femoris and obturator arteries

- ⭐ **Femoral artery:** begins at the **mid-inguinal point** (midway between symphysis pubis and ASIS), behind the inguinal ligament, as the continuation of the **external iliac artery**; ends at the **junction of the upper 2/3 and lower 1/3 of the thigh** through the **adductor hiatus** as the **popliteal artery**. **Upper half superficial (femoral triangle), lower half deep (adductor canal)**; upper 1.5 inches in the **lateral compartment of the femoral sheath**; **pulse 2–3 cm below the mid-inguinal point**; the **femoral head lies behind it, separated by psoas**.
- **Branches in the triangle:** **3 superficial** (superficial epigastric, superficial external pudendal, superficial circumflex iliac) and **3 deep** (profunda femoris, deep external pudendal, muscular). **In the adductor canal:** muscular branches and the **descending genicular artery**.
- **Profunda femoris (deep femoral):** **largest branch**; **chief supply to muscles of all three compartments**; branches: **4 perforating arteries, lateral femoral circumflex, medial femoral circumflex**.
- **Obturator artery:** from the **anterior division of the internal iliac** in the pelvis; enters the adductor compartment through the **obturator canal**; muscular branches and an **articular branch to the hip**.
- Note: the slide says the femoral vein is medial to the artery and "at the base of the triangle it becomes posterior"; textbooks place the vein medial at the base and posterior toward the apex/adductor canal. Do not build answers on this wording.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Femoral artery: mid-inguinal point → adductor hiatus; upper half in the triangle, lower half in the canal.
- 3 superficial + 3 deep branches; profunda femoris = largest, supplies all 3 compartments.
- Obturator artery: internal iliac → obturator canal → hip articular branch.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the origin, course and termination of the femoral artery.</summary>

**Answer:** It begins at the mid-inguinal point as the continuation of the external iliac artery, runs in the femoral triangle (upper half) and adductor canal (lower half), and ends at the junction of the upper two-thirds and lower third of the thigh by passing through the adductor hiatus to become the popliteal artery.

**Why:** Slide "Femoral artery".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the branches of the femoral artery.</summary>

**Answer:** In the triangle: three superficial (superficial epigastric, superficial external pudendal, superficial circumflex iliac) and three deep (profunda femoris, deep external pudendal, muscular). In the adductor canal: muscular branches and the descending genicular artery.

**Why:** Slide "Femoral artery – branches".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the profunda femoris artery and what are its branches?</summary>

**Answer:** The largest branch of the femoral artery and chief supply of all three thigh compartments; gives four perforating arteries and the lateral and medial femoral circumflex arteries, which share in the anastomoses.

**Why:** Slide "Profunda femoris artery".
</details>

## 5. Femoral anastomoses

- **Anastomosis** = collateral circulation giving an **alternative route** when the regional one is blocked; in the lower limb it connects the **internal iliac** with the **femoral (external iliac)** artery.
- ⭐ **Four anastomoses:** **trochanteric, cruciate, longitudinal (back of the thigh), genicular.**

| Anastomosis | Details |
|---|---|
| **Trochanteric** | **Floor of the trochanteric fossa**; formed by **superior gluteal, inferior gluteal, ascending branches of the lateral and medial circumflex femoral arteries**; supplies **retinacular arteries to the femoral head**; **avascular necrosis** = bone death from loss of blood supply (**medial femoral circumflex** gives the largest contribution) |
| **Genicular** | Around the knee; branches of **popliteal, anterior tibial, posterior tibial, femoral and profunda femoris**; keeps the knee and leg supplied **during knee flexion when the popliteal artery is kinked** |

- **Clinical (atherosclerosis):** **proximal femoral block** → **cruciate and trochanteric** anastomoses; **distal femoral block** → **perforating branches of profunda femoris** + popliteal branches; **gradual popliteal block** → **genicular** anastomosis and widened collaterals.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four anastomoses: trochanteric, cruciate, longitudinal, genicular.
- Trochanteric → femoral head supply (medial circumflex = most important); avascular necrosis.
- Proximal block → cruciate/trochanteric; distal block → profunda perforators; popliteal block → genicular.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define anastomosis and name the four femoral anastomoses.</summary>

**Answer:** A collateral circulation giving an alternative route when the regional vessel is blocked. The four are trochanteric, cruciate, longitudinal (back of thigh) and genicular.

**Why:** Slides "Femoral anastomoses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the trochanteric anastomosis and its importance.</summary>

**Answer:** It lies in the floor of the trochanteric fossa and is formed by the superior and inferior gluteal arteries and the ascending branches of the lateral and medial circumflex femoral arteries. It supplies retinacular arteries to the femoral head and links the internal iliac with the femoral artery; the medial circumflex femoral artery contributes most, so its loss risks avascular necrosis.

**Why:** Slide "Trochanteric anastomosis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does the circulation of the limb survive a femoral or popliteal artery block?</summary>

**Answer:** Proximal femoral block: cruciate and trochanteric anastomoses. Distal femoral block: perforating branches of profunda femoris with popliteal branches. Gradual popliteal block: genicular anastomosis and enlarged collateral vessels.

**Why:** Slides "Clinical importance of femoral anastomoses".
</details>

## 6. Popliteal vessels and femoral vein

- **Popliteal artery:** continuation of the femoral at the **adductor hiatus**; ends at the **lower border of popliteus** into the **anterior and posterior tibial arteries**. **Anterior relations:** popliteal surface of femur, knee joint, popliteus; **posterior:** popliteal vein, tibial nerve, fascia, skin. **Branches:** muscular (hamstrings, calf), **5 genicular**, **sural** (small cutaneous, with the sural nerve), 2 terminal.
- **Popliteal vein:** starts at the lower border of popliteus from the **venae comitantes** of the anterior and posterior tibial arteries; ascends **superficial to the artery**, crossing it **medial to lateral**; continues as the **femoral vein at the adductor hiatus**; tributaries: **small saphenous vein** and veins matching the artery's branches.
- **Femoral vein:** continuation of the popliteal vein; ends behind the inguinal ligament as the **external iliac vein**; tributaries match the femoral artery's branches and it receives the **great saphenous vein**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Popliteal artery ends at the lower border of popliteus → anterior + posterior tibial arteries; 5 genicular branches.
- Popliteal vein crosses the artery medial → lateral; receives the small saphenous vein.
- Femoral vein → external iliac vein; receives the great saphenous vein.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the popliteal artery (origin, relations, branches).</summary>

**Answer:** Continues from the femoral artery at the adductor hiatus; anterior to it are the popliteal surface of the femur, the knee joint and popliteus; posterior are the popliteal vein, tibial nerve, fascia and skin. Branches: muscular, 5 genicular, sural and the anterior and posterior tibial arteries.

**Why:** Slide "Popliteal artery".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the popliteal vein.</summary>

**Answer:** It begins at the lower border of popliteus by the union of the venae comitantes of the tibial arteries, ascends superficial to the artery crossing it from medial to lateral, receives the small saphenous vein and continues as the femoral vein at the adductor hiatus.

**Why:** Slide "Popliteal vein".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the femoral vein and the obturator artery.</summary>

**Answer:** The femoral vein continues the popliteal vein, ends behind the inguinal ligament as the external iliac vein and receives the great saphenous vein. The obturator artery arises from the anterior division of the internal iliac artery and enters the thigh via the obturator canal, giving muscular and hip articular branches.

**Why:** Slides "Femoral vein", "Obturator artery".
</details>

## 7. Femoral shaft fractures and patellar dislocation

- **Femoral shaft fractures:** **high-energy injuries** (young; associated pulmonary/cerebral injuries); low-energy in the elderly. **Complications:** displacement, **femoral nerve/artery injury**, **massive haemorrhage (closed 1000–1500 ml; open may be double)**, shortened limb with tense swollen thigh, **fat embolism**, **compartment syndrome**.
- **Muscle pulls:** **flexors → lesser trochanter, abductors → greater trochanter, adductors → linea aspera, gastrocnemius → lower femur.**
  - **Upper third:** proximal segment **flexed and laterally rotated (iliopsoas), abducted (gluteus medius/minimus)**; distal segment pulled **medially by adductors**; shortening by spasm.
  - **Middle third:** proximal **flexed (iliopsoas) and adducted**; distal **posteriorly by gastrocnemius**.
  - **Supracondylar:** distal fragment **tilted backwards by gastrocnemius**; sharp edge may tear the **popliteal artery**; **knee flexion** can correct it non-operatively.
- **Fascia lata:** tough deep fascia enveloping the thigh; features: **iliotibial tract** and **saphenous opening**.
- ⭐ **Compartment syndrome:** raised pressure in a closed compartment (usually **anterior thigh**); **5 Ps** (pain/swelling, pulselessness, paresthesia, pallor, paralysis) — **high suspicion, do not wait for all 5 Ps**; **urgent fasciotomy**.
- **Patella:** the **Q angle** (quadriceps pull vs femoral shaft) gives a **natural tendency for lateral dislocation**, prevented by: **forward projection of the lateral femoral condyle**, **stronger pull of vastus medialis oblique than vastus lateralis**, and the **medial patellar retinaculum** (attached to the whole medial margin).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Femoral shaft fracture: 1000–1500 ml blood loss, fat embolism, compartment syndrome.
- Displacement depends on the site; supracondylar → popliteal artery risk.
- Compartment syndrome → fasciotomy; Q angle → lateral patellar dislocation prevented by 3 factors.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the anatomical complications of a femoral shaft fracture.</summary>

**Answer:** Displacement of fragments, injury to the femoral nerve and artery, massive haemorrhage (1000–1500 ml closed, double if open), a shortened tense swollen thigh, fat embolism and compartment syndrome.

**Why:** Slide "Femoral shaft fractures".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the displacement in upper-third, middle-third and supracondylar fractures.</summary>

**Answer:** Upper third: proximal fragment flexed, laterally rotated and abducted; distal pulled medially. Middle third: proximal flexed and adducted; distal pulled posteriorly by gastrocnemius. Supracondylar: distal fragment tilted backwards by gastrocnemius, risking the popliteal artery.

**Why:** Slides "Mechanism of displacement".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is compartment syndrome and how is the patella kept from dislocating?</summary>

**Answer:** Compartment syndrome: pressure rise in a closed compartment with the 5 Ps; urgent fasciotomy. The Q angle tends to dislocate the patella laterally; it is prevented by the lateral femoral condyle's forward projection, the stronger pull of vastus medialis oblique, and the medial patellar retinaculum.

**Why:** Slides "Compartment syndrome", "Natural tendency of patella to dislocate".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Obturator nerve: anterior division lies IN FRONT of adductor brevis; posterior division BEHIND it (in front of adductor magnus).**
- **Femoral nerve is OUTSIDE the femoral sheath, lateral to the artery.**
- **Saphenous nerve = longest cutaneous nerve; it crosses the artery superficially in the adductor canal.**
- **Sciatic nerve gives NO gluteal muscle branches; it divides at the apex of the popliteal fossa.**
- **Femoral artery ends at the junction of the upper 2/3 and lower 1/3 of the thigh (adductor hiatus).**
- **Medial femoral circumflex artery = largest contribution to the femoral head; trochanteric anastomosis = retinacular arteries.**
- **Proximal femoral block → cruciate + trochanteric; distal → profunda perforators; popliteal → genicular.**
- **Supracondylar fracture: gastrocnemius tilts the distal fragment backwards → popliteal artery risk.**
