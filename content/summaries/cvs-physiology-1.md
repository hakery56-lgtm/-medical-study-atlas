## 1. Functions of the heart & the two circuits

### Functions of the heart
1. **Producing blood pressure.**
2. **Directing blood flow** by distinguishing between the **pulmonary and systemic** circulations.
3. **Maintaining unidirectional blood flow** through the **valves**.
4. **Controlling blood distribution.**
5. **Adjusting contraction rate and strength** to match blood delivery to varying metabolic demands.

### The two circuits
| Circuit | Role |
|---|---|
| **Pulmonary** | transports blood **between the heart and the lungs** (right side of the heart) |
| **Systemic** | circulates blood **between the heart and the rest of the body** (left side of the heart) |

### Vessels
- **Arteries** carry blood **away from the heart**; **veins** return blood **to the heart**; **capillaries** exchange **gases, nutrients and wastes**.

### Blood flow through the heart (pulmonary circuit)
- **Right ventricle (RV)** receives blood from the **right atrium** and pumps it to the lungs via the **pulmonary trunk (PT)**.
- The PT branches into the **right and left pulmonary arteries (PA)** — carry **deoxygenated** blood to the lungs, where the blood **gives up CO₂ and picks up O₂**.
- **Pulmonary veins (PV)** carry **oxygenated** blood from the lungs to the heart (left atrium).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Heart functions: produces pressure, separates pulmonary and systemic flow, unidirectional flow (valves), controls distribution, adjusts rate and strength.
- Pulmonary circuit = heart ↔ lungs; systemic circuit = heart ↔ body.
- Arteries away from the heart, veins to the heart, capillaries exchange.
- RV → pulmonary trunk → pulmonary arteries (deoxygenated) → lungs → pulmonary veins (oxygenated) → left atrium.

</div>

**Answer:** Producing blood pressure; directing blood flow by separating the pulmonary and systemic circulations; maintaining unidirectional flow through the valves; controlling blood distribution; and adjusting the rate and strength of contraction to the metabolic demands.

**Why:** Five functions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the two circuits of the cardiovascular system.</summary>

</details>

**Why:** A simple pathway question.
</details>

## 2. Pulmonary vs systemic circulation

| | **Pulmonary** | **Systemic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Resistance** | **low** | **high** |
| **Pressure** | **low — 25/10 mmHg** | **high — 120/80 mmHg** |

- The two circulations are **parallel subcircuits** with **unidirectional flow**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pulmonary: low resistance, low pressure (25/10 mmHg). Systemic: high resistance, high pressure (120/80 mmHg).
- They are parallel subcircuits with unidirectional flow.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the pulmonary and systemic circulations.</summary>

**Answer:** The pulmonary circulation has low resistance and low pressure (about 25/10 mmHg), whereas the systemic circulation has high resistance and high pressure (about 120/80 mmHg); both are parallel subcircuits with unidirectional flow.

**Why:** Two rows, four numbers/words.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the normal pulmonary arterial pressure?</summary>

**Answer:** About 25 mmHg systolic and 10 mmHg diastolic (25/10 mmHg).

**Why:** Compare with 120/80.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. In what sense are the two circuits "parallel"?</summary>

**Answer:** Each side of the heart pumps blood simultaneously through its own circuit in a single direction, and the two circuits work side by side (the output of each ends as the input of the other).

**Why:** Parallel subcircuits with unidirectional flow.
</details>

## 3. The heart valves

### Atrioventricular (AV) valves
| Side | Valve | Between |
|---|---|---|
| **Right** | ⭐ **tricuspid** | right atrium and right ventricle |
| **Left** | ⭐ **mitral (bicuspid)** | left atrium and left ventricle |

- Allow **one-way flow** and **prevent backflow (regurgitation)** into the atria during ventricular contraction.
- **Semilunar valves:** **pulmonary** and **aortic**.

### Support structures
- **Papillary muscles:** small muscular projections from the ventricular walls.
- **Chordae tendineae ("heart strings")** connect the papillary muscles to the valve leaflets.
- Papillary muscles **contract with the ventricular wall**, **tightening the chordae** → **prevent eversion/prolapse** of the cusps back into the atria → **one-way flow** (atria → ventricles → great arteries) and **no regurgitation despite high ventricular pressure**.
- **Papillary muscle dysfunction** (ischaemia, **rupture after myocardial infarction**) → **valvular insufficiency** → regurgitation and heart-failure symptoms.

### Valve function — a **passive** process
- **Open** when a **forward pressure gradient** (atria > ventricles) pushes blood forward.
- **Close** when a **backward pressure gradient** (ventricles > atria) pushes blood back.
- ⭐ **AV valves do not contract actively** — they respond passively to pressure gradients.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Tricuspid = right AV valve; mitral (bicuspid) = left AV valve; both prevent regurgitation into the atria.
- Papillary muscles + chordae tendineae stop the cusps from prolapsing.
- Valves open and close passively with pressure gradients (atria > ventricles = open; ventricles > atria = closed).
- Papillary muscle ischaemia/rupture → regurgitation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name and locate the atrioventricular valves and state their function.</summary>

**Answer:** The tricuspid valve lies between the right atrium and right ventricle; the mitral (bicuspid) valve lies between the left atrium and left ventricle. They allow one-way flow from atria to ventricles and prevent backflow (regurgitation) into the atria during ventricular contraction.

**Why:** Name + position + function.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the support structures of the AV valves and their importance.</summary>

**Answer:** Papillary muscles project from the ventricular walls and are connected to the valve leaflets by the chordae tendineae ("heart strings"). They contract with the ventricle, tighten the chordae and prevent the cusps from everting into the atria, so blood cannot regurgitate despite the high ventricular pressure. Ischaemia or rupture of a papillary muscle after myocardial infarction causes valvular insufficiency, regurgitation and heart-failure symptoms.

**Why:** Anatomy + function + clinical.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do the AV valves open and close?</summary>

**Answer:** Passively: they open when atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure (forward gradient) and close when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure (backward gradient); they do not contract actively.

**Why:** "Passive" is the key word.
</details>

## 4. Cardiac muscle

### Types
The heart has **three major types** of cardiac muscle: **atrial muscle**, **ventricular muscle** and **specialized excitatory and conductive fibers**.

### Structure
- Atrial and ventricular fibers contract **like skeletal muscle but for much longer**.
- Arranged in a **latticework** (fibers divide, recombine and spread again).
- **Striated** like skeletal muscle, with typical **myofibrils** (actin and myosin filaments sliding side by side).

### Intercalated disks
- Specialized **cell-to-cell contacts**; **membranes interdigitate**.
- ⭐ **Desmosomes** hold the cells together.
- ⭐ **Gap junctions** let **action potentials pass** from cell to cell.
- Atria and ventricles each behave **electrically as a single unit**.

### Mitochondria
- **30% of the cell volume** (vs **2% in skeletal muscle**).

### Cardiac muscle does not fatigue — 3 reasons
1. **Rich blood supply** (oxygen-rich tissue).
2. **High number of mitochondria** and efficient waste removal.
3. **Myoglobin** (oxygen-storing pigment).

### Metabolism
- Uses a **variety of substrates** (fats, carbohydrates, proteins) for oxidation; **main ATP source = mitochondrial aerobic respiration**.
- When a region is **oxygen-starved (ischaemia, e.g. heart attack)**, cells metabolize **anaerobically**, using **glycogen** and producing **lactic acid**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Three types of cardiac muscle: atrial, ventricular, specialized excitatory/conductive fibers.
- Striated, latticework arrangement, same actin–myosin sliding as skeletal muscle.
- Intercalated disks: desmosomes (adhesion) + gap junctions (electrical spread) → functional unit.
- Mitochondria = 30% of cell volume (2% in skeletal muscle); no fatigue because of blood supply, mitochondria, myoglobin.
- Ischaemia → anaerobic metabolism of glycogen → lactic acid.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of cardiac muscle.</summary>

**Answer:** The heart has atrial muscle, ventricular muscle and specialized excitatory and conductive fibers. The fibers are striated and arranged in a latticework, with typical myofibrils whose actin and myosin filaments slide as in skeletal muscle but contract for a much longer duration. Cells are joined by intercalated disks containing desmosomes that hold the cells together and gap junctions that allow action potentials to pass between cells; mitochondria occupy 30% of the cell volume compared with 2% in skeletal muscle.

**Why:** Types + structure + disks + mitochondria.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does cardiac muscle not undergo fatigue?</summary>

**Answer:** Because of its rich blood supply (oxygen-rich tissue), its high number of mitochondria with efficient removal of waste products, and the presence of myoglobin, the oxygen-storing pigment.

**Why:** Three reasons exactly as in the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What happens to cardiac muscle metabolism during ischaemia?</summary>

**Answer:** The ischaemic cells switch from aerobic to anaerobic metabolism, using glycogen to produce ATP and forming lactic acid.

**Why:** Normoxia → ATP + CO₂; hypoxia → glycogen → ATP + lactate.
</details>

## 5. Physiological properties of cardiac muscle

### Electrical properties
| # | Property | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ⭐ **Syncytium** | a piece of heart acts as a **single unit** when stimulated; **functional syncytium** (not an anatomical one) |
| 2 | ⭐ **Rhythmicity (automaticity, chronotropism)** | ability to **depolarize spontaneously** without nervous stimulation |
| 3 | **Excitability** | ability to respond to an adequate stimulus by generating an **action potential** |
| 4 | **Conductivity (dromotropism)** | ability to **conduct** impulses initiated in the **SA node** |

### Mechanical properties
| # | Property | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ⭐ **Contractility (inotropism)** | converts **electrical energy into mechanical work** |
| 2 | ⭐ **All-or-none law** | **subthreshold stimulus → no response**; **threshold → maximal contraction**; **stronger stimuli → no further increase** |
| 3 | **Long absolute refractory period** | cannot be re-stimulated while contracted → **cannot be tetanized** |
| 4 | ⭐ **Plateau phase** | **long action potential and sustained contraction** — **unique to cardiac muscle** |

- In **skeletal muscle**, repeated stimuli before relaxation cause **summation then tetanus**; in the **heart**, the **plateau + long refractory period** prevent tetanus and allow **rhythmical contractions**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Electrical: syncytium, rhythmicity (automaticity), excitability, conductivity.
- Mechanical: contractility, all-or-none law, long absolute refractory period (no tetanus), plateau phase.
- Chronotropism = rate (rhythmicity); dromotropism = conduction; inotropism = contraction.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the electrical properties of cardiac muscle.</summary>

**Answer:** Syncytium (the heart acts as a single functional unit), rhythmicity/automaticity (spontaneous depolarization without nervous stimulation), excitability (responding to an adequate stimulus with an action potential) and conductivity (conducting impulses initiated in the SA node).

**Why:** Four properties with Greek-derived names (chrono-, dromo-, ino-).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. State the all-or-none law of the heart.</summary>

**Answer:** A subthreshold stimulus produces no response; a threshold stimulus produces a maximal contraction; stronger stimuli do not increase the contraction further.

**Why:** Three parts.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why can cardiac muscle not be tetanized?</summary>

**Answer:** Because its action potential has a long plateau and its absolute refractory period is long, so it cannot be stimulated again while it is contracted; this prevents summation and tetanus and allows rhythmical contraction and relaxation.

**Why:** A key difference from skeletal muscle.
</details>

## 6. The conductive system of the heart

- Made of **specialized myocardial fibers** that **contract weakly (few contractile fibrils)**.
- Main function is **not contraction** but **automatic rhythmic discharge (pacemaker)** and **conduction of impulses** through the heart.

### Conduction velocities (lecture table)
| Tissue | Resting membrane potential (mV) | Conduction velocity (m/s) |
|---|---|---|
| **SA node** | 40–60 | **0.05** |
| **Atrial muscle** | 70–80 | **0.5** |
| **Internodal tracts** | 80–90 | **1.0** |
| **AV node** | 60 | **0.05** (slowest, like the SA node) |
| **Purkinje fibers** | 70 | **4.0** (fastest) |
| **Ventricular muscle** | 90 | **0.5** |

(Bundle of His and bundle branches ≈ 2 m/s.)

### The pathway
1. ⭐ **SA node** — near the junction of the **SVC and right atrium**; fibers continuous with atrial fibers; **normal primary pacemaker** (self-excitatory, **unstable resting membrane potential** = automaticity).
2. **Interatrial tract (Bachmann's bundle)** — runs from the **SA node to the left atrium** → near-simultaneous depolarization of both atria.
3. **Internodal tracts** — **three bundles (anterior, middle, posterior)** connecting the **SA and AV nodes**.
4. ⭐ **AV node** — **posterior wall of the right atrium, just behind the tricuspid valve**; **the only normal path from atria to ventricles**.
5. **Bundle of His (AV bundle)** — continuation of the AV node in the **interventricular septum**; divides into **right and left bundle branches**; the **left** splits into **anterior and posterior fascicles**.
6. **Bundle branches** — on each side of the **interventricular septum**.
7. ⭐ **Purkinje fibers** — arise from the AV bundle and spread through the ventricles.

### The AV nodal delay
| Step | Time |
|---|---|
| SA node → AV node | **0.03 s** |
| Delay within the AV node | **0.09 s** |
| Delay in penetrating the AV bundle | **0.04 s** |
| **Total delay** | **≈ 0.16 s** |

- The delay lets the **atria contract before the ventricles**, **improving ventricular filling**.
- Cause: **fewer gap junctions** in AV nodal and bundle fibers → **higher resistance to ion flow** → **slow conduction**.
- The AV node = **physiological gatekeeper**.

### The fibrous skeleton & accessory pathways
- Atria and ventricles are separated by a **continuous fibrous skeleton (insulator)** — conduction only through the **AV bundle**, **one way (atria → ventricles)**.
- Rarely an **accessory pathway** crosses the barrier, letting impulses **re-enter the atria** → **re-entry arrhythmias**.

### Purkinje fibers
- **Very large fibers** (larger than ventricular muscle fibers); **conduction 1.5–4.0 m/s** (**≈ 6× faster than ventricular muscle**, **≈ 150× faster than AV nodal fibers**).
- Reason: **abundant gap junctions at intercalated discs** → **low resistance**.
- Ensures **near-simultaneous activation of the whole ventricular myocardium**; the stimulus starts at the **apex**, so contraction begins at the **apex** and travels toward the **base** → blood is pumped into the **aorta and pulmonary trunk**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Order: SA node → internodal tracts (+ Bachmann's bundle) → AV node → bundle of His → right/left bundle branches → Purkinje fibers → ventricular muscle.
- SA node = primary pacemaker (junction of SVC and right atrium); AV node = behind the tricuspid valve, the only normal atrioventricular path.
- AV delay ≈ 0.16 s (0.03 + 0.09 + 0.04) gives time for atrial contraction.
- Slowest = SA and AV nodes (0.05 m/s); fastest = Purkinje fibers (4 m/s).
- Contraction starts at the apex and moves toward the base.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the conductive system of the heart in order.</summary>

**Answer:** The SA node (primary pacemaker near the junction of the SVC and right atrium) spreads the impulse through the atria, using the interatrial tract (Bachmann's bundle) to the left atrium and the three internodal tracts to the AV node. The AV node (posterior wall of the right atrium behind the tricuspid valve) is the only normal path to the ventricles; the impulse then passes along the bundle of His in the interventricular septum, the right and left bundle branches (the left dividing into anterior and posterior fascicles) and finally the Purkinje fibers, which spread through the ventricular muscle.

**Why:** The sequence with one fact per structure.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the AV nodal delay and why is it important?</summary>

**Answer:** The total delay before excitation reaches the ventricular muscle is about 0.16 s (SA to AV node 0.03 s, delay within the AV node 0.09 s and delay in the AV bundle 0.04 s). It allows the atria to contract and fill the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins. It is caused by fewer gap junctions in the AV nodal and bundle fibers, which increase the resistance to ion flow.

**Why:** Numbers + purpose + mechanism.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are Purkinje fibers so fast and why does ventricular contraction start at the apex?</summary>

**Answer:** Purkinje fibers are very large and have abundant gap junctions at the intercalated discs, giving low resistance to ion flow and a conduction velocity of 1.5–4 m/s (about 6 times faster than ventricular muscle and 150 times faster than the AV node). This activates the whole ventricular myocardium almost simultaneously; because the stimulus begins at the apex, contraction starts at the apex and moves toward the base, pushing blood out into the aorta and pulmonary trunk.

**Why:** Mechanism of speed + direction of contraction.
</details>
