## 2. Brain development: critical period and plasticity

- Two systems: **prefrontal cortex** (planning, decision-making, impulse control, working memory, risk evaluation) and **limbic and reward-related systems** (emotion, motivation, reward processing, emotional responses).
- ⭐ **Critical period** = a specific timeframe when the brain is **particularly receptive** to certain stimuli and learning. Example: the **first year of life** for building a **perceptual map of phonemes** (distinct units of sound).
- **Perceptual narrowing:** a gradual reduction in discriminating **non-native** sounds while native discrimination is kept or improved (experience-dependent plasticity).

| Age | Speech-sound perception |
|---|---|
| **0-6 months** | Broad sensitivity: distinguish many phonemes from different languages |
| **6-10 months** | Exposure to the native language: increasing specialization |
| **10-12 months** | Less sensitive to some non-native contrasts; good native discrimination |

- Examples: English **/b/ vs /p/** (bat, pat); Japanese **/r/ vs /l/** (right, light).
- Language learning is **not limited to the first year**; later learners may find native-like pronunciation harder.
- ⭐ **Plasticity** = the brain's ability to **change and adapt** (structure, connections, functional organization) in response to experience, learning, environment or injury.
- Four mechanisms: **synaptic strengthening** (more efficient transmission), **synaptic weakening** (reduced efficacy), **formation of new connections** (synaptogenesis), **reorganization of brain areas**.
- Examples: learning a musical instrument, motor learning, **recovery after stroke**, learning and memory.
- **Cross-modal plasticity:** in congenital deafness the **auditory cortex** processes **visual and somatosensory** information. Plasticity continues throughout life, greater in early development.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Critical period = window of special receptivity; example phoneme perception in the first year.
- Perceptual narrowing: non-native contrasts fade by 10-12 months while native ones are kept.
- Plasticity = strengthening/weakening synapses, new connections, reorganization; cross-modal example in deafness.

</div>

## 3. Cognitive development and Piaget's key concepts

- ⭐ **Cognitive development** = how a person **perceives, thinks and understands** the world through interaction of **genetic and learned** factors. **Cognition** includes perception, memory, learning, reasoning, problem solving and language.
- Two theories: **organismic stage theory (Piaget)** and **collaborative learning theory (Vygotsky)**.
- Piaget: children pass through **four key stages**. Key concepts: **organization** (schemas), **adaptation** (assimilation, accommodation), **equilibration** (equilibrium and disequilibrium).

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Schema** | Mental framework of knowledge about a person, object, event or concept, formed by experience | Mother, dog, food, car, ball |
| **Assimilation** | Fitting **new information into an existing schema** | "Cat" added to the "dog" schema |
| **Accommodation** | **Changing/revising the schema** for new information | Separate "dog" and "cat" schemas by voice, ear shape, size |

- A **rich environment** helps form many schemas.
- Note: the slide ends with "equilibrium / disequilibrium ... all equal to concept formation", which is unclear wording; the key terms are the names above.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Schema = organized knowledge unit; assimilation = fit into existing; accommodation = change the schema.
- Piaget has four stages; Vygotsky offers the social alternative.
- Cognition = perception, memory, learning, reasoning, problem solving, language.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define a schema and give examples.</summary>

**Answer:** A mental framework (organized pattern of knowledge) about a person, object, event or concept, formed by experience, e.g. schemas for mother, dog, food, car and ball.

**Why:** Slide "Organization / Schemas".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate assimilation from accommodation with an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Assimilation fits new information into an existing schema (a cat is put in the "dog" schema); accommodation revises the schema (the child separates dog from cat by voice and ear shape).

**Why:** Slide "Adaptation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is cognition?</summary>

**Answer:** The processes of acquiring and understanding sensory information: perception, memory, learning, reasoning, problem solving and language.

**Why:** Slide "Cognition refers to the processes...".
</details>

## 4. Stage 1: Sensorimotor stage (birth to 2 years)

- Infants learn by relating **sensory experiences** (hearing, seeing) to **motor actions** (mouthing, grasping). Main concept: ⭐ **object permanence**.

| Development | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Symbolic thought** | Using symbols (words, objects) to represent ideas; groundwork for language and abstract thinking |
| **Representational play** | Using objects for other things (cup as a hat, apple as a ball) |
| **Object permanence** | Objects still exist when out of sight; usually **8-12 months** |
| **Recognition of self** | Seeing oneself as a separate individual (mirror), around **18-24 months** |
| **Deferred imitation** | Imitating an action after a delay; shows memory |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Birth-2 years; sensory experiences linked to motor actions.
- Five developments: symbolic thought, representational play, object permanence, recognition of self, deferred imitation.
- Foundation for language, memory and later abstract thinking.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** Symbolic thought, representational play, object permanence, recognition of self and deferred imitation.

**Why:** Slide "Sensorimotor stage".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define object permanence and say when it develops.</summary>

**Answer:** Understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be seen; it usually develops around 8-12 months.

**Why:** Slide "Object permanence".
</details>

**Answer:** The ability to imitate an action after a delay; it shows the child's memory and understanding of observed behavior.

## 5. Stage 2: Preoperational stage

- Children use **symbols (words)** to represent concepts and **start speaking**, the number of schemas increases, but they **lack mental operations** for logical thinking. Main concept: ⭐ **egocentrism**.
- **Egocentrism:** seeing the world from **one's own viewpoint**; difficulty understanding that others have different feelings, thoughts and perspectives; can hinder social interaction. Examples: assuming everyone likes the same toy or ice cream; assuming others know what the child knows.
- Other features: language and symbolic thinking, **animism** (attributing life to inanimate objects), **intuitive rather than logical** thinking.
- Note: the slide titles give 2-6 years, while the egocentrism and summary slides give 2-7 years.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Symbols and language grow, but logic is missing.
- Egocentrism is the hallmark; animism and intuitive thinking also appear.
- Egocentrism hinders understanding of others' emotions.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define egocentrism and give an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Seeing the world primarily from one's own viewpoint and struggling to understand that others have different feelings or thoughts, e.g. assuming everyone shares the child's excitement about a toy.

**Why:** Slide "Egocentrism".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What can the preoperational child do and not do?</summary>

**Answer:** He uses words as symbols and starts speaking, but lacks the mental operations needed for logical thinking.

**Why:** Slide "Stage 2: Preoperational stage".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define animism.</summary>

**Answer:** Attributing life to inanimate objects, a feature of the preoperational stage.

## 6. Stage 3: Concrete operational stage (7-11 years)

- Logical thinking about **concrete, observable** situations. Main concept: ⭐ **conservation**.

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Conservation** | Quantity (mass, volume, number) stays the same despite change in appearance |
| **Classification** | Categories and hierarchies (cat, animal, living thing) |
| **Seriation** | Ordering along a quantitative dimension (pencils by length) |
| **Reversibility** | An action can be mentally reversed (5 + 3 = 8; 8 - 3 = 5) |
| **Transitivity** | A > B and B > C so A > C |
| **Reduced egocentrism** | Better perspective-taking than before |

- **Types of conservation:** liquid, number, matter (mass) acquired **early**; **length and weight later**. Test: the **liquid/beakers problem**.
- Some grasp of conservation marks the **end of the preoperational and start of the concrete operational** stage.
- Reasoning is tied to **immediate reality**, not the hypothetical world.
- Note: the slides give 7-11 years in the title and 6-11 years on another slide.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Concrete operational = logic about the concrete; conservation is the key concept.
- Six features: conservation, classification, seriation, reversibility, transitivity, reduced egocentrism.
- Length and weight conservation appear later than liquid, number and matter.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define conservation and explain how it is tested.</summary>

**Answer:** Knowing that quantitative properties (mass, volume, number) remain the same despite a change in appearance; tested by the liquid/beakers problem, where water is poured into a taller glass. A child who knows the amount is unchanged has begun to grasp conservation.

**Why:** Slide "Conservation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define seriation, transitivity and reversibility.</summary>

**Answer:** Seriation: ordering objects along a quantitative dimension. Transitivity: if A > B and B > C then A > C. Reversibility: an action can be mentally reversed (5 + 3 = 8 and 8 - 3 = 5).

**Why:** Slides "Concrete operational stage".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What limits concrete operational reasoning?</summary>

**Answer:** It is tied to immediate, tangible reality and not to the hypothetical world of possibility.

**Why:** Slide "Concrete operational stage (6-11 years)".
</details>

## 7. Stage 4: Formal operational stage (about 12 years onward)

- Development of **abstract, hypothetical and systematic** reasoning. Main concept: ⭐ **abstract thinking**.
- Features: abstract thinking, hypothetical reasoning, **scientific problem-solving**, considering different possibilities, thinking about future consequences. Example: "What would happen if all antibiotics became ineffective?"
- **Flask task:** the concrete child mixes liquids haphazardly; the formal child **plans systematically**, listing all combinations and testing each.
- Can judge the **logic of verbal statements** without concrete situations ("mice bigger than horses, horses bigger than cats, so mice bigger than cats" is logically true).
- Personal identity and moral reasoning develop.

| Stage | Age | Main concept |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensorimotor** | 0-2 | Object permanence |
| **Preoperational** | 2-7 | Egocentrism |
| **Concrete operational** | 7-11 | Conservation |
| **Formal operational** | 12+ | Abstract thinking |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Formal operational: abstract, hypothetical, systematic reasoning from about 12 years.
- Flask task separates concrete (haphazard) from formal (systematic) thinking.
- Summary table: object permanence, egocentrism, conservation, abstract thinking.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the characteristics of the formal operational stage.</summary>

**Answer:** Abstract thinking, hypothetical reasoning, scientific problem-solving, considering different possibilities and thinking about future consequences.

**Why:** Slide "Stage 4: Formal operational stage".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do concrete and formal operational children differ in the flask task?</summary>

**Answer:** The concrete child mixes the liquids haphazardly; the formal child makes a systematic plan by identifying all possible combinations and testing each.

**Why:** Slide "Flask scientific thinking task".

## 8. Vygotsky's sociocultural (collaborative learning) theory

- Cognitive development is shaped by **social interaction, cultural context and language** (Lev Vygotsky, 1896-1934).

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cultural and social importance** | Development does not occur in a vacuum; children learn from parents, teachers and peers |
| **Language as a tool for thought** | **Private speech** is a step toward **self-regulation and planning** (Piaget saw it as egocentric) |
| **Inner speech** | After **age 7**; internalized thought; enhances memory, problem-solving, decision-making |
| **Zone of proximal development (ZPD)** | Tasks mastered **with help** but not yet alone |
| **Scaffolding** | Temporary support (adults/peers), gradually reduced |

- Speech sequence: **social speech → private speech → inner speech**.
- Scaffolding steps: "I do it for you" (full) → "We do it together" (partial) → "You do it yourself" (independent).
- **Piaget vs Vygotsky:** Piaget = progressive defined stages by age, logic from interaction with the physical world; Vygotsky = social and cultural factors, especially language.
- Note: the slide is titled "collaborative learning theory", usually known as the sociocultural theory.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five concepts: social-cultural influence, language, inner speech, ZPD, scaffolding.
- Private speech = self-regulation (Vygotsky) vs egocentric (Piaget).
- ZPD = can do with help; scaffolding = support gradually removed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the zone of proximal development and scaffolding.</summary>

**Answer:** The ZPD is the set of tasks a child can master with assistance from adults or peers but not yet alone; scaffolding is temporary social support that is gradually reduced as the child becomes competent.

**Why:** Slide "Collaborative learning theory (Vygotsky)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the development of speech according to Vygotsky.</summary>

**Answer:** Social speech (talking to others) then private speech (talking to oneself to guide thinking) then inner speech (internalized, after age 7), which supports memory, problem-solving and decision-making.

**Why:** Slides "Language as a tool for thought" and "Inner speech".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare Piaget and Vygotsky.</summary>

**Answer:** Piaget stressed a progressive process of defined stages and ages with logic arising from interaction with the physical world; Vygotsky stressed social and cultural factors, especially language and interaction with people.

**Why:** Slide "Comparison with Piaget".
</details>

## 9. Kohlberg's theory of moral development

- Describes how the child **accepts moral values** at different ages: **3 levels, 6 stages** (a 7th is not universally accepted). **No definite age limit**; a person may sometimes behave by the previous stage. **Universal sequence** in all cultures (rate and endpoint may differ). Based on **moral dilemmas** (conflict between two or more moral principles).

| Level | Stage | Basis | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preconventional** (morality externally defined, consequences) | **1. Obedience and punishment** (about 4-7 y) | Obey authority to avoid punishment | Does not steal for fear of punishment |
| | **2. Instrumental relativist** (about 7-10 y) | Self-interest, risks and benefits ("you help me, I help you") | Helps a classmate who may help back |
| **Conventional** (social systems have an interest in behavior) | **3. Good interpersonal relationships** (about 10-13 y) | Social approval, being "good" | Does not cheat to keep a good reputation |
| | **4. Maintaining social order** (13+ y) | Laws needed for order; duty | Obeys traffic rules for stability |
| **Postconventional** (rules rest on principles applicable everywhere) | **5. Social contract** (adolescence/adulthood) | Laws are contracts, changeable; personal rights may precede laws | Speaks out against an unjust law |
| | **6. Universal ethical principles** (late adolescence/adulthood) | Justice, equality, human dignity | Disobeys a law against human rights |
| | **7. Cosmic orientation** (not universally accepted) | Values transcend societal norms; concern for all humanity and environment | Protection of the environment and living beings |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Preconventional (1-2) = consequences and self-interest; conventional (3-4) = approval and order; postconventional (5-6) = social contract and universal principles.
- No fixed age limit; the sequence is universal.
- Stage 7 (cosmic) is not universally accepted.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name Kohlberg's three levels and the stages in each.</summary>

**Answer:** Preconventional (stage 1 obedience and punishment; stage 2 instrumental relativist), conventional (stage 3 good interpersonal relationships; stage 4 maintaining social order), postconventional (stage 5 social contract; stage 6 universal ethical principles; stage 7 cosmic orientation in some interpretations).

**Why:** Slides "Three levels of moral development".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Distinguish Stage 3 from Stage 4.</summary>

**Answer:** Stage 3 is driven by a desire for social approval and being seen as good; stage 4 is driven by respect for laws, rules and authority to maintain social order, even without personal benefit.

**Why:** Slide "Conventional level".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does Kohlberg say about age and universality?</summary>

**Answer:** There is no definite age limit for moving between stages, and a person may sometimes act by the previous stage; the sequence is universal across cultures, although the rate and endpoint may differ.

**Why:** Slide "Moral development".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Assimilation fits new information into the existing schema; accommodation changes the schema.**
- **Object permanence = sensorimotor; egocentrism = preoperational; conservation = concrete operational; abstract thinking = formal operational.**
- **Piaget saw private speech as egocentric; Vygotsky saw it as self-regulation.**
- **ZPD is what the child can do with help; scaffolding is the help (gradually reduced).**
- **Kohlberg stage 2 is self-interest; stage 3 is social approval; stage 4 is law and order; stage 5 is social contract.**
- **Perceptual narrowing reduces non-native discrimination, not native.**
