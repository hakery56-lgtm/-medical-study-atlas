## 1. Capillary beds

- Capillaries **permit and regulate metabolic exchange** between blood and tissues; they always work in **networks called capillary beds**.

### How a capillary bed is organized
1. Supplied by one or more **terminal arteriole branches called metarterioles**, continuous with **thoroughfare channels** that connect to the **postcapillary venules**.
2. **True capillaries branch from the metarterioles**; the origin of each is encircled by smooth muscle cells acting as ⭐ **precapillary sphincters** that **control blood flow** into the capillaries, which converge into the **thoroughfare channels** (these **lack muscle**).
3. The sphincters **contract and relax cyclically — 5 to 10 cycles per minute** — so blood passes through the capillaries in a **pulsatile** way.
4. **Sphincters relaxed** → the capillary bed is **well perfused**; **sphincters closed** → blood flows **directly from the metarterioles and thoroughfare channels into the postcapillary venules**.
5. **High metabolic rate** tissues (**kidney, liver, cardiac and skeletal muscle**) have an **abundant capillary network**.
6. **Low metabolic rate** tissues (**smooth muscle, dense connective tissue**) have **few capillaries**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Capillary bed = metarteriole → true capillaries (with precapillary sphincters) → thoroughfare channel (no muscle) → postcapillary venule.
- Sphincters cycle 5–10 times per minute; relaxed = bed perfused; closed = blood bypasses the capillaries.
- High metabolic rate (kidney, liver, heart, skeletal muscle) = abundant capillaries; low (smooth muscle, dense connective tissue) = few.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of a capillary bed.</summary>

**Answer:** A capillary bed is supplied by one or more metarterioles (terminal arteriole branches) continuous with thoroughfare channels that connect to the postcapillary venules. True capillaries branch from the metarterioles, each origin encircled by smooth muscle that acts as a precapillary sphincter controlling blood flow; the capillaries converge into the thoroughfare channel, which lacks muscle.

**Why:** Four parts in order.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the function of the precapillary sphincters?</summary>

**Answer:** They control the blood flow into the capillaries by contracting and relaxing cyclically (5–10 cycles per minute), making flow pulsatile; when relaxed the bed is well perfused, and when closed blood passes directly from metarterioles and thoroughfare channels into postcapillary venules.

**Why:** Flow control + cycles.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which tissues have abundant capillary networks and which have few?</summary>

**Answer:** Tissues with high metabolic rates — kidney, liver, cardiac and skeletal muscle — have abundant capillaries, whereas tissues with low metabolic rates such as smooth muscle and dense connective tissue have few.

**Why:** Capillary density follows metabolic rate.
</details>

## 2. Arteriovenous shunts & portal systems

### Arteriovenous (AV) shunts (anastomoses)
- Most capillary beds are supplied by arterioles and drain into venules.
- **AV shunts** = **alternative pathways** in certain organs such as the **skin**; they **directly connect the arterial and venous systems and temporarily bypass the capillaries**.
- In skin, blood flow can be varied according to **external conditions** (e.g. temperature).

### Portal systems
| Type | Pathway | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Venous portal** | **one capillary bed → a vein → a second capillary bed** | **anterior pituitary gland and liver** |
| ⭐ **Arterial portal** | **afferent arteriole → capillaries (glomerulus) → efferent arteriole** | **kidney** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- AV shunts bypass capillaries (e.g. skin) and vary skin blood flow.
- Venous portal systems (anterior pituitary, liver): capillary bed → vein → capillary bed. Arterial portal system (kidney): afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are arteriovenous shunts and where are they found?</summary>

**Answer:** Alternative pathways that directly connect the arterial and venous systems and temporarily bypass the capillaries; they are found in certain organs such as the skin, where blood flow can be varied according to external conditions.

**Why:** Definition + site + purpose.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define portal systems and give examples.</summary>

**Answer:** In a venous portal system one capillary bed drains into a vein that then branches into a second capillary bed (anterior pituitary and liver); in an arterial portal system an afferent arteriole supplies a capillary tuft (glomerulus) that drains into an efferent arteriole (kidney).

**Why:** Venous vs arterial portal.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the kidney's circulation called an arterial portal system?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the glomerular capillaries are interposed between two arterioles (afferent and efferent), the efferent arteriole then supplying the peritubular capillaries and vasa recta.

**Why:** Two arterioles with capillaries between.
</details>

## 3. Veins

- **Thin wall, wide lumen, thin media, poorly developed smooth muscle and minimal elastic fibers**; carry blood back to the heart from the microvasculature **under very low pressure**.

### 1) Venules
- **a. Postcapillary venules:** a **single layer of endothelial cells** on a basal lamina, **surrounded by pericytes**; the **junctions between the endothelial cells are the loosest of the microvasculature** → **facilitates transendothelial migration of leukocytes** during inflammation and the **loss of fluid → tissue edema** in the inflammatory response.
- **b. Muscular venules:** media of **1 to 2 smooth muscle cells**, thin adventitia.

### 2) Small and medium-sized veins
- Usually **close and parallel to the corresponding muscular arteries**; include **most named veins with diameters ≤ 10 mm**; **vasa vasorum numerous**.
- **Intima** thin with subendothelial tissue; **media** with small bundles of smooth muscle cells in a network of reticular and delicate elastic fibers; **adventitia thicker than the media**, with collagen and a little smooth muscle and elastic fibers.

### 3) Large veins (vena cava, subclavian, portal veins)
- **Intima: no internal elastic lamina.**
- **Media relatively thin (30%)**: alternating concentric smooth muscle and collagen fibers; **no external elastic lamina**.
- ⭐ **Adventitia thicker than the media (70% of the wall)**, frequently with **longitudinal bundles of smooth muscle** that **strengthen the wall and prevent distension**.
- Both media and adventitia contain elastic fibers.

### Vein valves
- **Thin, paired folds of the tunica intima** projecting across the lumen, **rich in elastic fibers** and **covered on both sides by endothelium**; they ensure **one-way flow (no backflow)**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Veins: thin wall, wide lumen, thin media, low pressure.
- Postcapillary venules have the loosest endothelial junctions → leukocyte migration and edema in inflammation.
- Large veins: media 30%, adventitia 70% (longitudinal smooth muscle); no internal or external elastic lamina.
- Valves are paired folds of the intima, covered by endothelium on both sides.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the postcapillary venule and its role in inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** It is formed of a single layer of endothelial cells on a basal lamina surrounded by pericytes; its endothelial junctions are the loosest of the microvasculature, which facilitates transendothelial migration of leukocytes and the characteristic loss of fluid during inflammation that produces tissue edema.

**Why:** Structure + inflammation.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the structure of a large vein.</summary>

**Answer:** The intima has no internal elastic lamina; the media is relatively thin (30% of the wall) with alternating concentric smooth muscle and collagen fibers and no external elastic lamina; the adventitia is thicker (70% of the wall) and frequently contains longitudinal bundles of smooth muscle that strengthen the wall and prevent distension; elastic fibers are present in the media and adventitia. Examples: vena cava, subclavian and portal veins.

**Why:** 30 / 70 rule — opposite to elastic arteries.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are vein valves and what is their structure?</summary>

**Answer:** Thin, paired folds of the tunica intima that project across the lumen, are rich in elastic fibers and are covered on both sides by endothelium; they prevent backflow so blood moves toward the heart.

**Why:** One-way flow.
</details>

## 4. Comparison of the blood vessel types

| Vessel | Outer diameter | Intima | Media | Adventitia | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Elastic arteries** | > 10 mm | endothelium + connective tissue with smooth muscle | **many elastic lamellae alternating with smooth muscle** | connective tissue thinner than media; **vasa vasorum** | conduct blood from the heart; **elastic recoil** moves blood forward under steady pressure |
| **Muscular arteries** | 10–1 mm | **prominent internal elastic lamina** | **many smooth muscle layers**, much less elastic | thinner than media; vasa vasorum may be present | **distribute blood to all organs**; maintain pressure/flow by vasodilation and constriction |
| **Small arteries** | 1–0.1 mm | less smooth muscle | **3–10 layers** of smooth muscle | thinner than media; **no vasa vasorum** | distribute to arterioles, adjusting flow |
| **Arterioles** | 100–10 µm | endothelium only | **1–3 layers** of smooth muscle | very thin | **resist and control flow**; **major determinant of systemic blood pressure** |
| **Capillaries** | 10–4 µm | endothelium only | a few pericytes | none | **exchange metabolites by diffusion** |
| **Venules** | 10–100 µm | endothelium; **no valves** | pericytes + scattered smooth muscle | none | drain capillary beds; **site of leukocyte exit** |
| **Small veins** | 0.1–1 mm | endothelium; scattered smooth muscle | **thin, 2–3 loose layers** of smooth muscle | thicker than media | collect blood from venules |
| **Medium veins** | 1–10 mm | endothelium; **valves** | **3–5** distinct layers of smooth muscle | thicker; longitudinal smooth muscle may be present | carry blood to larger veins, **no backflow** |
| **Large veins** | > 10 mm | endothelium; smooth muscle; **prominent valves** | **5 layers**, much collagen | **thickest layer**, bundled longitudinal smooth muscle | **return blood to the heart** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Arteries: media thickest (elastic arteries: elastic lamellae; muscular: many smooth muscle layers; arterioles: 1–3 layers = pressure control).
- Capillaries: endothelium only; venules: site of leukocyte exit, no valves.
- Veins: adventitia thicker than media; valves in medium and large veins.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare muscular arteries and arterioles.</summary>

**Answer:** Muscular arteries (10–1 mm) have a prominent internal elastic lamina and many smooth muscle layers with little elastic material, distributing blood to organs. Arterioles (100–10 µm) have only endothelium in the intima, 1–3 smooth muscle layers and a very thin adventitia; they resist and control flow to the capillaries and are the major determinant of systemic blood pressure.

**Why:** Diameter, media, role.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare arteries and veins of the same size.</summary>

**Answer:** Veins have a thinner media with fewer smooth muscle layers, a thicker adventitia than media, a wider lumen and valves (medium and large veins), carrying blood back to the heart at low pressure; arteries have a thicker media (elastic or smooth muscle) and distribute blood at higher pressure without valves.

**Why:** Synthesis of the table (standard comparison).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the role of venules?</summary>

**Answer:** They drain the capillary beds and are the site where leukocytes leave the vasculature; they have endothelium without valves, pericytes and scattered smooth muscle cells, and no adventitia (muscular venules have a thin adventitia).

**Why:** Drainage + leukocyte exit.
</details>

## 5. The heart wall — three layers

The walls of all four chambers have three tunics: the **internal endocardium**, the middle **myocardium** and the external **epicardium**.

### 1. Endocardium
- **Thin inner endothelium** + **supporting fibroelastic connective tissue** with scattered smooth muscle fibers.
- A **deep layer of connective tissue = subendocardial layer** containing **branches of the impulse-conducting system** (modified cardiac muscle fibers).

### 2. Myocardium
- The **thickest layer**; **cardiac muscle fibers arranged spirally** around each chamber.
- **Much thicker in the ventricles, particularly the left**, than in the atria (strong force needed for systemic and pulmonary circulation).
- Fibers: **long, cylindrical, branching and anastomosing**; **fainter striations** than skeletal muscle; attached by **intercalated disks**; **single, oval, central nuclei**.
- ⭐ **No ability to regenerate** — injured cardiac muscle is **replaced by fibrous tissue**.

### 3. Epicardium
- **Simple squamous mesothelium** on a **loose connective tissue** layer with the **coronary vessels, nerves and adipose tissue**.
- = the **visceral layer of the pericardium**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Heart wall: endocardium (endothelium + subendocardial layer with Purkinje fibers), myocardium (thickest, thicker in the left ventricle), epicardium (mesothelium = visceral pericardium).
- Cardiac muscle: branching fibers, intercalated disks, one central nucleus; cannot regenerate (replaced by fibrous tissue).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the layers of the heart wall.</summary>

**Answer:** The endocardium (endothelium, fibroelastic connective tissue and a deep subendocardial layer containing conducting system branches), the myocardium (thickest layer of spirally arranged cardiac muscle) and the epicardium (simple squamous mesothelium on loose connective tissue with coronary vessels, nerves and fat; the visceral pericardium).

**Why:** Three tunics, one fact each.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the histology of the myocardium.</summary>

**Answer:** It is the thickest layer, made mainly of cardiac muscle fibers arranged spirally around each chamber, thicker in the ventricles (especially the left) than the atria. The fibers are long, cylindrical, branching and anastomosing, with fainter striations than skeletal muscle, joined by intercalated disks, each with a single oval central nucleus; they cannot regenerate, so injured fibers are replaced by fibrous tissue.

**Why:** Structure + clinical (post-infarct scar).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the left ventricular myocardium the thickest?</summary>

**Answer:** Because a strong force is required to pump blood through the high-resistance systemic circulation.

**Why:** Function determines thickness.
</details>

## 6. Cardiac skeleton & conducting system

### Cardiac skeleton
- **Prominent dense irregular connective tissue** that **separates the atrial from the ventricular musculature**, **forms part of the interventricular and interatrial septa** and **extends into the valve cusps and chordae tendineae**.

### Conducting system
- **Modified cardiac muscle cells** specialized to **generate and conduct depolarization waves** that stimulate rhythmic contraction; present in the **subendocardial layer and adjacent myocardium**.
- The **AV bundle passes through an opening in the cardiac skeleton** into the interventricular septum and **divides into left and right bundles** in the myocardium and subendocardial layer.
- ⭐ **Purkinje fibers:** **pale-staining**, **larger than the adjacent contractile fibers**, **one or two central nuclei**, **sparse myofibrils restricted to the periphery**, **much glycogen**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cardiac skeleton: dense irregular connective tissue separating atria from ventricles; supports valves and chordae.
- AV bundle passes through the skeleton and splits into right and left bundles.
- Purkinje fibers: pale, larger, 1–2 central nuclei, peripheral sparse myofibrils, abundant glycogen.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the cardiac skeleton and what are its functions?</summary>

**Answer:** Prominent dense irregular connective tissue that separates the atrial muscle from the ventricular muscle, forms part of the interventricular and interatrial septa and extends into the valve cusps and the chordae tendineae, giving attachment to the muscle and the valves and insulating the atria from the ventricles.

**Why:** Position + extension.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the histology of Purkinje fibers.</summary>

**Answer:** They are modified cardiac muscle fibers that are pale-staining and larger than the adjacent contractile fibers, with one or two central nuclei, sparse myofibrils restricted to the periphery of the cytoplasm and abundant glycogen; they conduct the impulse from the AV bundle branches to the ventricular myocardium.

**Why:** Five histological features.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Where is the conducting system located in the heart wall?</summary>

**Answer:** In the subendocardial layer of the endocardium and in the adjacent myocardium; the AV bundle passes through the cardiac skeleton into the interventricular septum and bifurcates into left and right bundles.

**Why:** Layer + path.
</details>

## 7. The lymphatic vascular system

- **Lymphatic capillaries** begin locally as tubes of **very thin endothelial cells** that **lack tight junctions** and rest on a **discontinuous basal lamina**; **fine anchoring filaments of collagen** extend from the basal lamina to the connective tissue, **preventing collapse**.
- Present in most tissues with blood microvasculature — **exceptions: bone marrow and most of the CNS**.
- **Larger lymphatic vessels** are **similar to veins** but with **thinner walls**, **no clear separation between tunics** and **more numerous internal valves**.
- **Clinical importance:** lymphatics **facilitate the spread of pathogens, parasites and malignant cells**; **surgical removal of lymph nodes** is a standard procedure to detect **cancer metastasis**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Lymphatic capillaries: thin endothelium without tight junctions, discontinuous basal lamina, anchoring collagen filaments; absent in bone marrow and most of the CNS.
- Larger lymphatics: like veins but thinner walls, more valves.
- Clinical: spread of infection and cancer; lymph-node removal to detect metastasis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe lymphatic capillaries.</summary>

**Answer:** They originate as tubes of very thin endothelial cells that lack tight junctions and rest on a discontinuous basal lamina; fine anchoring collagen filaments from the basal lamina to the surrounding connective tissue prevent them from collapsing. They are present in most tissues with blood microvasculature except the bone marrow and most of the CNS.

**Why:** Structure + exceptions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do larger lymphatic vessels differ from veins?</summary>

**Answer:** They have a similar structure but thinner walls, no clear separation between the tunics and more numerous internal valves.

**Why:** Three differences.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are lymphatic vessels clinically important?</summary>

**Answer:** They facilitate the spread of pathogens, parasites and malignant cells; surgical removal of lymph nodes is a standard way to determine whether cancer has metastasized.

**Why:** Metastasis route.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Thoroughfare channels lack muscle**; **metarterioles** have **precapillary sphincters** at the capillary origins.
- Sphincter cycle = **5–10 per minute**.
- **Venous portal:** anterior pituitary + liver; **arterial portal:** kidney.
- **Large veins:** media **30%**, adventitia **70%**; **elastic arteries:** the opposite (media 70%).
- **Postcapillary venule** = loosest junctions = leukocyte exit + edema.
- **Venules have no valves; medium and large veins have valves.**
- **Cardiac muscle cannot regenerate** (fibrous scar).
- **Epicardium = visceral pericardium.**
- **Purkinje fibers** are **larger and paler** than contractile fibers.
- **Lymphatic capillaries lack tight junctions**; absent in **bone marrow and most CNS**.

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. **Capillary bed:** metarteriole → precapillary sphincters (5–10 cycles/min) → true capillaries → thoroughfare channel → postcapillary venule.
2. **AV shunts** (skin) bypass capillaries; **portal systems**: venous (pituitary, liver) vs arterial (kidney).
3. **Veins:** thin wall, wide lumen; **postcapillary venules** (leukocyte exit, edema); **large veins** media 30% / adventitia 70%; **valves** = intimal folds.
4. **Vessel table:** elastic arteries (> 10 mm) → muscular (10–1 mm) → small arteries → arterioles (pressure control) → capillaries → venules → veins.
5. **Heart:** endocardium (subendocardial layer), myocardium (thickest, no regeneration), epicardium (visceral pericardium).
6. **Cardiac skeleton** separates atria from ventricles; **Purkinje fibers** pale, large, 1–2 central nuclei, glycogen-rich.
7. **Lymphatic capillaries:** no tight junctions, anchoring filaments; metastasis route.
