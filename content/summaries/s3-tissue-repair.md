## 1. Systemic effects of inflammation (the acute-phase response)

- ⭐ Inflammation is associated with **cytokine-induced systemic reactions** called the **acute-phase response**. Key cytokines: **TNF, IL-1, IL-6 and type I interferons**, released in response to **LPS, viral RNA** and other stimuli.

### 1) Fever
- **Rise of body temperature by 1–4 °C.** Substances that induce it are **pyrogens**; the rise is caused by **prostaglandins (PGE2) made in the hypothalamus**.
- **Exogenous pyrogens** (e.g. **LPS**) make leukocytes release **IL-1 and TNF (endogenous pyrogens)** → more enzymes that convert **arachidonic acid → prostaglandins** → PGE2 **resets the hypothalamic set point higher**.
- **NSAIDs, including aspirin, reduce fever by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis.**

### 2) Acute-phase proteins
- **Plasma proteins mostly made in the liver**, rising up to several hundred-fold: **C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, serum amyloid A (SAA).**
- **CRP and SAA** bind **microbial cell walls**, act as **opsonins** and **fix complement**.
- **Fibrinogen** makes red cells form **stacks (rouleaux)** that sediment faster = the basis of the **ESR**.
- **Hepcidin** (iron-regulating peptide) rises; chronic elevation **reduces iron availability** → **anemia of chronic inflammation.**

### 3) Leukocytosis
| Finding | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Leukocytosis 15,000–20,000/mL** | Common in inflammation, especially **bacterial** infection |
| **40,000–100,000/mL = leukemoid reaction** | Resembles leukemia counts |
| **Neutrophilia** | Most **bacterial** infections |
| **Lymphocytosis** | **Viral:** infectious mononucleosis, mumps, German measles |
| **Eosinophilia** | **Allergies, parasitic** infestations |
| **Leukopenia** | **Typhoid fever**, some viruses, rickettsiae, certain protozoa |

### 4–5) Other features
- **Increased pulse and blood pressure, decreased sweating, rigors, anorexia.**
- **DIC and septic shock:** in **sepsis** large amounts of **organisms and LPS** → huge **TNF and IL-1**; **high TNF causes DIC**; **LPS and TNF induce tissue factor on endothelial cells** (starts coagulation) and **inhibit natural anticoagulants.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Acute-phase response = cytokine (TNF, IL-1, IL-6, IFN-I)-induced systemic changes.
- Fever: LPS → IL-1/TNF → PGE2 in hypothalamus; NSAIDs block it.
- Acute-phase proteins: CRP, fibrinogen (ESR), SAA, hepcidin (anemia of chronic disease); leukocytosis pattern by cause; sepsis → TNF/IL-1 → DIC.

</div>

## 2. Overview of repair and proliferative capacity of tissues

- **Two types of repair:** **1) regeneration** (proliferation of residual uninjured cells and maturation of tissue stem cells); **2) deposition of connective tissue to form a scar.**
- **Regeneration** = replacing damaged components and **returning to a normal state** (e.g. **skin and intestinal epithelia, liver**).
- **Scar formation** when tissues **cannot restitute completely** or the **supporting structures are severely damaged.**
- **Fibrosis** = extensive **collagen** deposition (lungs, liver, kidney after chronic inflammation; myocardium after infarction). **Organization** = fibrosis in a space occupied by **inflammatory exudate** (organizing pneumonia).
- Proliferating cells in repair: **remnants of the injured tissue, vascular endothelial cells, fibroblasts.** The ability to repair depends on the tissue's **intrinsic proliferative capacity.**

| Tissue type | Cells | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Labile** | **Continuously lost and replaced** from stem cells/progenitors; **regenerate readily if stem-cell pool preserved** | **Bone marrow hematopoietic cells; surface epithelia** (skin, oral cavity, vagina, cervix); **duct cuboidal epithelia; GI, uterine, tubal columnar epithelium; urinary transitional epithelium** |
| **Stable** | **Quiescent (G0)** but **divide after injury** | **Parenchyma of liver, kidney, pancreas; endothelial cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle** |
| **Permanent** | **Terminally differentiated, non-proliferative** → **injury = scar** | **Most neurons and cardiac muscle** (skeletal muscle: **satellite cells** give some regeneration) |

### Mechanisms of regeneration
- Depends on the **type of tissue** and **severity of injury.** **Intestinal/skin epithelia:** residual cells and stem-cell-derived cells replace injured ones **if the basement membrane is intact**; residual epithelial cells produce growth factors and **migrate** to fill the defect.
- **Parenchymal organs** (pancreas, adrenal, thyroid, lung) have some regenerative capacity; **after removal of a kidney the other shows compensatory hypertrophy and hyperplasia of proximal duct cells.**
- **Repair by scarring** if injury is **severe or chronic**, damages **parenchymal cells/epithelia and the connective tissue framework**, or injures **non-dividing cells**; a scar **"patches" rather than restores**; "scar" is used for **skin wound healing** and **collagen replacement of parenchymal cells (heart after MI).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Repair = regeneration or scar (connective tissue deposition); scar when restitution impossible or framework destroyed.
- Labile (marrow, surface epithelia) > stable (liver, kidney, pancreas; G0) > permanent (neurons, cardiac muscle: scar).
- Regeneration needs intact basement membrane; scar patches rather than restores.

</div>

## 3. Steps of scar formation and healing by first vs second intention

### Steps
| Step | Timing | Events |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Hemostasis** | **Within minutes** | **Platelet hemostatic plug** stops bleeding and gives a **scaffold** for inflammatory cells |
| **II. Inflammation** | **6–48 hours** | Acute and chronic inflammation **eliminate agents and clear debris**; **M1 macrophages** clear microbes/necrotic tissue and promote inflammation; **M2 macrophages** make **growth factors** for the next stage |
| **III. Cell proliferation** | **Up to 10 days** | **Epithelial cells** migrate and cover the wound; **endothelial cells → angiogenesis**; **fibroblasts** lay down **collagen** (scar) |
| **IV. Remodeling** | **Starts 2–3 weeks**, continues **months–years** | Connective tissue reorganized into a **stable fibrous scar** |

- **Granulation tissue** (unique to healing wounds) = **proliferating fibroblasts + loose connective tissue + new blood vessels + scattered chronic inflammatory cells**; grossly **pink, soft, granular** (beneath a scab).

### First vs second intention
| | **First intention (primary union)** | **Second intention (secondary union)** |
|---|---|---|
| Wound | **Clean, uninfected, sutured incision**; only focal basement-membrane disruption and death of few cells | **Larger** cell/tissue loss: **large wounds, abscesses, ulcers, infarcts** |
| Process | **Epithelial regeneration predominates** over fibrosis | More complex: **regeneration + scarring** |
| Inflammation | Mild | **More intense** (more debris, exudate, fibrin) |
| Granulation tissue | Little | **Abundant** |
| Contraction | **Minimal** | **Wound contracts (myofibroblasts)** |
| Scar | **Small** | **Large**; larger **clot/scab rich in fibrin and fibronectin** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hemostasis (minutes) → inflammation (6–48 h; M1 then M2) → proliferation (≤ 10 days) → remodeling (2–3 weeks onward).
- Granulation tissue = fibroblasts + new vessels + loose ECM + inflammatory cells.
- First intention: clean incision, small scar; second: large wound, abundant granulation, contraction, large scar.

</div>

**Answer:** Tissue unique to healing wounds made of proliferating fibroblasts, loose connective tissue, new blood vessels and scattered chronic inflammatory cells; grossly pink, soft and granular.

## 4. Angiogenesis, fibroblasts, remodeling and morphology

### Angiogenesis
- **Development of new vessels (sprouting) from existing vessels**; critical for **healing**, **collateral circulation in ischemia** and **tumor growth.** New vessels are **leaky** (incomplete interendothelial junctions; **VEGF** raises permeability) → **persistent edema** in healing wounds.

### Fibroblasts and connective tissue deposition
- Two steps: **fibroblast migration/proliferation** and **ECM deposition**, orchestrated by **PDGF, FGF-2 and TGF-β.** Fibroblasts enter from the wound edges; some become **myofibroblasts** that **pull the margins together**; they make **collagen**, the major scar component. ⭐ **TGF-β is the most important cytokine for connective tissue synthesis.**
- As healing progresses: **fewer fibroblasts and vessels, more ECM.** **Collagen synthesis begins on days 3–5** and lasts weeks; net accumulation = **↑ synthesis and ↓ degradation.** The mature scar is **pale and avascular** (vascular regression).

### Remodeling
- Strength rises through **collagen cross-linking, larger collagen fibers** and **a shift from type III to type I collagen**; well-sutured skin reaches **70–80% of normal strength by 3 months.** **Contraction:** first **myofibroblasts**, later cross-linking. **Matrix metalloproteinases** degrade connective tissue and the **scar shrinks.**

### Morphology and stains
- **Granulation tissue:** **fibroblasts + thin-walled delicate capillaries** in loose ECM with **macrophages**; amount depends on the **size of the deficit** and **intensity of inflammation.**
- **Scar:** **inactive spindle fibroblasts, dense collagen, fragments of elastic tissue, other ECM.**
- **Trichrome:** **collagen blue**, red cells orange, muscle red. **Elastin stain:** elastic fibers. **Reticulin** (**type III collagen**): stroma of normal organs, present in **early scars.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Angiogenesis by sprouting; new vessels leaky (VEGF) → edema.
- TGF-β = key cytokine; collagen synthesis from day 3–5; myofibroblasts contract the wound.
- Remodeling: cross-linking, type III → type I collagen, MMPs, 70–80% strength at 3 months; trichrome = collagen blue.

</div>

## 5. Factors impairing repair and excessive scarring

### Factors that impair tissue repair
- **Infection** (prolongs inflammation, increases local injury); **diabetes**; **nutrition** (**protein malnutrition, vitamin C deficiency** inhibit collagen synthesis); **glucocorticoids** (**inhibit TGF-β**, diminish fibrosis); **mechanical factors** (pressure or torsion → **dehiscence**); **poor perfusion** (arteriosclerosis, diabetes, varicose veins); **foreign bodies** (steel, glass, bone); **type and extent of injury**; **location and character of the tissue.**

### Excessive scarring
- Excess repair components can cause **hypertrophic scars and keloids.** A **keloid** = scar tissue that **grows beyond the boundaries of the original wound and does not regress** (thick dermal collagen microscopically).
- The slide also lists **exuberant granulation, desmoids (aggressive fibromatoses) and wound contraction** under excessive repair, without definitions.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Impaired repair: infection, diabetes, malnutrition (protein, vitamin C), steroids, mechanical stress, poor perfusion, foreign bodies.
- Excessive scarring: hypertrophic scar and keloid (extends beyond the wound, doesn't regress).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List factors that impair wound healing.</summary>

**Answer:** Infection, diabetes, poor nutrition (protein malnutrition, vitamin C deficiency), glucocorticoids, mechanical factors causing dehiscence, poor perfusion (arteriosclerosis, diabetes, varicose veins), foreign bodies, and the type, extent and location of injury.

**Why:** Slide "Factors that impair tissue repair".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do glucocorticoids and vitamin C deficiency impair repair?</summary>

**Answer:** Glucocorticoids are anti-inflammatory, inhibit TGF-β production and diminish fibrosis; vitamin C deficiency (like protein malnutrition) inhibits collagen synthesis and retards healing.

**Why:** Slide "Factors that impair tissue repair".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a keloid?</summary>

**Answer:** Excessive scar tissue that grows beyond the boundaries of the original wound and does not regress; microscopically there is thick connective tissue deposition in the dermis.

**Why:** Slides "Excessive scarring".
</details>
