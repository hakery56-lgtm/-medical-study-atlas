# Physical Examination of the Knee Joint — Exam Night Review

Clinical skills lecture (Husham Salah Hashem and Ali Samer, M.B.Ch.B., based on Macleod's Clinical Examination, 15th ed.): **surface anatomy of the knee, the clinical approach to knee pain, the Look-Feel-Move examination, special tests (collateral and cruciate ligaments, menisci, patella), the common peroneal nerve, the OSCE checklist and a clinical case.** After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

## 1. Surface anatomy

- **Anterior:** patella, quadriceps tendon (above), **patellar ligament** (inferior pole of patella → **tibial tuberosity**), femoral and tibial condyles.
- **Medial and lateral:** **joint lines** (assess meniscal tenderness), **MCL** (medial femoral epicondyle → medial proximal tibia), **LCL** (lateral femoral epicondyle → **fibular head**), head of fibula (posterolateral).
- **Posterior:** **popliteal fossa** (diamond-shaped): biceps femoris (superolateral), semitendinosus + semimembranosus (superomedial), gastrocnemius heads (inferior); **popliteal artery** pulse.

| Landmark | Clinical importance |
|---|---|
| **Tibial tuberosity** | Osgood-Schlatter disease |
| **Medial / lateral joint line** | Medial / lateral meniscal injury |
| **Fibular head** | ⭐ Common peroneal nerve injury |
| **Prepatellar region** | Prepatellar bursitis |
| **Popliteal fossa** | Baker's cyst |
| **Patella** | Dislocation and fractures |
| **MCL and LCL** | Collateral ligament injuries |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- MCL: medial femur → medial tibia; LCL: lateral femur → fibular head.
- Common peroneal nerve winds around the neck of the fibula.
- Joint lines are the sites of meniscal tenderness.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. State the attachments of the MCL and LCL.</summary>

**Answer:** MCL: medial femoral epicondyle to the medial proximal tibia. LCL: lateral femoral epicondyle to the head of the fibula.

**Why:** Slide 3 (Medial and Lateral landmarks).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the boundaries of the popliteal fossa.</summary>

**Answer:** Superolateral biceps femoris; superomedial semitendinosus and semimembranosus; inferior the two heads of gastrocnemius; the popliteal artery lies deep.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Match landmarks to conditions.</summary>

**Answer:** Tibial tuberosity - Osgood-Schlatter; joint lines - meniscal injury; fibular head - common peroneal nerve; prepatellar - bursitis; popliteal fossa - Baker's cyst; patella - dislocation/fracture.

**Why:** Slide 5 (Key Landmarks Summary).
</details>

## 2. Clinical approach to knee pain

| Category | Features | Think of |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Acute (traumatic or overuse)** | Recent injury; clear trauma history | **MCL/LCL, ACL, menisci**; test stability, focal tenderness, effusion, special tests |
| **2. Chronic overuse** | Pain **≥ about 6 weeks**, no sudden trauma, progressive, less activity needed to provoke pain, no instability | **Patellofemoral pain syndrome (diagnosis of exclusion)**, patellar conditions, pes anserinus disorders, iliotibial band syndrome, degenerative meniscal tears, **OA (> 40 years)** |
| **3. No trauma or overuse** | Fever or constitutional symptoms | Cause **less likely musculoskeletal**; investigate further |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Patellofemoral pain syndrome is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- Think OA in chronic knee pain over 40 years.
- Fever or constitutional symptoms point away from a musculoskeletal cause.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify knee pain into diagnostic categories.</summary>

**Answer:** Acute (traumatic or overuse), chronic associated with overuse, and pain without trauma or overuse.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define chronic overuse knee pain and list its causes.</summary>

**Answer:** Pain of about 6 weeks or more without sudden trauma, progressive. Causes: patellofemoral pain syndrome, patellar conditions, pes anserinus disorders, iliotibial band syndrome, degenerative meniscal tears, OA.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does fever in knee pain without trauma suggest?</summary>

**Answer:** The cause is less likely musculoskeletal; further investigation of systemic or other conditions is required.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

## 3. Introduction and Look

- **Before the exam:** hand hygiene, introduce yourself and confirm name and age, explain and get **verbal consent**, privacy and chaperone, ask about pain, **expose both legs**, comfort; **stand first** (gait, alignment) then **supine**. ⭐ **Always compare both knees; examine the unaffected knee first.**
- **Look:** gait and alignment: normally **slight valgus**; **genu valgum** (knock knees), **genu varum** (bow legs); unilateral deformity is almost always pathological. Standing: RA bilateral valgus, OA varus, unilateral Paget's disease.
- **Skin:** scars, sinuses, erythema. **Quadriceps wasting** occurs very quickly; measure thigh circumference **20 cm above the tibial tuberosity** on both sides.
- **Leg length** discrepancy and **flexion deformity** (from the hip, knee or both).
- **Swelling:** prepatellar bursa enlargement (**housemaid's knee**); effusion (large effusion: **horseshoe-shaped** swelling above the knee); swelling **beyond joint margins** → infection, major injury or rarely tumour; **Baker's cyst** in the popliteal fossa.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Compare both knees; unaffected knee first.
- Slight valgus is normal; unilateral deformity is pathological.
- Swelling beyond the joint margins: think infection, major injury, tumour.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the preparation steps before examining the knee.</summary>

**Answer:** Wash hands, introduce yourself, explain and obtain consent, privacy and chaperone, ask about pain, expose both legs, make the patient comfortable (standing first, then supine); unaffected knee first.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What do you look for with the patient standing and walking?</summary>

**Answer:** Gait, posture and alignment: normal slight valgus; genu valgum, genu varum; unilateral deformity is almost always pathological.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What do you look for with the patient supine?</summary>

**Answer:** Skin lesions, quadriceps wasting (thigh circumference 20 cm above the tibial tuberosity), leg length, flexion deformity, swelling (housemaid's knee, effusion, Baker's cyst).

**Why:** Slides 10-11.
</details>

## 4. Feel

- **Warmth:** compare with the **dorsum of the hand**, always against the opposite knee.
- **Patellar tap:** supine, knee extended; empty the suprapatellar pouch (left hand sliding down the thigh), press briskly on the patella with the right fingertips. **Positive:** patella taps against femur → **moderate effusion.**
- **Bulge (ripple) test:** extend knee, relax quadriceps; stroke medial side up, empty the pouch, stroke down the lateral side. **Positive:** ripple on the **medial** side. Detects **small** effusions; **false negative in tense effusions.**
- **Synovitis:** palpate both sides of the quadriceps tendon for **sponginess.**
- **Joint lines:** palpate medial and lateral, localise tenderness. In adolescents, tibial tuberosity tenderness → **Osgood-Schlatter disease** (traction apophysitis).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Patellar tap = moderate effusion; bulge test = small effusion.
- Sponginess beside the quadriceps tendon = synovitis.
- Adolescent tibial tuberosity tenderness = Osgood-Schlatter.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the patellar tap test.</summary>

**Answer:** Empty the suprapatellar pouch, hold it, and press briskly over the patella; the patella tapping against the femur indicates a moderate effusion.

**Why:** Slide 12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the bulge test and its limitation.</summary>

**Answer:** Milk fluid from the medial side to the pouch and lateral side, empty the pouch, then stroke down the lateral side; a ripple on the medial side is positive. It detects small effusions but may be falsely negative in tense effusions.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do you assess for synovitis and joint-line tenderness?</summary>

**Answer:** Extended knee, relaxed quadriceps: feel both sides of the quadriceps tendon for sponginess; palpate medial and lateral joint lines for localised tenderness.

**Why:** Slides 13-14.
</details>

## 5. Move

- **Active:** supine, flex the knee towards the chest, then extend. **Normal ROM 0-140°.** Feel for **crepitus** (chondromalacia patellae in younger females, or OA). Record ROM (fixed flexion 15° + flexion to 110° = **15-110°**). **Extensor lag:** lift the leg with the knee extended.
- **Passive extension:** femur and tibia aligned; full extension = **0°**; restricted in **meniscal tears, OA, inflammatory arthritis.** **Hyperextension (genu recurvatum):** beyond neutral; up to **10°** is normal.
- **Passive flexion:** patient **prone**; restriction may be due to a tear of the **posterior horn of the meniscus.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Normal range 0-140°; record fixed flexion first (e.g. 15-110°).
- Up to 10° of hyperextension is normal.
- Restricted passive flexion: posterior horn meniscal tear.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the normal range of movement and how is ROM recorded?</summary>

**Answer:** 0-140°; with fixed flexion of 15° and flexion up to 110° the ROM is recorded as 15-110°.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What may restrict passive extension?</summary>

**Answer:** Meniscal tears, osteoarthritis and inflammatory arthritis.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is passive flexion tested and what may limit it?</summary>

**Answer:** Prone, flex the knee to maximum and compare sides; a tear of the posterior horn of the meniscus may restrict it.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

## 6. Special tests

| Test | Structure assessed |
|---|---|
| **Valgus stress** | **MCL** |
| **Varus stress** | **LCL** |
| **Anterior drawer** | **ACL** |
| **Lachman** | **ACL** |
| **Posterior drawer** | **PCL** |
| **Rapid knee extension** | Meniscal injury screening |
| **McMurray** | Medial and lateral menisci |
| **Patellar apprehension** | Patellar instability |

- **Collateral ligaments:** supine, knee fully extended, ankle held between elbow and side, thumbs on joint line; compare with the other side; repeat at **30° flexion** if stable. **Normal:** no opening. **Partial tear:** pain without opening. **Complete tear:** abnormal opening. **Major opening in full extension** = combined collateral + cruciate injury.
- **ACL** (medial aspect of lateral femoral condyle → anterior tibial plateau; limits anterior tibial movement). **Anterior drawer:** knee **90°**, foot stabilised, hamstrings relaxed; check **posterior sag**; pull the tibia anteriorly; **> 1.5 cm** suggests ACL rupture; **posterior sag = false positive.** **Lachman:** knee **20-30°**, stabilise femur, pull tibia forward; ACL deficiency = increased translation with **soft or absent endpoint.**
- **PCL, posterior drawer:** knee 90°, push the tibia posteriorly; excessive movement = PCL laxity.
- **Menisci:** present with twisting injury (young sporty), degenerative horizontal cleavage tears (middle age), slow-onset effusion, joint-line tenderness. **Rapid extension** from 30° flexion: joint-line pain = possible tear. **McMurray:** fully flex; **medial** meniscus = **external rotation + varus** (abduct leg); **lateral** meniscus = **internal rotation + valgus** (adduct leg); slowly extend; **click or clunk with discomfort** = tear.
- **Patella:** look for bursa swelling, palpate borders for tenderness. **Apprehension test:** push the patella laterally while slowly flexing; patient resists due to fear = previous dislocation or instability. Other patellofemoral tests are unreliable.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Valgus stress = MCL; varus stress = LCL.
- Anterior drawer / Lachman = ACL; posterior drawer = PCL.
- McMurray: external rotation + varus = medial meniscus; internal rotation + valgus = lateral meniscus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe collateral ligament testing and its interpretation.</summary>

**Answer:** Knee extended, valgus stress for MCL and varus stress for LCL, thumbs on the joint line, compare sides, repeat at 30° if stable. Pain without opening is a partial tear; opening is a complete tear; major opening in extension suggests combined collateral and cruciate injury.

**Why:** Slides 17-18.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the anterior drawer and Lachman tests.</summary>

**Answer:** Both test the ACL. Anterior drawer: knee 90°, pull the tibia forward, more than 1.5 cm suggests rupture, posterior sag causes false positives. Lachman: knee 20-30°, stabilise the femur, increased translation with soft or absent endpoint.

**Why:** Slides 18-20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the McMurray test.</summary>

**Answer:** Fully flex the knee; medial meniscus: externally rotate the foot with varus stress; lateral meniscus: internally rotate with valgus stress; slowly extend and feel or hear a click or clunk with discomfort.

**Why:** Slide 21.
</details>

## 7. Common peroneal nerve, wrap-up and case

- **Causes of injury:** fractures around the **fibular head**, compression in thin immobile patients, repetitive kneeling, squatting, sitting with legs crossed. **Presentation: foot drop.**
- **Motor:** test **dorsiflexion, eversion, big toe extension (EHL)**; **inversion and ankle reflex are preserved.** **Sensory:** dorsum of the foot.
- **OSCE checklist:** introduction → gait → Look → Feel → Move → special tests (**anterior drawer, Lachman, McMurray**) → distal neurovascular assessment → examine the joint above and below.
- **Case:** 60-year-old obese woman, 7 months bilateral knee pain, worse with walking, stairs and squatting, better with rest, worse in the evening, gelling, **10 min** morning stiffness, progressive over 3 years, no trauma. Exam: antalgic gait, **genu varus**, fixed flexion deformity, quadriceps wasting, bony enlargement, **no effusion**, joint-line tenderness, limited movement with crepitus. **Diagnosis: primary osteoarthritis** (chronic overuse category, over 40, stable knee; varus ← medial compartment).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Common peroneal injury: foot drop; inversion and ankle reflex preserved.
- Finish the exam with the neurovascular bundle and the joints above and below.
- Chronic overuse pain, over 40, stable knee, varus = primary OA.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List causes and presentation of common peroneal nerve injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Fibular head fractures, compression in thin immobile patients, kneeling, squatting, crossing legs; presents with foot drop.

**Why:** Slide 23.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do you examine the common peroneal nerve?</summary>

**Answer:** Test ankle dorsiflexion, eversion and big toe extension; inversion and ankle reflex are preserved; test sensation over the dorsum of the foot.

**Why:** Slide 23.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Reason out the diagnosis in the case of the 60-year-old woman.</summary>

**Answer:** Chronic overuse pain, age over 40, stable knee, varus, medial compartment, bony enlargement and crepitus without effusion: primary osteoarthritis.

**Why:** Slide 24.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Valgus stress = MCL;** varus stress = LCL (not the reverse).
- **Posterior sag** can cause a **false-positive anterior drawer.**
- **Bulge test** can be falsely negative in **tense** effusions; patellar tap detects **moderate** effusions.
- McMurray: **external rotation + varus = medial;** internal rotation + valgus = lateral.
- Common peroneal injury spares **inversion and the ankle reflex.**
- **Patellofemoral pain syndrome** is a diagnosis of **exclusion.**

2. Fibular head = common peroneal nerve; tibial tuberosity = Osgood-Schlatter; popliteal fossa = Baker's cyst.
3. Three pain categories: acute, chronic overuse, no trauma/overuse.
4. Always compare both knees; unaffected side first.
5. Thigh circumference measured 20 cm above the tibial tuberosity.
6. Patellar tap (moderate) and bulge test (small) for effusion.
7. Normal ROM 0-140°; hyperextension up to 10° is normal.
8. Anterior drawer at 90° (> 1.5 cm); Lachman at 20-30°; posterior drawer for PCL.
9. McMurray for the menisci; patellar apprehension for instability.
10. Foot drop; test dorsiflexion, eversion, big toe extension; sensation on the dorsum of the foot.
11. OSCE order: introduction, gait, Look, Feel, Move, special tests, neurovascular, joint above and below.
