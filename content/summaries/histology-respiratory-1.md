## 1. Divisions of the respiratory system and the respiratory mucosa

| Portion | Parts |
|---|---|
| **Conducting portion** | **Nasal cavities, nasopharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, terminal bronchioles** |
| **Respiratory portion** (**gas exchange**) | **Respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs, alveoli** |

- **Respiratory mucosa** covers **most of the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, nasopharynx, larynx, trachea and bronchi**.
- It consists of: **(1) Epithelium** = **pseudostratified columnar (respiratory) epithelium**; **(2) Lamina propria** = **highly vascular**, **firmly attached to the periosteum or perichondrium** of the underlying bone or cartilage.
- The lamina propria contains **immune cells** (**macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells, mast cells, lymphoid nodules**) and **mucoserous glands**; their mucus + the mucus of **goblet cells** form the **mucous blanket**, propelled by **cilia toward the pharynx**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Conducting portion ends at the **terminal bronchioles**; gas exchange starts at the **respiratory bronchioles**.
- Respiratory mucosa = **pseudostratified columnar ciliated epithelium** + **vascular lamina propria** attached to bone/cartilage.
- Mucous blanket (goblet cells + mucoserous glands) is moved by cilia toward the pharynx.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Divide the respiratory system into its two portions and list their parts.</summary>

**Answer:** The conducting portion (nasal cavities, nasopharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, terminal bronchioles) carries air; the respiratory portion (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs, alveoli) is where gas exchange takes place.

**Why:** Slide "The Respiratory System consists of".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the respiratory mucosa.</summary>

**Answer:** It covers most of the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, nasopharynx, larynx, trachea and bronchi and has a pseudostratified columnar (respiratory) epithelium over a highly vascular lamina propria firmly attached to the periosteum or perichondrium of underlying bone or cartilage.

**Why:** Slide "Conducting portion — Respiratory mucosa".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does the lamina propria of the respiratory mucosa contain?</summary>

**Answer:** Immune cells (macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells, mast cells, lymphoid nodules) and mucoserous glands, whose mucus together with goblet cell mucus forms the mucous blanket moved by cilia toward the pharynx.

**Why:** Slide "Conducting portion — Respiratory mucosa".
</details>

## 2. Respiratory epithelium — the five cell types

All five cell types **contact an unusually thick basement membrane**.

| Cell | Key facts |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Ciliated columnar cells** | **Most abundant**; about **300 cilia** per apical surface |
| **Goblet cells** | Apical granules of **mucin glycoproteins**; secretion **increases in infection or irritation** |
| **Brush cells** | Fewer columnar cells with a **tuft of microvilli**; **chemosensory receptors** resembling **gustatory cells**; **synapse with afferent nerve endings** on their basal surface |
| **Small granule cells** | **Diffuse neuroendocrine system**; like basal cells but with **numerous dense granules**; **about 3%** of cells |
| **Basal cells** | **Small rounded cells** on the basement membrane, **do not reach the lumen**; **stem cells** for the other types |

- **Immotile cilia syndrome:** immobility of cilia and flagella (in some cases **dynein deficiency**) → **male infertility** and **chronic respiratory tract infections in both sexes**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five cells: ciliated, goblet, brush, small granule, basal; ciliated = most abundant (~300 cilia).
- Basal = stem cells; small granule = neuroendocrine (~3%); brush = chemosensory.
- Immotile cilia syndrome: dynein deficiency → infertility + chronic respiratory infections.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the five cell types of the respiratory epithelium and the role of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Ciliated columnar cells (most abundant, move mucus), goblet cells (mucin), brush cells (chemosensory), small granule cells (neuroendocrine, about 3%) and basal cells (stem cells).

**Why:** Slides "Respiratory epithelium", "Ciliated columnar cells", "Goblet cells", "Brush cells", "Small granule cells / Basal cells".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is immotile cilia syndrome?</summary>

**Answer:** A disorder caused by immobility of cilia and flagella, in some cases due to deficiency of dynein, causing infertility in men and chronic respiratory tract infections in both sexes.

**Why:** Slide "Ciliated columnar cells — Immotile cilia syndrome".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare brush cells and small granule cells.</summary>

**Answer:** Brush cells have a small apical surface with a tuft of microvilli and act as chemosensory receptors that synapse with afferent nerve endings; small granule cells belong to the diffuse neuroendocrine system, resemble basal cells but have numerous dense granules and make up about 3% of the cells.

**Why:** Slides "Brush cells" and "Small granule cells".
</details>

## 3. Functions of the conducting portion

| Problem | What deals with it |
|---|---|
| **Dry air** | **Moistened** by goblet cells and mucus |
| **Particles** | **Trapped** by hairs, goblet cells and mucus; **removed** by cilia |
| **Cold air** | **Warmed** by blood vessels near the epithelium |
| **Bacteria** | **Destroyed** by lymphoid cells under the epithelium |

- **Support and flexibility:** **cartilage, elastic and collagen fibers and smooth muscle** keep the airway open and give flexibility and extensibility.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Moisten (goblet cells + mucus), trap (hairs, goblet cells, mucus), remove (cilia), warm (vessels), destroy bacteria (lymphoid cells).
- Cartilage + elastic/collagen fibers + smooth muscle = rigid yet flexible airway.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does the conducting portion condition and clean inspired air?</summary>

**Answer:** Goblet cells and mucus moisten dry air; hairs, goblet cells and mucus trap particles and cilia remove them; blood vessels near the epithelium warm cold air; lymphoid cells under the epithelium destroy bacteria.

**Why:** Slide "Function of conducting portion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which structures remove particles from the airway?</summary>

**Answer:** The cilia move the particle-laden mucus; the particles are first trapped by hairs, goblet cells and mucus.

**Why:** Slide "Function of conducting portion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What provides support and flexibility to the conducting portion?</summary>

**Answer:** A combination of cartilage, elastic and collagen fibers and smooth muscle, ensuring an uninterrupted supply of air.

**Why:** Slide "Function of conducting portion".
</details>

## 4. The nasal cavities

- Each nasal cavity has **two components**: the **external dilated vestibule** and the **nasal cavity proper** (inside the skull).
- **Vestibule:** nasal **skin** with **sweat glands, sebaceous glands and coarse, moist vibrissae** that **filter particulate material**.
- The two cavities are separated by the **osseous nasal septum**; each lateral wall has **three conchae (turbinate bones)**.
- ⭐ **Middle and inferior conchae = respiratory epithelium**; **roof and superior conchae = olfactory epithelium**.
- **Lamina propria:** **capillary loops near the surface** carry blood **counter to the flow of inspired air**, releasing heat to **warm** it, while **small seromucous glands** secrete water to **humidify** it.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Vestibule = skin + vibrissae; nasal cavity proper = septum + three conchae.
- Middle/inferior conchae respiratory; roof/superior concha olfactory.
- Counter-current capillaries warm the air; seromucous glands humidify it.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the two components of each nasal cavity.</summary>

**Answer:** The external dilated vestibule, lined by skin with sweat glands, sebaceous glands and vibrissae that filter particles, and the nasal cavity proper within the skull, separated from its fellow by the osseous nasal septum and bearing three conchae on each lateral wall.

**Why:** Slide "The nasal cavities (respiratory area)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which areas of the nasal cavity have respiratory and which have olfactory epithelium?</summary>

**Answer:** The middle and inferior conchae have respiratory epithelium; the roof of the nasal cavities and the superior conchae have specialized olfactory epithelium.

**Why:** Slide "The nasal cavities (respiratory area)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is inspired air warmed and humidified in the nasal cavity?</summary>

**Answer:** Capillary loops near the surface carry blood counter to the airflow and release heat, and small seromucous glands secrete water that humidifies the air.

**Why:** Slide "Lamina propria of nasal cavities".
</details>

## 5. The olfactory region

- **Location:** specialized mucosa covering the **superior concha** at the roof of the nasal cavity; about **10 cm² and up to 100 µm thick**; **pseudostratified columnar epithelium** with **three cell types**.
- **Olfactory (Bowman's) glands:** **large serous glands** in the lamina propria; produce a **constant flow of fluid** around the olfactory cilia so new odorants can reach them.

| Cell | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Basal cells** | Small, spherical or cone-shaped; layer at the basal lamina; **stem cells** for the other two |
| **Supporting cells** | **Columnar**, broad apex and narrow base; **microvilli** in a fluid layer |
| ⭐ **Olfactory neurons** | **Bipolar neurons**; nuclei **between supporting and basal cell nuclei**; dendrites with **non-motile cilia** bearing chemoreceptors |

- **Pathway:** axons → small nerves in the lamina propria → **foramina of the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone** → **olfactory nerve (CN I)** → synapse in the **olfactory bulb**.
- **Clinical:** olfactory neurons are **regularly replaced** by epithelial stem cells, so smell loss from toxic fumes or epithelial injury is **usually temporary**; **ethmoid bone damage shearing the axons** (with blocked regeneration through the cribriform plate) can cause **more permanent loss**.
- Note: the slide writes "100 m" for the thickness; read it as 100 µm.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Olfactory epithelium: superior concha, ~10 cm², up to 100 µm; basal + supporting + olfactory neurons.
- Olfactory neurons = bipolar, non-motile cilia; axons cross the cribriform plate to form CN I → olfactory bulb.
- Neurons are replaced from basal stem cells; ethmoid injury can cause permanent anosmia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name and describe the three cell types of the olfactory epithelium.</summary>

**Answer:** Basal cells (small stem cells at the basal lamina), supporting cells (columnar with microvilli in a fluid layer) and olfactory neurons (bipolar neurons with non-motile cilia bearing chemoreceptors, nuclei between those of supporting and basal cells).

**Why:** Slide "Cells of olfactory epithelium".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Trace the olfactory pathway from the epithelium to the brain.</summary>

**Answer:** Axons leave the epithelium, unite in the lamina propria as small nerves, pass through foramina in the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone, form the olfactory nerve (CN I) and synapse with neurons in the olfactory bulb.

**Why:** Slide "Cells of olfactory epithelium".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is loss of smell often temporary, and when is it permanent?</summary>

**Answer:** Olfactory neurons are replaced regularly from the epithelial stem cells, so toxic or physical injury is usually temporary; damage to the ethmoid bone can shear the axons and, if regeneration through the cribriform plate is also blocked, loss becomes more permanent.

**Why:** Slide "Medical application".
</details>

## 6. The paranasal sinuses

- **Bilateral air-filled cavities** in the **frontal, maxillary, ethmoid and sphenoid** bones.
- **Lining:** **thinner respiratory epithelium with fewer goblet cells**; lamina propria has **few small glands** and is **continuous with the periosteum**.
- They **communicate with the nasal cavities through small openings**; mucus is moved into the nasal passages by **ciliated cells**.
- **Functions:** ⭐ **lighten the skull** and give **resonance and amplification to the voice**.
- **Sinusitis:** inflammation of the sinuses that may persist, mainly because of **obstruction of the drainage orifices**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sinuses: frontal, maxillary, ethmoid, sphenoid; thin respiratory epithelium with few goblet cells.
- Drain into the nasal cavities by ciliary movement; lighten the skull and give voice resonance.
- Sinusitis persists because drainage orifices are obstructed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Where are the paranasal sinuses and how are they lined?</summary>

**Answer:** In the frontal, maxillary, ethmoid and sphenoid bones; they are air-filled and lined by a thinner respiratory epithelium with fewer goblet cells over a lamina propria with few small glands that is continuous with the periosteum.

**Why:** Slide "Paranasal sinuses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does mucus leave the sinuses?</summary>

**Answer:** The sinuses communicate with the nasal cavities through small openings, and ciliated epithelial cells move the mucus into the nasal passages.

**Why:** Slide "Paranasal sinuses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the functions of the sinuses and why may sinusitis persist?</summary>

**Answer:** They lighten the skull and give resonance and amplification to the voice; sinusitis may persist for long periods mainly because the drainage orifices become obstructed.

**Why:** Slide "Paranasal sinuses".
</details>

## 7. The larynx

- **Size/role:** short air passage (**4 cm × 4 cm**) between the **pharynx and trachea**; held open by cartilages connected by ligaments; their movement by **skeletal muscles** takes part in **phonation**.

| Cartilage type | Which |
|---|---|
| **Hyaline** | **Thyroid, cricoid, inferior arytenoid** |
| **Elastic** | **Epiglottis, cuneiform, corniculate, superior arytenoid** |

- **Epiglottis:** prevents food/fluid entering the larynx; **lingual surface = stratified squamous**, **laryngeal surface = transition to ciliated pseudostratified (respiratory) epithelium**; **mixed mucous and serous glands** in the lamina propria.
- Below the epiglottis: **two pairs of folds** separated by the **ventricle**:
  - **Vestibular folds (upper, immovable):** **respiratory epithelium**, **seromucous glands**, occasional **lymphoid nodules**.
  - ⭐ **Vocal folds (lower):** **non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium** (protects against abrasion and desiccation), **vocal ligament** (dense regular **elastic** tissue) at the free edge, **vocalis muscle** (**striated**) deep to the mucosa for movement.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hyaline: thyroid, cricoid, inferior arytenoid; elastic: epiglottis, cuneiform, corniculate, superior arytenoid.
- Epiglottis: stratified squamous on the lingual side → respiratory epithelium on the laryngeal side.
- Vestibular folds = respiratory epithelium, immovable; vocal folds = non-keratinized stratified squamous + vocal ligament + vocalis muscle.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify the laryngeal cartilages.</summary>

**Answer:** Hyaline: thyroid, cricoid and inferior arytenoid. Elastic: epiglottis, cuneiform, corniculate and superior arytenoid; all are connected by ligaments.

**Why:** Slide "Larynx".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the epiglottis.</summary>

**Answer:** A flattened structure from the upper rim of the larynx that prevents food or fluid from entering it; the lingual surface has stratified squamous epithelium that changes on the laryngeal surface to ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium, with mixed mucous and serous glands in the lamina propria.

**Why:** Slide "Larynx (The epiglottis)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the vestibular folds with the vocal folds.</summary>

**Answer:** The upper vestibular folds are immovable, covered with respiratory epithelium over seromucous glands and lymphoid nodules; the lower vocal folds have non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, a vocal ligament of dense elastic tissue and a deep vocalis (striated) muscle that moves them for phonation.

**Why:** Slide "Larynx" (folds).
</details>

## 8. The trachea

- **10-12 cm** long in adults; **typical respiratory mucosa**; lamina propria with numerous **seromucous glands** producing **watery mucus**.
- **About 12 C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings** between submucosa and adventitia keep the lumen open; **open ends are posterior**, against the **esophagus**, bridged by the **trachealis muscle (smooth muscle)** and a **fibroelastic sheet** attached to the perichondrium.
- ⭐ **Trachealis muscle:** **relaxes in swallowing** (esophagus bulges into the trachea; elastic layer prevents over-distention); **contracts strongly in the cough reflex** → narrower lumen, faster expelled air.
- **Wall layers (inside out):** **(1) Mucosa** (ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium with goblet cells + lamina propria); **(2) Submucosa** (not clearly demarcated from the lamina propria; **mixed glands** in the deeper lamina propria and submucosa); **(3) Hyaline cartilage rings** (incomplete dorsally) with the **trachealis muscle** dorsally, internal to the gap; **(4) Adventitia**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Trachea: 10-12 cm, ~12 C-shaped hyaline rings open posteriorly, closed by the trachealis muscle.
- Trachealis relaxes in swallowing and contracts in coughing.
- Layers: mucosa, submucosa, cartilage rings (+ trachealis), adventitia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the cartilage support of the trachea.</summary>

**Answer:** About 12 C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage between the submucosa and adventitia keep the lumen open; their open ends lie posteriorly against the esophagus and are bridged by the trachealis muscle and a fibroelastic sheet.

**Why:** Slide "The trachea".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the role of the trachealis muscle?</summary>

**Answer:** It relaxes during swallowing so the esophagus can bulge into the tracheal lumen, with the elastic layer preventing over-distention, and contracts strongly in the cough reflex to narrow the lumen and increase the velocity of expelled air.

**Why:** Slide "The trachea".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the layers of the tracheal wall.</summary>

**Answer:** Mucosa (ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium with goblet cells and lamina propria), submucosa, hyaline cartilage rings with the dorsal trachealis muscle, and adventitia of connective tissue.

**Why:** Slide "The trachea" (second slide).
</details>

- **Immotile cilia syndrome = dynein deficiency → infertility in MEN + chronic respiratory infections in BOTH sexes.**
