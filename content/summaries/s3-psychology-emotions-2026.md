## 1. Emotion, feeling, affect and mood

- ⭐ **Emotion** = a **complex state of feeling** with **physical and psychological changes** that influence **thought and behavior**, usually involving the **ego**; **unique, perception-based, subjective.**

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Emotion** | **The response**: complex psychological state with subjective experience, physiological and behavioral response; **short-lived, triggered by specific events** | **Fear, joy** |
| **Feeling** | **The subjective experience**: physical or emotional sensations | **"I feel afraid"; sharp foot pain; warmth from a hug** |
| **Affect** | **The observable expression** (face, voice, body language); may or may not be **congruent with mood**; **normal, constricted, blunt or flat** | **Fearful facial expression** |
| **Mood** | **Pervasive and sustained emotion** coloring perception; described by **intensity, duration, fluctuations, adjectival type** | **Cheerful or irritable for days** |

- ⭐ **Mood = what the patient says they have been feeling over a period of time; affect = what the clinician observes at the time of examination.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Emotion = response; feeling = subjective experience; affect = observable expression; mood = sustained state.
- Mood is reported; affect is observed.
- Mood description: intensity, duration, fluctuation, type.

</div>

## 2. Components and responses of emotion

- ⭐ **Four components:** **1. cognitive appraisal** (interpretation of the situation), **2. subjective experience** (feeling), **3. physiological response** (body changes), **4. behavioral expression** (observable behavior).
- **Medical student example:** "I may not know the answer; everyone is watching me" → anxious, nervous, embarrassed, afraid → ↑ heart rate, faster breathing, sweaty palms, "butterflies" → hesitation, avoiding eye contact, shaky voice.
- **Anger example (6 steps):** situation (trigger) → feeling → physical changes → psychological changes → influence on thought and behavior → **role of the ego** (my safety, rights, reputation, self-esteem, values).
- **Physiological response:** controlled by the **sympathetic nervous system (fight or flight)**: sweating palms, racing heartbeat, rapid breathing, dilated pupils, decreased digestion, increased blood pressure, muscle tension.
- **Behavioral response** = the **expression of emotion** (tied to **emotional intelligence**): **facial expression** (wide eyes, raised eyebrows, open mouth), **body language** (tense posture, hands up, leaning back) and responses to threat: **freeze, run (flight), fight.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four components: appraisal, feeling, physiological response, behavioral expression.
- Physiological response = sympathetic (fight or flight).
- Threat behaviors: freeze, flight, fight.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the four components of emotion with an example from the lecture.</summary>

**Answer:** Cognitive appraisal ("this is a threat to me"), subjective experience (anxiety), physiological response (↑ heart rate, sweating) and behavioral expression (hesitation, shaky voice).

**Why:** Slide "The Four Components of Emotion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which system controls the physiological response and what are its signs?</summary>

**Answer:** The sympathetic nervous system: sweating palms, racing heartbeat, rapid breathing, dilated pupils, decreased digestion, increased blood pressure and muscle tension.

**Why:** Slide "1. Physiological Response".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the behavioral responses to threat?</summary>

**Answer:** Freeze, run (flight) and fight, plus fearful facial expression and defensive body language.

</details>

## 3. Primary, secondary and tertiary emotions

| Level | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | **First, immediate reactions to a stimulus**; **8 basic emotions** | **Love, joy, surprise, anger, sadness, fear, disgust, anticipation** |
| **Secondary** | **Develop from primary emotions with cognitive processing**; shaped by **social and cultural contexts**; **reflective, evaluative** | **Guilt, shame, pride, embarrassment, jealousy** |
| **Tertiary** | **Augment first and secondary emotions**; **deeper cognitive processing and self-reflection**; vary between persons | **Nostalgia, existential angst, fulfillment** |

- **Eight primary emotions:** **love** (deep affection), **joy** (happiness), **surprise** (sudden reaction), **anger** (irritation or hostility), **sadness** (sorrow, grief), **fear** (threat or danger), **disgust** (dislike or rejection), **anticipation** (expectation).
- **Secondary from primary:** fear + "what will people think?" → **shame**; fear/sadness + "I did something wrong" → **guilt**; joy + "I achieved something" → **pride**; fear/shame + "everyone is looking at me" → **embarrassment**; love/anger + comparison → **jealousy.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Eight primary emotions: love, joy, surprise, anger, sadness, fear, disgust, anticipation.
- Secondary: guilt, shame, pride, embarrassment, jealousy.
- Tertiary: nostalgia, existential angst, fulfillment.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the eight primary emotions.</summary>

**Answer:** Love, joy, surprise, anger, sadness, fear, disgust and anticipation.

**Why:** Slide "The Eight Primary Emotions".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do secondary emotions arise? Give two examples.</summary>

**Answer:** From the cognitive interpretation of primary emotions: fear + "what will people think?" gives shame; joy + "I achieved something important" gives pride.

**Why:** Slide "Examples of Secondary Emotions".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are tertiary emotions?</summary>

**Answer:** Emotions that augment primary and secondary ones, involving deeper cognitive processing and self-reflection (nostalgia, existential angst, fulfillment).

**Why:** Slide "Primary versus Secondary Emotion".
</details>

## 4. Theories of emotion

| Approach | Idea | Example (boss's phone call) |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **A. Peripheral: James-Lange** | **Stimulus → body changes → feeling** | Heart rate, sweating, tension → anxiety |
| **A. Peripheral: facial feedback** | **Facial expression influences emotion** (via facial feedback to the brain) | Frowning → ↑ anxiety; smiling → ↑ happiness or ↓ anxiety |
| ⭐ **B. Cognitive appraisal** | **Emotion depends on how we interpret the situation** | Threat → anxiety/fear; praise → happiness/excitement |
| ⭐ **C. Affective neuroscience** | **Brain circuits** evaluate stimuli and produce emotion | Amygdala, thalamus, visual cortex, prefrontal cortex |

- **Brain imaging:** emotional disorders (anxiety, phobias) may involve **increased amygdala activity** and **altered amygdala-prefrontal connections.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- James-Lange: body changes first, then the feeling.
- Cognitive appraisal: interpretation decides the emotion.
- Affective neuroscience: amygdala and prefrontal cortex circuits.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the James-Lange theory.</summary>

**Answer:** We feel emotion because of the physiological changes that follow a stimulus (stimulus → body changes → feeling).

**Why:** Slide "A. Peripheral Theories".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What does the cognitive appraisal theory say about the boss's phone call?</summary>

**Answer:** Appraised as a threat it causes anxiety; appraised as praise it causes happiness and excitement; the same situation gives different emotions depending on appraisal.

**Why:** Slide "B. Cognitive Appraisal Theory".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does brain imaging suggest in anxiety and phobias?</summary>

**Answer:** Increased amygdala activity and altered connections between the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex.

**Why:** Slide "C. The Affective Neuroscience Approach".
</details>

## 5. Brain circuits of fear

- ⭐ **Slower circuit:** eyes → **thalamus** (relay) → **visual cortex** (processes shape, size, color) → **amygdala** (interprets as threat, fear response) → increased heart rate, shallow breathing, sweaty palms, fearful face, running; **total about 0.12 s.**
- ⭐ **Faster circuit:** eyes → thalamus → **amygdala directly**, **bypassing the visual cortex** → **almost instantaneous**, **before conscious identification** (survival value).
- **Timeline:** initial perception **0-50 ms** → faster circuit → slower circuit (~0.12 s) → **prefrontal cortex** evaluation and decision → behavior.
- **Prefrontal cortex (PFC):** **decision-making, planning, reasoning, regulation of emotion**; remembers emotions, anticipates consequences (rewards and punishments), regulates the amygdala; the **party versus exam** example shows emotional drives can override known consequences (amygdala and reward system).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Faster circuit: thalamus → amygdala (bypasses visual cortex).
- Slower circuit: thalamus → visual cortex → amygdala (~0.12 s).
- PFC evaluates and regulates the fear response.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the faster and slower fear circuits.</summary>

**Answer:** The faster circuit goes thalamus → amygdala, bypassing the visual cortex, giving an almost instantaneous response before conscious identification; the slower circuit goes thalamus → visual cortex → amygdala (~0.12 s) and identifies the threat accurately.

**Why:** Slides "Slower Circuit" and "Faster Circuit".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the roles of the amygdala, thalamus and visual cortex?</summary>

**Answer:** The thalamus relays sensory information; the visual cortex processes the detailed image; the amygdala detects the threat and triggers the fear response.

**Why:** Slide "Brain circuits involved".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does the prefrontal cortex do in emotion?</summary>

**Answer:** Evaluates the situation, plans, reasons, anticipates consequences and regulates the emotional response of the amygdala, guiding behavior (run, stay or seek help).

**Why:** Slide "C. Prefrontal Cortex".
</details>

## 6. Normal and abnormal emotional reactions

- ⭐ **Normal emotional reactions:** normal responses to events (e.g. the **grief reaction after death of a loved one**, or a healthy person's reaction to a **life-threatening diagnosis**).
- ⭐ **Abnormal emotional reactions:** **understandable in the context of stressful events** but with **more prolonged impairment of functioning.**

| Symptom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Depression** | **Persistent sadness, low mood, loss of interest or pleasure** |
| **Anxiety** | **Excessive fear, worry, apprehension** |
| **Irritability** | **Easily annoyed or angry** |
| **Blunted affect** | **Markedly reduced intensity of emotional expression** |
| **Flat affect** | **Almost complete absence of observable expression** |
| **Euphoria** | **Unusually elevated well-being** |
| **Dysphoria** | **Persistent distress, dissatisfaction or unease** |
| **Anhedonia** | **Reduced ability to experience pleasure** |
| **Apathy** | **Reduced motivation or emotional engagement** |
| **Pathological guilt** | **Excessive or inappropriate guilt** |
| **Fearfulness** | **Excessive or disproportionate fear** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Normal = grief reaction or response to a serious diagnosis; abnormal = prolonged functional impairment.
- Blunted = reduced; flat = almost absent affect.
- Anhedonia = no pleasure; apathy = no motivation.

</div>
