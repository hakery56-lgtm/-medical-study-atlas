## 1. Surgical neck fracture of the humerus

**Case:** a 70-year-old woman falls on an outstretched hand — pain, inability to lift the arm, ecchymosis over the proximal arm, crepitus, **radial pulse intact**.

- **High-risk groups:** **elderly (osteoporosis), seizures, electrocution**.
- **Structures at risk:** ⭐ **axillary nerve** (about **5 cm distal to the acromion**) and the **posterior circumflex humeral artery** beside it.
- Called **"surgical"** because of the **high risk of neurovascular injury** (unlike the anatomical neck).
- Axillary nerve supplies **deltoid and teres minor** → injury gives:
  - **Loss of abduction beyond 15°** (deltoid abducts **15°–110°**; **supraspinatus initiates the first 15°**).
  - **Loss of shoulder contour** (deltoid wasting, "flat shoulder").
  - **Sensory loss over the "regimental badge" area** (upper lateral arm).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Surgical neck → axillary nerve + posterior circumflex humeral artery.
- Deltoid 15°–110°; supraspinatus 0°–15°.
- Regimental badge sensory loss; flat shoulder.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the surgical neck of the humerus called "surgical", and which structures are at risk?</summary>

**Answer:** Because fractures there carry a high risk of neurovascular injury. The axillary nerve, about 5 cm below the acromion, and the posterior circumflex humeral artery wind around the surgical neck.

**Why:** Key definition from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the clinical effects of axillary nerve injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Paralysis of the deltoid and teres minor: loss of abduction from 15° to 110° (the supraspinatus still starts the first 15°), flattening of the shoulder contour from deltoid wasting, and sensory loss over the regimental badge area of the upper lateral arm.

**Why:** Motor + sensory + deformity.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which patients are most prone to this fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** Elderly patients with osteoporosis, patients with seizures (violent muscle contraction) and victims of electrocution.

**Why:** A classic list.
</details>

## 2. Midshaft humeral fracture

**Case:** a 35-year-old construction worker falls from scaffolding — mid-arm deformity, **weak wrist extension**, numbness over the **dorsum of the hand**.

- **"Hanging arm"** deformity: the distal fragment hangs by gravity.
- ⭐ **Radial nerve** wraps around the **spiral groove** (**10–14 cm** distal to the acromion) with the **profunda brachii artery**.
  - The **triceps is usually spared** (its branches arise **proximal** to the groove).
  - Test by **wrist and finger extension**; the nerve then emerges lateral to brachioradialis (sensory: **dorsum of the hand**).
- ⭐ **Wrist drop** = paralysis of the wrist and finger extensors (most characteristic sign).

### Displacement depends on the site of the fracture
| Site | Proximal segment | Distal segment |
|---|---|---|
| **Above the bicipital groove** | **Abducted** (greater tuberosity muscles, mainly supraspinatus) | **Adducted** (muscles of the bicipital groove) |
| **Above the deltoid tuberosity** | **Adducted** (bicipital groove muscles) | **Abducted and pulled up by the deltoid** |
| **Below the deltoid tuberosity** | **Abducted by the deltoid** | **Adducted and pulled up by biceps and triceps** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Spiral groove: radial nerve + profunda brachii artery; wrist drop.
- Triceps spared; sensory loss on the dorsum of the hand.
- Displacement follows the muscle attachments above/below the pectoralis major–deltoid levels.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the radial nerve injured in midshaft fractures and what is the typical sign?</summary>

**Answer:** It lies in the spiral groove directly on the shaft (10–14 cm below the acromion) with the profunda brachii artery, so it can be lacerated or stretched. The typical sign is wrist drop; the triceps is spared because its branches leave the nerve proximal to the groove.

**Why:** Mechanism + sign.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain how the displacement of the fragments depends on the site of the fracture.</summary>

**Answer:** Above the bicipital groove, the proximal fragment is abducted by supraspinatus and the distal fragment adducted by the muscles of the bicipital groove. Above the deltoid tuberosity, the proximal fragment is adducted and the distal fragment is abducted and pulled up by the deltoid. Below the deltoid tuberosity, the proximal fragment is abducted by the deltoid, and the distal fragment is adducted and pulled up by the biceps and triceps.

**Why:** Reproduces the lecture table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which artery accompanies the radial nerve in the spiral groove?</summary>

**Answer:** The profunda brachii artery, so both can be injured in a midshaft fracture.

**Why:** Direct one-liner.
</details>

## 3. Medial epicondylar fracture

**Case:** a 12-year-old boy falls on an outstretched hand — medial elbow pain, swelling, tenderness; weakness of finger abduction/adduction; tingling over the ring and little fingers.

- Common in **children and adolescents** (**10–20%** of pediatric elbow fractures).
- **Mechanism:** fall on an outstretched hand — **avulsion by the flexor-pronator muscles**; or direct blow. **Elbow dislocation** accompanies up to **50%**.
- **Anatomy:** the medial epicondyle gives attachment to the **flexor-pronator group** (FCU, PT, FDS…) and the **ulnar collateral ligament**; the ⭐ **ulnar nerve (C8–T1)** passes **behind it in the cubital tunnel** and is easily stretched.
- **Ulnar nerve injury:** motor — intrinsic weakness, **partial claw hand (ring and little fingers)**, loss of finger abduction/adduction, weak grip; sensory — **medial 1½ fingers and hypothenar eminence**.
- **Complications:** ulnar nerve palsy, **elbow instability** (ulnar collateral ligament), nonunion.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Avulsion by flexor-pronators; ulnar nerve behind the epicondyle.
- Partial claw (4th and 5th fingers); sensory loss medial 1½ fingers.
- Ulnar collateral ligament → elbow instability.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the ulnar nerve vulnerable in medial epicondylar fractures?</summary>

**Answer:** It passes in the groove directly behind the medial epicondyle (cubital tunnel), so the fracture and its swelling stretch or entrap it.

**Why:** Anatomy explains the symptoms.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the features and complications of ulnar nerve injury at the elbow.</summary>

**Answer:** Weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles, partial clawing of the 4th and 5th fingers, loss of finger abduction/adduction, weak grip, and sensory loss over the medial 1½ fingers and hypothenar eminence. Other complications of the fracture: elbow instability and nonunion.

**Why:** Motor + sensory + fracture complications.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is attached to the medial epicondyle and what is the mechanism of the fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** The flexor-pronator group (FCU, pronator teres, FDS…) and the ulnar collateral ligament. The fracture is an avulsion by the flexor-pronator muscles during a fall on an outstretched hand.

**Why:** Connects anatomy with mechanism.
</details>

## 4. Pediatric supracondylar fracture

**Case:** a 6-year-old falls from monkey bars onto an outstretched hand — swollen, painful elbow held in extension; **no radial pulse but warm, pink fingers**.

- Age **5–8 years**; **extension type (>95%)**: distal fragment displaced **posteriorly**; **flexion type (<5%)**: displaced **anteriorly**.
- **Nerves:** ⭐ **anterior interosseous nerve (AIN)** — most common in the extension type; **radial nerve** — second; **ulnar nerve** — typically the flexion type.
- **Why the AIN?** Its fascicles lie in the **dorsal part of the median nerve**, directly behind the fracture site.
- **Median nerve injury** at the elbow → **ape hand and benediction hand**.
- **AIN palsy:** pure motor → **no sensory loss**; the **"OK" sign** fails (pulp-to-pulp pinch) because of loss of **FPL and index FDP**.
- ⭐ **Brachial artery** injury → **Volkmann's ischemic contracture**: the proximal fragment is displaced forward and downward, compressing the brachial artery and median nerve; when collateral circulation fails, the **flexor muscles become ischemic, necrose and are replaced by fibrous tissue**, which shortens → **flexion deformity of wrist and fingers**.
- **Key exam:** neurovascular assessment (pulse, capillary refill, Doppler).
- **Bony triangle** (olecranon + two epicondyles) is **preserved** in a supracondylar fracture but **disturbed** in elbow dislocation.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Extension type >95%; AIN is the most commonly injured nerve.
- OK sign for AIN; no sensory loss.
- Brachial artery → Volkmann's contracture.
- Triangle intact in fracture, lost in dislocation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the AIN the nerve most commonly injured in extension-type supracondylar fractures?</summary>

**Answer:** The AIN fascicles lie in the dorsal part of the median nerve, directly behind the fracture site, so they are compressed or stretched by the displaced posterior fragment.

**Why:** Answer from the lecture MCQ.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is Volkmann's ischemic contracture and how does it arise?</summary>

**Answer:** In a supracondylar fracture the proximal fragment is displaced forward and downward, compressing the brachial artery and median nerve. If the collateral circulation cannot compensate, the forearm flexor muscles become ischemic and necrotic and are replaced by fibrous tissue that thins and shortens, giving a flexion deformity of the wrist and fingers.

**Why:** Mechanism in four steps.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the olecranon–epicondyle triangle preserved in a supracondylar fracture but disturbed in elbow dislocation?</summary>

**Answer:** A supracondylar fracture lies above the three bony points, which therefore keep their triangular relationship. In dislocation the olecranon comes to lie more or less in line with the epicondyles.

**Why:** Clinical sign used to separate the two.
</details>

## 5. Radiological anatomy of the elbow

### Bony landmarks
Medial and lateral **epicondyles**, **trochlea** and **capitulum**, **olecranon**, **coronoid process** (anterior stability), **radial head** (forearm rotation), **radial tuberosity** (biceps tendon), trochlear notch, radial notch.

### Ossification centers
- **Primary** centers form the **shafts (diaphyses)**; **secondary** centers form the **ends (epiphyses)** and appear **after birth**.
- Importance: **age estimation** (predictable sequence), **injury diagnosis** (secondary centers can **mimic fractures**), **growth monitoring**.
- ⭐ **CRITOE** — the six elbow secondary centers in order:

| Letter | Center | Age |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | Capitellum | **1 year** |
| **R** | Radial head | **3 years** |
| **I** | Internal (medial) epicondyle | **5 years** |
| **T** | Trochlea | **7 years** |
| **O** | Olecranon | **9 years** |
| **E** | External (lateral) epicondyle | **11 years** |

### Radiological lines
- **Anterior humeral line:** along the anterior cortex of the distal humerus → should pass through the **middle third of the capitellum**. Displacement → **supracondylar fracture**.
- **Radiocapitellar line:** through the center of the radial head and neck → should pass through the **center of the capitellum**. Displacement → **radial head dislocation**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- CRITOE: 1-3-5-7-9-11 years.
- Anterior humeral line → middle third of capitellum; radiocapitellar line → center of capitellum.
- Secondary centers can mimic avulsion fractures.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give the elbow ossification centers in order with their ages.</summary>

**Answer:** CRITOE: capitellum (1 year), radial head (3), internal/medial epicondyle (5), trochlea (7), olecranon (9), external/lateral epicondyle (11).

**Why:** A classic memory question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. A 3-year-old has only the capitellum visible. Which center appears next?</summary>

**Answer:** The radial head (at about 3 years), following the CRITOE sequence.

**Why:** Prevents mistaking normal centers for fractures.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the two radiological lines of the elbow and their significance.</summary>

**Answer:** The anterior humeral line along the anterior cortex of the distal humerus should intersect the middle third of the capitellum; displacement suggests a supracondylar fracture. The radiocapitellar line through the center of the radial head and neck should pass through the capitellum center; failure indicates radial head dislocation.

**Why:** Lines are tested with X-ray images.
</details>

## 6. Nursemaid's elbow (radial head subluxation)

**Case:** a 2-year-old girl refuses to use her left arm after her mother pulled her up by the wrist — arm in slight flexion and pronation, **no swelling or deformity**.

- **Definition:** subluxation of the **radial head from under the annular ligament**.
- **Age:** **1–4 years** (lax ligaments, underdeveloped radial head); recurrence is less likely after **~5 years**.
- **Mechanism:** sudden **longitudinal traction on a pronated, extended forearm** — the **annular ligament slips over the radial head** and is trapped in the radiocapitellar joint.
- **Typical position:** arm **flexed and pronated**, refuses supination.
- **Management:** **closed reduction** — supination + flexion (a click is felt); usually **no immobilization**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Annular ligament trapped; age 1–4 years.
- Pulled by the hand/wrist; arm held flexed and pronated.
- Reduce by supination + flexion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the mechanism of nursemaid's elbow?</summary>

**Answer:** Sudden longitudinal traction on a pronated, extended forearm (for example, pulling a child by the hand) makes the annular ligament slip over the radial head and become trapped between the radial head and the capitulum.

**Why:** Mechanism + anatomy.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is it common in young children?</summary>

**Answer:** Their ligaments are lax and the radial head is small and not fully developed, so it slips easily; it enlarges with age, making recurrence unlikely after about 5 years.

**Why:** Anatomy of the age group.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is it recognized and treated?</summary>

**Answer:** The child holds the arm flexed and pronated, refuses supination and has no swelling or bruising. Treatment is closed reduction by supination plus flexion (a click is felt), usually without immobilization.

**Why:** Presentation + management.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Nerve ↔ site ↔ deformity:** **axillary** — surgical neck — flat shoulder; **radial** — spiral groove — wrist drop; **ulnar** — behind the medial epicondyle — claw hand.
- **AIN palsy** has **no sensory loss**; test = **OK sign**.
- **Extension type:** AIN most common; **flexion type:** ulnar nerve.
- In a supracondylar fracture the **bony triangle is preserved**; in dislocation it is **lost**.
- **CRITOE** = 1-3-5-7-9-11 years — the **radial head (3 years)** is second, not the olecranon.
- Nursemaid's elbow is reduced by **supination + flexion**, not by extension.
