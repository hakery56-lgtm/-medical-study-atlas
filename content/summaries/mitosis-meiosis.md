## 1. Embryology & mitosis

### Embryology
- **From a single cell to a baby in 9 months** — a developmental process that is an amazing integration of increasingly complex phenomena. Its study = ⭐ **Embryology**.
- Stages (figure): fertilized egg → 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 days → **blastocyst** → 1 month … 9 months.

### Mitosis
- The process whereby **one cell divides into 2 daughter cells that are genetically identical to the parent cell**; **each daughter cell contains the total 46 chromosomes**.
- **Somatic cells:** 23 homologous pairs = **diploid number 46** — **22 pairs of autosomes + 1 pair of sex chromosomes**: **XX = genetically female**; **XY = genetically male**.
- **Stages:** **prophase → prometaphase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase** → two daughter cells.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Embryology = study of development from one cell to a baby in 9 months.
- Mitosis: 1 cell → 2 identical diploid daughter cells (46 chromosomes).
- Somatic cells = 22 autosome pairs + 1 sex-chromosome pair (XX female, XY male).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is embryology?</summary>

**Answer:** The study of the developmental process from a single cell to a baby over nine months, an integration of increasingly complex phenomena.

**Why:** Opening definition.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define mitosis and give its stages.</summary>

**Answer:** The process by which one cell divides into two daughter cells that are genetically identical to the parent cell, each containing the full 46 chromosomes. Its stages are prophase, prometaphase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase.

**Why:** Definition + five stages.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the chromosomes of a human somatic cell.</summary>

**Answer:** They appear as 23 homologous pairs forming the diploid number of 46: 22 pairs of matching autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes — XX in a genetic female and XY in a genetic male.

**Why:** 22 + 1.
</details>

## 2. Meiosis, crossover & its results

### Meiosis
- The cell division in the **germ cells** that generates the **male and female gametes (sperm and egg)**.
- **Two divisions — meiosis I and meiosis II** — reduce the chromosome number to the **haploid number 23**.
- **Meiosis I:** primary spermatocytes and primary oocytes **replicate DNA** (each of the 46 chromosomes becomes 2 sister chromatids); then **homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis)** — **exact, point for point, except for the XY combination**; the **homologous pairs separate into two daughter cells** (diploid → haploid).
- **Meiosis II:** separates the **sister chromatids**; each gamete has **23 chromosomes**.

### Crossover
1. A **critical event in meiosis I**.
2. **Interchange of chromatid segments between paired homologous chromosomes.**
3. Occurs **during separation**.
- **Chiasma** = point of interchange, temporarily united forming an **X-like structure**.
- **30–40 crossovers (1–2 per chromosome)** per meiosis I division; **most frequent between genes far apart** on a chromosome.

### Results of meiosis
1. ⭐ **Genetic variability** is increased by **(a) crossover** (redistributes genetic material) and **(b) random distribution of homologous chromosomes** to the daughter cells.
2. Each germ cell has the **haploid number**, so at **fertilization the diploid number 46 is restored**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Meiosis = germ cells; two divisions; 46 → 23; meiosis I separates homologous pairs, meiosis II separates sister chromatids.
- Synapsis = pairing of homologues (not in mitosis).
- Crossover (chiasma) = exchange of chromatid segments in meiosis I → recombination; 30–40 per division.
- Meiosis gives genetic variability (crossover + random distribution) and restores 46 at fertilization.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe meiosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Meiosis occurs in germ cells to generate sperm and eggs. In meiosis I, primary spermatocytes and oocytes replicate their DNA so each of the 46 chromosomes has two sister chromatids; homologous chromosomes pair exactly (synapsis) and then separate into two daughter cells, reducing the number from diploid to haploid. Meiosis II then separates the sister chromatids, giving gametes with 23 chromosomes.

**Why:** Replication → synapsis → separation of homologues → separation of chromatids.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is crossover and why is it important?</summary>

**Answer:** Crossover is the interchange of chromatid segments between paired homologous chromosomes during meiosis I, occurring at chiasmata (X-like structures); about 30–40 crossovers (1–2 per chromosome) occur per division, most frequently between genes far apart. It redistributes genetic material and so enhances genetic variability.

**Why:** Definition + number + significance.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the results of meiotic divisions.</summary>

**Answer:** Genetic variability is enhanced by crossover and by random distribution of homologous chromosomes to daughter cells, and each germ cell contains the haploid number of chromosomes so that the diploid number of 46 is restored at fertilization.

**Why:** Two results.
</details>

## 3. Chromosomal abnormalities — overview & numerical abnormalities

### Overview (figures from the lecture)
- **50% of conceptions** end in **spontaneous abortion**; **50% of these** are due to **chromosomal abnormalities** → **25% of conceptuses have a major defect**.
- **Chromosomal abnormalities = 10%** of major birth defects; **gene mutations = an additional 8%**.

### 1. Numerical abnormalities
- Normal somatic cell = **46 (diploid, 2n)**; normal gamete = **23 (haploid, n)**.
- **Euploid** = any **exact multiple of n** (diploid, triploid, tetraploid…).
- ⭐ **Aneuploid** = any chromosome number **that is not euploid**.
- **Trisomy** = an **extra** chromosome; **monosomy** = **one chromosome missing**.
- May arise in **meiotic or mitotic** divisions.

### Nondisjunction
- **Failure of separation of paired chromosomes** during meiosis; in the **1st or 2nd** meiotic division.
- Result: **one cell receives 22 chromosomes and the other 24**.
- **Commonly in meiosis; occasionally in mitosis.**
- **Mitotic nondisjunction = mosaicism** (some cells normal, others abnormal), occurring in the embryonic cell during the earliest cell divisions.
- In women the incidence **increases with age, especially ≥ 35 years**.
- Affected individuals show few or many features of a **syndrome**, depending on the number and distribution of cells involved.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 50% of conceptions are lost; half of these have chromosomal abnormalities; chromosomal abnormalities cause 10% of major birth defects, gene mutations 8%.
- Euploid = exact multiple of n; aneuploid = not euploid (trisomy, monosomy).
- Nondisjunction = failure of chromosomes to separate (meiosis I or II) → 22 + 24; mitotic nondisjunction → mosaicism; maternal age ≥ 35 increases risk.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define euploidy, aneuploidy, trisomy and monosomy.</summary>

**Answer:** Euploid refers to any exact multiple of the haploid number (diploid, triploid…); aneuploid is any chromosome number that is not euploid; trisomy is the presence of an extra chromosome; monosomy is the absence of one chromosome.

**Why:** Four definitions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is nondisjunction and what are its consequences?</summary>

**Answer:** Failure of separation of paired chromosomes during meiotic division (first or second), so one cell receives 22 and the other 24 chromosomes. It is common in meiosis (increasing with maternal age ≥ 35) and occasional in mitosis, where it produces mosaicism. The affected individual shows the characteristic features of a syndrome, depending on how many cells are involved.

**Why:** Mechanism + age + mosaicism.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is mosaicism?</summary>

**Answer:** The presence of cells with normal and abnormal chromosome numbers in the same individual, resulting from mitotic nondisjunction in the earliest embryonic cell divisions.

**Why:** Mitotic vs meiotic nondisjunction.
</details>

## 4. Translocations & the classic syndromes

### Translocations
Chromosomes break and a piece of one attaches to another:
- **Balanced:** breakage and reunion between 2 chromosomes with **no critical genetic material lost** → **normal** individuals.
- **Unbalanced:** **part of a chromosome is lost** → **altered phenotype**.
- Example: **unbalanced translocation between the long arms of chromosomes 14 and 21** (meiosis I or II) gives gametes with an **extra copy of chromosome 21** → one cause of **Down syndrome**.

### ⭐ Trisomy 21 — Down syndrome
- **Intellectual disability** (varying degrees), **cardiac defects, hypotonia**.
- **Craniofacial:** **upward-slanting eyes, flat facies, small ears**.
- ↑ **leukemia, infections, thyroid dysfunction**, **premature aging**; **nearly all develop Alzheimer's disease signs after 35**.
- **Incidence:** **1 in 2000** (mothers < 25 y), **1 in 300** (age 35), **1 in 100** (age 40).

### ⭐ Klinefelter syndrome (47, XXY)
- Usually **nondisjunction of the XX homologues**; cells have **47 chromosomes**, sex complement **XXY**.
- **Sterility, testicular atrophy, hyalinization of the seminiferous tubules, usually gynecomastia**; **only males**; usually detected at **puberty**.
- **Sex chromatin body** in **80%** of cases; incidence ≈ **1 in 500 males**.

### ⭐ Turner syndrome (45, X)
- **55%** are **monosomic for X** and **chromatin-negative** (meiotic nondisjunction); the rest: **structural X abnormalities** or **mitotic nondisjunction (mosaicism)**.
- ⭐ **The only monosomy compatible with life** — yet **98% of such fetuses are spontaneously aborted**; survivors are **unmistakably female** in appearance.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Translocation: balanced (normal) vs unbalanced (abnormal; 14/21 → Down syndrome).
- Down syndrome (trisomy 21): intellectual disability, cardiac defects, hypotonia, upward-slanting eyes, flat face, small ears; risk rises with maternal age (1/2000 → 1/300 at 35 → 1/100 at 40).
- Klinefelter 47,XXY: male sterility, testicular atrophy, gynecomastia; ~1/500 males.
- Turner 45,X: only viable monosomy; 98% aborted; female phenotype.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate between balanced and unbalanced translocations.</summary>

**Answer:** In a balanced translocation breakage and reunion occur between two chromosomes without loss of critical genetic material, so the individual is normal. In an unbalanced translocation part of a chromosome is lost, producing an altered phenotype; e.g. an unbalanced 14/21 translocation gives gametes with an extra copy of chromosome 21, a cause of Down syndrome.

**Why:** Normal vs abnormal + example.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe Down syndrome.</summary>

**Answer:** Trisomy 21 with varying degrees of intellectual disability, cardiac defects and hypotonia; craniofacial features include upward-slanting eyes, flat facies and small ears. There is a high incidence of leukemia, infections and thyroid dysfunction, premature aging, and nearly all develop Alzheimer's signs after 35. The incidence is 1 in 2000 conceptuses for women under 25, 1 in 300 at 35 and 1 in 100 at 40.

**Why:** Clinical + age incidence.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare Klinefelter and Turner syndromes.</summary>

**Answer:** Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) arises usually from nondisjunction of XX homologues; affected males have sterility, testicular atrophy, hyalinization of the seminiferous tubules and gynecomastia, with a sex chromatin body in 80% and an incidence of about 1 in 500 males. Turner syndrome (45,X): 55% are X-monosomic and chromatin-negative from meiotic nondisjunction (others have structural X abnormalities or mosaicism); it is the only monosomy compatible with life although 98% of fetuses are aborted, and survivors are female in appearance.

**Why:** Sex chromosome syndromes side by side.
</details>

## 5. Structural abnormalities & fragile sites

### Structural chromosome abnormalities
- Result from **chromosome breakage** caused by **environmental factors: viruses, radiation, drugs**; an infant with a **partial deletion** is abnormal when the broken piece is lost.
- **Microdeletions** spanning a few **contiguous genes** → **microdeletion (contiguous gene) syndromes**; sites = **contiguous gene complexes**, identified by **high-resolution chromosome banding**.

| Syndrome | Abnormality |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Cri-du-chat** | **partial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5** |
| ⭐ **Angelman** | **microdeletion on the long arm of chromosome 15 (q11–q13) inherited on the MATERNAL chromosome** |
| ⭐ **Prader–Willi** | the **same microdeletion** (chromosome 15) inherited on the **PATERNAL chromosome**; hypotonia |

→ Same deletion, different syndrome depending on the parent of origin = **genomic imprinting**.

### Fragile site
- Regions of a chromosome with a **propensity to separate or break** under certain cell manipulations; e.g. ⭐ **fragile X chromosome**.
- **Males affected more than females**; **second only to Down syndrome as a cause of chromosomal intellectual disability**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Structural abnormalities come from chromosome breakage (viruses, radiation, drugs); microdeletions → contiguous gene syndromes.
- Cri-du-chat = deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5.
- Angelman = maternal 15q11–q13 deletion; Prader–Willi = paternal 15q11–q13 deletion.
- Fragile X: common cause of chromosomal intellectual disability (after Down syndrome); males more affected.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What causes structural chromosome abnormalities?</summary>

**Answer:** Chromosome breakage caused by environmental factors such as viruses, radiation and drugs; when the broken piece is lost (partial deletion), the infant is abnormal. Microdeletions spanning a few contiguous genes produce microdeletion (contiguous gene) syndromes.

**Why:** Causes + microdeletions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare Angelman and Prader–Willi syndromes.</summary>

**Answer:** Both result from a microdeletion on the long arm of chromosome 15 (q11–q13). If the deletion is inherited on the maternal chromosome it produces Angelman syndrome; if on the paternal chromosome, Prader–Willi syndrome (with hypotonia).

**Why:** Parent of origin decides.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a fragile site? Give an example.</summary>

**Answer:** A region of a chromosome with a propensity to separate or break under certain cell manipulations; an example is the fragile X chromosome, which affects males more than females and is second only to Down syndrome as a cause of chromosomal mental retardation.

**Why:** Definition + example.
</details>

## 6. Gene mutation

- A **change in the structure or function of a single gene** = **single gene mutation**.
- Accounts for **8% of all human malformations**.
- **Dominant mutation:** a **single allele** is affected. **Recessive mutation:** **both alleles** are affected. Or the mutation may be **X-linked**.
- All chromosomes carry **two alleles (a "dose" of genetic determinants) except the X and Y** in males.
- Example (figure): **achondroplasia** — a gene mutation associated with **advanced paternal age**, affecting **bone formation**: **large head with prominent forehead, normal-sized torso with short arms and legs**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Gene mutation = change in a single gene; 8% of malformations.
- Dominant = one allele affected; recessive = both alleles affected; X-linked possible.
- Achondroplasia: gene mutation, advanced paternal age, short limbs, large head.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define gene mutation and distinguish dominant from recessive mutations.</summary>

**Answer:** A gene mutation is a change in the structure or function of a single gene, accounting for 8% of human malformations. In a dominant mutation a single allele is affected; in a recessive mutation both alleles are affected; mutations may also be X-linked.

**Why:** Allele count is the difference.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why do the X and Y chromosomes differ from the autosomes regarding gene mutations?</summary>

**Answer:** All chromosomes carry a double dose of alleles except the X and Y, which in a male are present once and therefore a mutation on them has no second allele to compensate (X-linked inheritance).

**Why:** Dosage of alleles.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe achondroplasia as an example of gene mutation.</summary>

**Answer:** It is caused by a gene mutation associated with advanced paternal age that affects bone formation, giving a large head with a prominent forehead and a normal-sized torso with short arms and legs.

**Why:** The lecture's figure.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Mitosis:** 2 identical diploid cells; **meiosis:** 4 haploid gametes (two divisions).
- **Synapsis** (homologue pairing) occurs in **meiosis I only**.
- **Crossover** = interchange between **homologous (non-sister) chromatids**.
- **Euploid** ≠ aneuploid; **trisomy** = 47, **monosomy** = 45.
- **Nondisjunction** → 22 + 24; mitotic nondisjunction = **mosaicism**.
- **Down incidence:** 1/2000 (<25), 1/300 (35), 1/100 (40).
- **Klinefelter 47,XXY** = male, sterile, gynecomastia; **Turner 45,X** = female; the **only viable monosomy**.
- **Angelman = maternal; Prader–Willi = paternal** (chromosome 15).
- **Cri-du-chat** = **5p deletion**.
- **Fragile X** = second to Down syndrome as a cause of chromosomal mental retardation.
