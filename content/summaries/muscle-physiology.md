## 1. Muscle types and organization

- **Muscles are excitable** (like nerves) and also **contractible, extensible and elastic.**

| | **Skeletal** | **Cardiac** | **Visceral (smooth)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Skeleton | Heart | **GI tract, uterus, eye, blood vessels** |
| **Function** | **Movement, heat, posture** | **Continuous pumping** | **Peristalsis, blood pressure, pupil size, hair erection** |
| **Appearance** | **Striated, multinucleated (eccentric)** | **Striated, one central nucleus** | **No striations, one central nucleus** |
| **Control** | **Voluntary** | Involuntary | Involuntary |

- **Half of body weight is muscle**: skeletal **40% (male), 32% (female).** Skeletal muscle is the **only organ of the muscular system** (muscle + nerve + vessels + connective tissue).
- ⭐ **Four connective tissue coverings:** **deep fascia** (whole muscle), **epimysium** (binds fascicles), **perimysium** (each fascicle), **endomysium** (each fiber). **Fascia → tendon** (beyond the muscle) or **aponeurosis** (muscle to muscle).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Skeletal: striated, multinucleated, voluntary; cardiac: striated, one nucleus; smooth: not striated.
- Endomysium = fiber; perimysium = fascicle; epimysium = muscle; deep fascia = outside.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare skeletal, cardiac and visceral muscle.</summary>

**Answer:** Skeletal: bone-attached, striated, multinucleated, voluntary (movement, heat, posture); cardiac: heart, striated with one central nucleus, involuntary pumping; visceral: GI tract, uterus, eye, vessels, non-striated with one nucleus, involuntary.

**Why:** Slide "Three types of muscular tissue".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name the four connective tissue coverings of skeletal muscle.</summary>

**Answer:** Deep fascia, epimysium, perimysium (around each fascicle) and endomysium (around each fiber).

**Why:** Slide "Four different connective tissue coverings".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are a tendon and an aponeurosis?</summary>

**Answer:** Fascia extending beyond the muscle end forms a tendon; fascia connecting muscle to muscle forms an aponeurosis.

**Why:** Slide "Connective tissue coverings".
</details>

## 2. Ultrastructure: sarcomere, filaments and organelles

- **Fiber = muscle cell → myofibrils → thick and thin filaments.** **Sarcolemma** = membrane; **sarcoplasm** = cytoplasm.
- ⭐ **Sarcomere** (**Z line to Z line**) = **functional unit.** **A band** = dark, **overlapping thick and thin**; **I band** = **thin only**, **Z line in its middle**; **H zone** = **thick only**; **M line** links thick filaments; **titin** (elastic) holds **myosin to the Z line.**
- **Thick filament** = **myosin** (tail + **2 globular heads**). **Thin filament** = **actin** double helix with **myosin binding sites**, **tropomyosin** spiraling around it and **troponin (3 subunits)** holding tropomyosin (**troponin-tropomyosin complex**).
- **Sarcoplasmic reticulum:** **ER surrounding each myofibril, stores Ca²⁺.** **T tubules:** **sarcolemma invaginations with extracellular fluid** between SR cisternae. **Triad** = **T tubule + 2 terminal cisternae**, near the filament overlap.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- A band = thick (dark); I band = thin only; H zone = thick only; Z to Z = sarcomere.
- Troponin holds tropomyosin; SR stores Ca²⁺; triad = T tubule + 2 cisternae.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of the sarcomere.</summary>

**Answer:** From Z line to Z line: the A band (dark, overlapping thick and thin filaments), I bands at the ends (thin only) with the Z line in the middle, an H zone (thick only) and M line; titin holds myosin to the Z line.

**Why:** Slide "Sarcomere structure".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the thick and thin filaments.</summary>

**Answer:** Thick: myosin molecules with a tail and two globular heads (cross bridges); thin: actin helix with myosin binding sites, tropomyosin around it and troponin (3 subunits) holding tropomyosin in place.

**Why:** Slides "Thick filament structure" and "Thin filament structure".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the SR, T tubules and triad?</summary>

**Answer:** The SR is an ER around each myofibril storing calcium; T tubules are sarcolemmal invaginations filled with extracellular fluid; a triad is a T tubule with two terminal SR cisternae.

**Why:** Slide "Specialized organelles of skeletal muscle".
</details>

## 3. Neuromuscular junction and motor unit

- **Motor neuron** = **alpha** nerve cell. **Neuromuscular junction** = branch of the motor neuron meeting the **sarcolemma** (a **synapse**); **motor end plate** = sarcolemma under the nerve ending with **many mitochondria and nuclei**; **synaptic cleft** = gap; axon ending has **vesicles of neurotransmitter.**
- ⭐ **Neurotransmitter = acetylcholine (ACh)**: crosses the cleft, binds receptors on the end plate, **stimulates contraction.**
- ⭐ **Motor unit** = **one alpha motor neuron + all fibers it innervates**; **all fibers contract together.** **Innervation ratio** varies: **gastrocnemius ~2,000 fibers per neuron; extraocular < 10** → **affects precision.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- NMJ: ACh across the synaptic cleft to the motor end plate.
- Motor unit = 1 alpha neuron + its fibers; ratio low = precise (eye), high = powerful (leg).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the neuromuscular junction.</summary>

**Answer:** The site where a branch of the alpha motor neuron meets the sarcolemma across the synaptic cleft; the end plate has many mitochondria and nuclei; acetylcholine from vesicles binds receptors and stimulates contraction.

**Why:** Slides "Motor neuron" and "Neurotransmitter".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define a motor unit and innervation ratio.</summary>

**Answer:** A motor unit is one alpha motor neuron and all the fibers it innervates; the innervation ratio (fibers per neuron) varies, e.g. gastrocnemius about 2,000 versus fewer than 10 in the eye muscles.

**Why:** Slides "The motor unit".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does the innervation ratio determine?</summary>

**Answer:** The precision of movement: few fibers per neuron means fine control.

**Why:** Slide "Motor unit".
</details>

## 4. Excitation-contraction coupling, cross bridge cycle and relaxation

- ⭐ **Sliding filament theory:** **sarcomeres shorten** as **thick and thin filaments slide**; thin filaments move **toward the center** from both ends. In contraction: **A band same; I band and H zone smaller; sarcomere shorter.**
- **Sequence:** motor impulse → axon depolarizes → **Ca²⁺ enters axon ending** → **ACh released** → binds **end-plate receptors** → fiber **depolarizes** → impulse down **T tubules** → **voltage-sensitive receptors → Ca²⁺ released from SR** → **Ca²⁺ binds troponin → tropomyosin moves → myosin binds actin** → **power stroke.**
- ⭐ **Cross bridge cycle (4 steps):** **1 attachment; 2 power stroke (Pi released, head pivots toward the M line, then ADP released); 3 new ATP binds → detachment; 4 ATP hydrolysis re-energizes (cocks) the head.**
- **Relaxation:** **acetylcholinesterase destroys ACh; Ca²⁺ pumped back into the SR (active transport); myosin-actin links broken (needs ATP)** → tropomyosin block restored.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Ca²⁺ → troponin → tropomyosin moves → cross bridges.
- Power stroke after Pi release; ATP needed to detach.
- Relaxation: AChE, SR Ca²⁺ pump, ATP.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Outline excitation-contraction coupling.</summary>

**Answer:** Axon depolarization admits Ca²⁺ and releases ACh; ACh depolarizes the end plate; the action potential spreads down T tubules and triggers Ca²⁺ release from the SR; Ca²⁺ binds troponin, tropomyosin uncovers actin and myosin heads cycle.

**Why:** Slides "Physiology of skeletal muscle contraction" and "Excitation-contraction coupling".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the cross bridge cycle.</summary>

**Answer:** Myosin attaches to actin; Pi release triggers the power stroke sliding actin toward the M line, then ADP is released; new ATP binds and detaches the head; ATP hydrolysis re-energizes it.

**Why:** Slide "The cross bridge cycle".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does relaxation occur?</summary>

**Answer:** Acetylcholinesterase destroys ACh, calcium is pumped back into the SR and ATP binding breaks the myosin-actin links; tropomyosin again blocks the binding sites.

**Why:** Slide "Muscle relaxation mechanism".
</details>

## 5. Energy for contraction and exercise

- ⭐ **ATP** is the energy source; **only a small store.** **Three pathways:**

| Pathway | Source | O₂ | ATP yield | Duration | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Creatine phosphate** | **CP + ADP** | **None** | **1 ATP per CP** | **~10-15 s** (< 30 s) | **100 m sprint** |
| **Anaerobic glycolysis** | **Glucose (glycogen) → lactic acid** | **None** | **2 ATP per glucose** | **30-60 s** | **400-800 m** |
| **Aerobic** | **Glucose, pyruvate, fatty acids, amino acids** | **Required** | **~38 ATP per glucose** (+ CO₂, H₂O) | **Hours** | **> 1,500 m, marathon** |

- **CP** is made when ATP is abundant; **anaerobic glycolysis is limited by lactic acid accumulation**, not glycogen; **glycogen depletion matters after > 90 min.**
- **Myoglobin** stores O₂. **Prolonged moderate exercise:** respiration, then blood **glucose and fatty acids.** **Intense exercise:** O₂ delivery fails → **glycolysis → lactic acid → fatigue.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- CP 10-15 s; glycolysis 30-60 s (2 ATP); aerobic hours (38 ATP).
- Lactic acid accumulation limits glycolysis and causes fatigue.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the three pathways supplying ATP to muscle.</summary>

**Answer:** Creatine phosphate (no O₂, 10-15 s), anaerobic glycolysis (no O₂, 2 ATP per glucose, 30-60 s, lactic acid) and aerobic metabolism (O₂, ~38 ATP per glucose, hours).

**Why:** Slide "Methods of regenerating ATP during muscle activity".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What limits anaerobic glycolysis?</summary>

**Answer:** Accumulation of lactic acid and other metabolites, not glycogen availability.

**Why:** Slide "Anaerobic glycolysis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does fatigue occur in intense exercise?</summary>

**Answer:** Oxygen delivery is exceeded so ATP comes from glycolysis, pyruvate becomes lactic acid and its build-up causes fatigue.

**Why:** Slide "Exercise and skeletal muscles".
</details>

## 6. Types of contraction and fiber types

| | **Isotonic** | **Isometric** |
|---|---|---|
| **Tension** | **Constant** | **Rises markedly** |
| **Length** | **Shortens or lengthens** | **Constant** |
| **Work** | **External work done** | **No work done** |
| **Efficiency** | **25%** | **0** |
| **Duration / energy** | **Long; less energy** | **Short; more energy** |
| **Example** | **Walking** | **Standing (posture)** |

- **Isotonic types:** **concentric** (shortens: biceps flexing the elbow) and **eccentric** (lengthens while contracting: lowering a load).

| Fiber | Features |
|---|---|
| **Slow oxidative (type I)** | **Slow, fatigue-resistant, red**, aerobic, small forces over long periods |
| **Fast oxidative (type IIa)** | **Red, fast**, mainly aerobic (can use glycolysis), **intermediate** motor neuron; **middle-distance running, swimming** |
| **Fast glycolytic (type IIb)** | **White**, anaerobic, **fatigue easily**, few mitochondria and little myoglobin, **greatest force**, large motor neurons; limb, eye and finger muscles |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Isotonic: constant tension, work done; isometric: constant length, no work.
- Concentric = shortening; eccentric = lengthening.
- Type I red/slow; IIa red/fast; IIb white/fast/fatigable.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare isotonic and isometric contraction.</summary>

**Answer:** Isotonic: constant tension with a length change, external work, 25% efficiency, long duration (walking); isometric: tension rises with constant length, no work, zero efficiency, short duration, more energy (standing posture).

**Why:** Slide "Isotonic versus isometric".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define concentric and eccentric contraction.</summary>

**Answer:** Concentric: the muscle shortens (biceps flexing the elbow); eccentric: it lengthens while contracting (lowering a load).

**Why:** Slide "Types of isotonic contractions".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the three skeletal fiber types.</summary>

**Answer:** Type I: slow, fatigue-resistant, red, aerobic; type IIa: fast, red, mainly aerobic with some glycolysis; type IIb: fast, white, anaerobic, easily fatigued, greatest force.

**Why:** Slides "Types of skeletal fibers".
</details>
