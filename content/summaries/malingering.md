## 1. Definition, motives and history

- ⭐ **Malingering** = **deliberate feigning or exaggeration of illness for personal gain.** **Motives:** **financial compensation (fraud), avoiding work or school, obtaining drugs, lighter criminal sentences, attention or sympathy.**
- **Not** somatization or factitious disorder, where the **gain is not readily apparent.** Legal terms: **fabricated** or **feigned mental illness.**
- **History:** documented since **Roman times** (**Galen**: colic to avoid a public meeting; injured knee to avoid a journey); **Soviet era:** widespread to escape sanctions, with limited medical dispensations → mistrust.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Deliberate + external gain.
- Different from somatization/factitious disorders.

</div>

## 2. Methods of feigning and predisposing factors

- **Physical methods:** **deceptive use of thermometers, inducing swelling, delaying wound healing, over-exercise, overdose, self-harm, reporting signs learned from textbooks.**
- **Behavioral methods:** **fabricated history** (seizures, heart attack) ± **agents mimicking disease** (neuroleptics for tremor).
- **Partial malingering** (functional overlay) = on top of a **real organic disease**; harder to detect.
- **Predisposing:** **enforced labor (industrial)**, **universal military service (military)**, **ability to sue (medicolegal)**; **health-care workers**; the lecture says **more in women**; **psychodynamic:** childhood **neglect or abuse.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Physical vs behavioral methods.
- Industrial, military and medicolegal malingering.

</div>

**Answer:** Malingering overlaid on a genuine organic disease (functional overlay), making detection harder.

## 3. Diagnosis and detection

- **DSM-IV-TR criteria:** **medicolegal context**, **marked discrepancy between claimed disability and objective findings**, **poor cooperation**, **antisocial personality disorder**; **limited use** in practice.
- **Unusual features:** **vague, inconsistent details**, **long record with multiple admissions in different cities**, **knowledge of textbook terminology.**
- **Detection clues:** admission **not fitting any disorder**, **medical terminology**, **medical employment**, **pseudologia fantastica**, **presenting when records or senior staff are unavailable (holidays, late Friday)**, **few visitors despite a "prestigious" or heroic history**, **calm acceptance of risky procedures and surgery**, **substance abuse (analgesics, sedatives)**, **symptoms only when observed**, **controlling, hostile, attention-seeking behavior**, **fluctuating course with new complications.**
- **Legal contexts:** **private investigators** (e.g., videotaping a "paralyzed" person walking) and **reports from relatives and colleagues.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Criteria are of limited use.
- Pseudologia fantastica = uncontrollable lying with fantastic false events.

</div>

## 4. Neuropsychological testing

| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| **CARB** (Computerized Assessment of Response Bias) | Detects **inconsistent or exaggerated** symptom reporting |
| **MMPI** (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory) | Personality test with **malingering and exaggeration scales** |
| **TOMM** (Test of Memory Malingering) | Detects malingering on **memory tasks** |

- Used when **brain damage** (head trauma, stroke) is claimed: look for **discrepancies between daily functioning and test performance.** **No single test is diagnostic**; use a **battery** with clinical information.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- CARB = response bias; MMPI = personality scales; TOMM = memory.
- No single test diagnoses malingering.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name three tests used and their purpose.</summary>

**Answer:** CARB (response bias), MMPI (malingering scales) and TOMM (memory malingering).

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What do neuropsychologists look for?</summary>

**Answer:** Discrepancies between reported daily functioning and performance on tests, in claimed brain damage.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Can a single test diagnose malingering?</summary>

**Answer:** No; a battery of tests is interpreted with other clinical information.

**Why:** Slide 8.

## 5. Treatment, ethics and societal impact

- **Treatment:** **psychological**, by cause: **psychotherapy, family therapy, CBT, pharmacotherapy.** **Inform the medical team** (nurses, ward assistants, physiotherapists).
- **Confrontation** may lead to **immediate discharge and presentation elsewhere.** Patients keep **the same safeguards**: it is **unethical and illegal** to **blacklist, search belongings or videotape without consent.**
- **Societal impact:** **absenteeism and lost productivity**; **depletion of social security, disability, compensation and insurance benefits**; **drain on medical resources** (depriving sicker patients); **criminal justice:** **harsher sentences**, **obstruction of justice** if malingering during competency evaluation.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Treatment is psychological.
- No blacklisting, searching or covert videotaping.

</div>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Malingering = conscious + external gain; factitious/somatization = no obvious gain.**
- **Partial malingering = overlay on real disease.**
- **DSM-IV-TR criteria include antisocial personality disorder but are of limited use.**
- **CARB = bias, MMPI = personality, TOMM = memory.**
- **Slide note:** the slide says malingering is more common in women; epidemiology sources usually report it as more common in men. No exam question is built on this statement.
