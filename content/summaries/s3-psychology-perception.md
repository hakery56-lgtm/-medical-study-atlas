## 1. Sensation versus perception

- ⭐ **Sensation** = receptors **detect physical or chemical stimuli** and **convert them to neural signals** (retina: light; cochlear hair cells: sound; olfactory receptors: chemicals; skin: pressure, temperature, harmful stimuli).
- **Perception** = **organizing and interpreting** sensory information to understand ourselves and the environment (recognize a face, a phone ring, burning food, a shadow as a person); it also **separates a real external event from an internally generated experience.**
- **Perception results from** sensory input + **attention, experience, expectations, context and brain processing.**

| Feature | Sensation | Perception |
|---|---|---|
| **Function** | **Detects** stimuli | **Interprets** information |
| **Process** | **Receptor activation and transduction** | **Organization and interpretation** |
| **Example** | Detecting light | Recognizing a face |
| **Clinical relevance** | **Sensory loss, receptor dysfunction** | **Agnosia, illusions, hallucinations** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sensation supplies information; perception gives it meaning.
- Agnosia, illusions and hallucinations = perception disorders.

</div>

## 2. Bottom-up and top-down processing

- **Bottom-up:** driven by **stimulus features**; features processed first, then integrated (red + circular + shape → stop sign).
- **Top-down:** **prior knowledge, expectations, experience, beliefs and context** shape interpretation; e.g., a doctor suspecting **pneumonia** interprets cough and fever accordingly — the **hypothesis guides attention but does not prove** the diagnosis.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Bottom-up = stimulus-driven; top-down = knowledge-driven.
- Hypotheses bias interpretation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define bottom-up processing with an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Processing driven by stimulus features: noticing a red circular object, then identifying it as a stop sign.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define top-down processing with an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Interpretation influenced by prior knowledge and expectations: a doctor suspecting pneumonia reads cough and fever through that diagnosis.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the risk of top-down processing in clinics?</summary>

**Answer:** The hypothesis influences attention and interpretation but does not prove the diagnosis, so errors can occur.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

## 3. Gestalt principles

- **Gestalt** = form, pattern or **organized whole** (Germany, early 20th century): perception **organizes** elements into patterns. Clinicians must not assume that a **pattern proves a diagnosis.**

| Principle | Meaning | Example / caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Figure-ground** | Main object vs background | Black text on white page; **separate important findings from background** |
| **Proximity** | Close elements grouped | Related symptoms look like a cluster; **no proof of causation** |
| **Similarity** | Shared color, shape, size grouped | Similar icons; related chart observations |
| **Closure** | Incomplete shapes seen as complete | Broken outline = circle; **fills history gaps with assumptions** |
| **Good continuation** | Smooth continuous paths | Intersecting lines continue; organizes edges and contours |
| **Common fate** | Same-direction movement grouped | Flock of birds; separates object from surroundings |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Six principles: figure-ground, proximity, similarity, closure, good continuation, common fate.
- Pattern recognition is not proof.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the Gestalt principles.</summary>

**Answer:** Figure-ground, proximity, similarity, closure, good continuation and common fate.

**Why:** Slides 10-20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the clinical caution of closure.</summary>

**Answer:** Clinicians may fill gaps in a patient's history with assumptions.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the clinical caution of proximity.</summary>

**Answer:** Symptoms documented together may look like a cluster but proximity does not establish a causal relationship.

**Why:** Slide 12.
</details>

## 4. Perceptual constancy and depth perception

- **Constancy:** **size** (a colleague walking away seems smaller on the retina but is not shrinking), **shape** (an open door still seen as a rectangle), **color** (white paper stays white indoors and outdoors).
- **Depth cues:**

| Type | Cues |
|---|---|
| **Binocular** (both eyes) | **Retinal disparity** (different image in each eye), **convergence** (eyes turn inward for near objects) |
| **Monocular** (one eye) | **Linear perspective, relative size, interposition (occlusion), texture gradient, relative height, light and shadow, motion parallax** |

- **Clinical relevance:** judging **distance, location, spatial relations** in **endoscopy, laparoscopy and surgery**; **hand-eye coordination.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Three constancies: size, shape, color.
- Binocular: disparity and convergence; seven monocular cues.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the three constancies.</summary>

**Answer:** Size constancy (same size despite changing retinal image), shape constancy (same shape despite viewing angle), color constancy (stable color under different lighting).

**Why:** Slides 22-26.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the binocular and monocular depth cues.</summary>

**Answer:** Binocular: retinal disparity and convergence. Monocular: linear perspective, relative size, interposition, texture gradient, relative height, light and shadow, motion parallax.

**Why:** Slides 28-29.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does depth perception matter clinically?</summary>

**Answer:** It lets clinicians judge distance and spatial relations in examinations and procedures such as endoscopy, laparoscopy and surgery.

**Why:** Slide 29.
</details>

## 5. Attention and perceptual disturbances

- **Attention** = **selective allocation of processing resources.** **Types:** **selective** (heart sounds amid noise), **sustained** (radiologist reviewing MRIs), **divided** (phone call + notes + labs), **involuntary capture** (sudden monitor alarm).
- **Illusion** = **misinterpretation of a real external stimulus** (coat as a person, wind as a voice, cable as a snake in poor light); **may occur in healthy people**, also in **delirium, sensory impairment, anxiety.**
- **Hallucination** = **perception-like experience with no external stimulus** (voice, person, smell); occurs in **psychiatric, neurological, sleep-related, toxic and medical conditions**; **alone does not establish schizophrenia.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four types of attention.
- Illusion = real stimulus misread; hallucination = no stimulus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the types of attention with an example each.</summary>

**Answer:** Selective (focus on heart sounds), sustained (long MRI review), divided (phone, notes and labs together), involuntary capture (alarm).

**Why:** Slides 30-31.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define an illusion and say whether it is pathological.</summary>

**Answer:** A misinterpretation of a real external stimulus; it may occur in healthy people and is not automatically pathological.

**Why:** Slide 32.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define a hallucination.</summary>

**Answer:** A perception-like experience without an appropriate external stimulus (auditory, visual, olfactory examples); it occurs in many conditions and alone does not prove schizophrenia.

**Why:** Slides 34-35.
</details>

- **Bottom-up = stimulus-driven; top-down = expectation-driven.**
- **Constancy types: size, shape, color.**
- **Hallucinations alone do not establish schizophrenia.**
- **Binocular: disparity and convergence; everything else is monocular.**
