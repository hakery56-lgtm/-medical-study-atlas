## 1. What is learning? Non-associative learning

- ⭐ **Learning** = a **relatively enduring (permanent) change in behavior or knowledge resulting from previous experience with stimuli and responses**.
- **Behavior** = any **observable response** (fainting, salivating, vomiting).
- **Three principles/kinds of learning:** **classical conditioning, operant conditioning, cognitive learning.**
- **Types:** (1) **non-associative**, (2) **associative** (classical + operant), (3) **cognitive**.

### Non-associative learning
- A **relatively permanent change in the strength of a response to a SINGLE stimulus due to repeated exposure**; two forms:

| | **Habituation** | **Sensitization** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | **gradual ↓ in response** to a repeated stimulus that is **neither rewarding nor harmful** | **progressive amplification (↑)** of a response following repeated stimulus |
| Meaning | "harmless, no need to respond strongly" — **habit → less response** | **sensitive → more response** |
| Example | patient hears **regular monitor sounds** → notices first → later hardly bothers | **painful wound**: light touch hurts → repeated touch → even light touch gives **stronger pain** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Learning = relatively permanent change in behavior/knowledge from experience.
- Non-associative: single stimulus. Habituation = ↓ response (monitor sounds); sensitization = ↑ response (painful wound).
- Three kinds: classical, operant, cognitive.

</div>

## 2. Associative and cognitive learning — overview

- **Associative learning** = learning an **association between two stimuli, or between a behavior and a stimulus**; two forms:
  - **Classical conditioning:** a **neutral stimulus gains the ability to produce a response originally elicited by another stimulus**.
  - **Operant conditioning:** the **consequences after a behavior increase or decrease the likelihood that it occurs again**.
- **Cognitive learning:** involves **mental processes (attention, memory)**; may occur by **observation or imitation**; may need **no external reward** or observable behavior.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Associative: classical (stimulus–stimulus) and operant (behavior–consequence).
- Cognitive: mental processes, observation/imitation, no reward needed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define associative learning and its two forms.</summary>

**Answer:** Learning an association between two stimuli or between a behavior and a stimulus; the forms are classical conditioning (a neutral stimulus acquires the ability to elicit a response) and operant conditioning (consequences change the likelihood of a behavior).

**Why:** Overview slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define cognitive learning.</summary>

**Answer:** Learning that involves mental processes such as attention and memory, may occur through observation or imitation, and may not require external rewards or observable behavior.

**Why:** Overview slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate classical from operant conditioning in one sentence each.</summary>

**Answer:** Classical conditioning pairs two stimuli so that a neutral stimulus triggers a response; operant conditioning uses the consequences (reward or punishment) that follow a behavior to make it more or less likely to recur.

**Why:** Pavlov vs Skinner.
</details>

## 3. Classical conditioning (Pavlov)

- **Procedure:** two stimuli are **paired** — the **conditioned stimulus (CS)** presented shortly **before** the **unconditioned stimulus (US)** → a **conditioned response** to the first stimulus.
- **Pavlov noticed dogs salivating BEFORE food** (assistant, food bowl sound, feeding time).
- **Neutral stimulus** = produces **no conditioned response before learning**.

### Elements
| Element | Meaning | Pavlov example |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Unconditioned stimulus (UCS)** | naturally produces an **automatic involuntary response without learning** | **food** |
| ⭐ **Unconditioned response (UCR)** | **automatic, unlearned** response to the UCS | **salivation to food** |
| ⭐ **Conditioned stimulus (CS)** | **previously neutral** stimulus that **after repeated pairing** produces a learned response | **food dish / bell** |
| ⭐ **Conditioned response (CR)** | **learned** response to the CS; **similar to the UCR but may be weaker** | **salivation to the dish** |
- **Unconditioned = unlearned; conditioned = learned.**

### Generalization, discrimination, extinction, spontaneous recovery
(Sara anxious at the **dentist's aftershave** smell.)
- ⭐ **Generalization:** **similar stimuli elicit similar responses**; the more similar, the larger the CR (church bell ≈ lunch bell; similar odors → anxiety).
- ⭐ **Discrimination:** learning to respond to **some stimuli but not others** (aftershave → anxiety; nail-polish smell → none); **adaptive**.
- ⭐ **Extinction:** the **CS repeatedly presented WITHOUT the US** → the CS no longer elicits the CR (needle without pain → fear decreases).
- ⭐ **Spontaneous recovery:** the **CR reappears after extinction without new conditioning** (smelling the aftershave again → anxiety temporarily returns).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- UCS (food) → UCR (salivation); CS (bell/dish) → CR (salivation after pairing). CS presented shortly before US.
- Generalization (similar → similar), discrimination (different → different), extinction (CS without US), spontaneous recovery (CR returns later).
- Example: needle (CS) → pain (US) → fear (CR).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the four elements of classical conditioning with Pavlov's example.</summary>

**Answer:** UCS: naturally produces an automatic response (food). UCR: the unlearned response (salivation to food). CS: a previously neutral stimulus that, after repeated pairing with the UCS, produces a learned response (food dish or bell). CR: the learned response to the CS, similar to the UCR but possibly weaker (salivation to the dish).

**Why:** Elements table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate generalization from discrimination.</summary>

**Answer:** Generalization is the tendency of stimuli similar to the CS to elicit a similar CR (the more similar, the larger the response); discrimination is learning to respond to certain stimuli and not others (aftershave causes anxiety but nail-polish smell does not), which is adaptive.

**Why:** Sara's example.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define extinction and spontaneous recovery with an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Extinction: repeated presentation of the CS without the US so that it no longer elicits the CR (a needle without pain gradually loses its fear). Spontaneous recovery: the extinguished CR reappears after some time without further conditioning (anxiety returns on smelling the aftershave again).

**Why:** Pavlov's phenomena.
</details>

## 4. Operant conditioning (B.F. Skinner)

- **Operant (instrumental) conditioning:** a behavior is followed by a **consequence (reward or punishment)** that **increases or decreases the chance of the behavior recurring**.
- **Law of Effect:** responses with **desirable results are learned ("stamped in")**.
- **Skinner box:** rat taught to press a lever — **positive reinforcement:** **food** for pressing; **negative reinforcement:** **pressing the lever turns off electric shock**.

### Reinforcement vs punishment
| | **Reinforcement (↑ behavior)** | **Punishment (↓ behavior)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive (add)** | **add something desirable** after correct behavior (treat when a dog sits) | **add something unpleasant** after behavior (spanking for cursing; extra homework for misbehavior) |
| **Negative (remove)** | **remove/avoid something unpleasant**: **escape** (snooze button) and **avoidance** (study to avoid bad grade) | **remove something valued** (send child to his room; ban phone for a week) |

### Reinforcement
- **Positive reinforcement:** a pleasant consequence **added** → behavior ↑ (studying → good grade; child with ADHD does homework → praise).
  - **Primary reinforcers:** **satisfy biological needs** — **food, water, sleep, warmth**.
  - **Secondary reinforcers:** acquire value by association with primary ones — **money**.
  - **Toilet-training example (Ahmed, 3 years):** target behavior = urinating in the toilet; **reduce distractions, give fluids**, **reinforce immediately**; ⭐ **shaping = reinforcing successive approximations** (says "I need the toilet" → walks to the bathroom → lowers pants → sits → urinates = strong reward).
- **Negative reinforcement:** behavior **removes or prevents** something unpleasant → behavior ↑.
  - **Escape:** behavior **terminates** an existing unpleasant stimulus (seat belt stops the warning sound; panic patient **leaves the mall** → anxiety falls → leaving more likely).
  - **Avoidance:** behavior **prevents** an unpleasant stimulus that hasn't happened yet (charging the phone early; social-anxiety patient **tells supervisor he is sick** to avoid the meeting).

### Punishment
- **Positive punishment:** **presenting an aversive stimulus** after a response (extra homework).
- **Negative punishment:** **removing a reinforcing stimulus** (allowance; phone ban).
- ⭐ **Punishment rarely works long term — four reasons:** (1) child **avoids the punisher** instead of the behavior; (2) encourages **lying**; (3) creates **fear and anxiety that don't promote learning**; (4) **hitting models aggression**.
- **Making punishment more effective:** (1) **immediately** after the behavior; (2) **consistent** (keep your promise; don't randomly weaken it); (3) **pair with reinforcement of the right behavior**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Reinforcement ↑ behavior; punishment ↓ behavior; positive = add, negative = remove.
- Negative reinforcement = escape or avoidance (NOT punishment).
- Primary reinforcers (food, water, sleep, warmth) vs secondary (money); shaping = successive approximations.
- Punishment: immediate, consistent, paired with reinforcement of correct behavior; fails long-term (avoids punisher, lying, fear, models aggression).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare positive and negative reinforcement with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Positive reinforcement adds a pleasant consequence so the behavior increases (a student studies and gets a good grade; a child with ADHD completes homework and is praised). Negative reinforcement removes or prevents an unpleasant stimulus so the behavior increases: escape (wearing a seat belt to stop the warning sound; leaving a crowded mall to stop panic) and avoidance (charging the phone before it dies; skipping a meeting to avoid social anxiety).

**Why:** Both ↑ behavior.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define primary and secondary reinforcers and shaping.</summary>

**Answer:** Primary reinforcers satisfy biological needs (food, water, sleep, warmth); secondary reinforcers gain value through association with primary ones (money). Shaping is reinforcing successive behaviors that get closer to the target (toilet training: asks to go → walks to bathroom → lowers pants → sits → urinates).

**Why:** Toilet-training example.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does punishment rarely work, and how can it be made effective?</summary>

**Answer:** Severe punishment makes the child avoid the punisher rather than the behavior, encourages lying, creates fear and anxiety that don't promote learning and models aggression. To be effective it should follow the behavior immediately, be consistent (keep the promise; not randomly weakened) and be paired with reinforcement of the right behavior.

**Why:** Uses and abuses of punishment.
</details>

## 5. Cognitive and observational learning

### Cognitive complex learning
- **Mental representation of the world** and operating on these representations; **active; no reward needed**.
- ⭐ **Insight learning:** **sudden unexpected solution to a problem ("Aha!")** — chair to reach a book; medical student suddenly diagnoses **diabetes mellitus** (thirst, polyuria, weight loss, high glucose). (**Köhler's box-and-banana and stick problem**.)
- ⭐ **Trial-and-error learning:** try → fail → try again → success; successful response becomes more likely (remote control; learning manual BP measurement). (**Thorndike's cat** escaping the puzzle box faster with repetition.)

### Observational learning (Bandura)
- Emphasizes **observation, imitation and self-reward**; **no need to perform any behavior or get external reward** to learn.
- ⭐ **Four processes:**
  1. **Attention (watch)** — the observer must attend to the model (woman afraid of spiders watches a person calmly holding one).
  2. **Memory (remember)** — retains what was seen.
  3. **Imitation (do)** — can reproduce the behavior.
  4. **Motivation (want)** — needs an incentive (she wants to go camping with friends).

### Communication skills link
- Learning principles shape **patient behavior and health beliefs**: **reinforce positive health behaviors, modify maladaptive ones; punishment may be ineffective; modeling influences behavior**.
- Use **encouragement/reinforcement for adherence**, explain behaviors to support **retention**, **model appropriate attitudes**, recognize how **past conditioning** shapes patient responses.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Insight = sudden "Aha" (Köhler); trial-and-error = gradual (Thorndike).
- Bandura's four processes: attention, memory, imitation, motivation.
- Clinical use: reinforce adherence, model behavior, explain clearly, appreciate past conditioning.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate insight learning from trial-and-error learning with medical examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Insight learning is a sudden, unexpected solution after understanding the relationships ("Aha!"), e.g. a student suddenly diagnosing diabetes mellitus from thirst, polyuria, weight loss and high glucose. Trial-and-error learning gradually finds the correct response through repeated attempts and mistakes, e.g. learning to measure blood pressure manually after incorrect cuff placement and positioning.

**Why:** Köhler vs Thorndike.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the four processes of observational learning.</summary>

**Answer:** Attention (watching the model), memory (remembering what was seen), imitation (being able to reproduce the behavior) and motivation (having a reason or incentive to imitate), as described by Bandura.

**Why:** Spider-fear example.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does the psychology of learning help communication with patients?</summary>

**Answer:** It guides clinicians to reinforce positive health behaviors and treatment adherence, explain behaviors to support retention, model appropriate attitudes and communication, and recognize how past experiences and conditioning shape patient responses; punishment is often ineffective.

**Why:** Communication skills theme.
</details>
