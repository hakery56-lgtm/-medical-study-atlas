## 2. Affinity, efficacy and potency

- ⭐ **Affinity = ability to bind; efficacy = ability to activate and produce a response.** **Affinity ≠ efficacy.**
- **High affinity + maximal efficacy = full agonist; high affinity + zero efficacy = antagonist.**
- ⭐ **Potency = dose needed for a given effect** (more potent = lower dose); depends on **affinity and efficacy.**
- **Metoprolol and propranolol:** both **antagonists: high affinity, zero efficacy.** **40 mg propranolol lowers BP more than 50 mg metoprolol → propranolol more potent.**

| | **Efficacy** | **Potency** |
|---|---|---|
| **Morphine vs aspirin** | **Morphine higher** | **Morphine higher** |
| **Furosemide vs thiazide** | **Furosemide higher (20-25% vs 5-8% of filtered Na⁺; works if GFR < 30)** | **Thiazide higher (12.5-25 mg) vs furosemide (20-40 mg)** |

- **Furosemide:** **loop of Henle (NKCC2); acute or severe edema.** **Thiazides:** **distal tubule (NCC); long-term hypertension or mild edema.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Efficacy = maximal effect; potency = dose for the effect; they are independent.
- Furosemide: more efficacious; thiazide: more potent.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate affinity, efficacy and potency.</summary>

**Answer:** Affinity is binding ability; efficacy is the ability to activate the receptor; potency is the dose needed for a given effect and depends on both.

**Why:** Q1 answers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which is more potent, metoprolol or propranolol, in this case?</summary>

**Answer:** Propranolol, because 40 mg gives a greater BP fall than 50 mg of metoprolol.

**Why:** Q1 answers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare furosemide and thiazides.</summary>

**Answer:** Furosemide is more efficacious (up to 20-25% Na⁺ excretion, effective with GFR below 30) but less potent; thiazides are more potent (low doses) but less efficacious (5-8%).

**Why:** Q2 answers.
</details>

## 3. Partial agonists and GPCRs

- ⭐ **Partial agonist:** binds and **only partially activates.** **Alone = weak agonist (submaximal response); with a full agonist = partial antagonist** (occupies receptors, blocks full effect).
- **Examples:** **pindolol, oxprenolol, acebutolol (intrinsic sympathomimetic activity);** with **high sympathetic tone**, pindolol acts as an **antagonist → ↓ HR and BP.**
- ⭐ **Adrenergic α and β receptors = GPCRs** (**seven transmembrane domains**) coupled to **G-proteins** (second messengers). **G = guanosine nucleotides (GTP/GDP).** **Response in seconds to minutes** (fits fight-or-flight).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Partial agonist = agonist alone, antagonist beside a full agonist.
- Adrenergic receptors = GPCRs, response in seconds to minutes.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain partial agonism with an example.</summary>

**Answer:** A partial agonist produces a submaximal response alone but, in the presence of a full agonist, occupies receptors and reduces its effect; example: pindolol in high sympathetic tone.

**Why:** Q3 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What type of receptors are adrenergic receptors?</summary>

**Answer:** G-protein-coupled receptors with seven transmembrane domains acting via intracellular G-proteins.

**Why:** Q4 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does G stand for and how fast do GPCRs respond?</summary>

**Answer:** Guanosine nucleotides (GTP/GDP); within seconds to minutes.

**Why:** Q4 answer.
</details>

## 4. Anaphylaxis and beta-blocker safety

- ⭐ **Anaphylaxis:** severe systemic allergy; **histamine** plus **leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines.** **Treat with epinephrine** (reverses hypotension, bronchospasm, laryngeal edema).
- ⭐ **Physiological antagonism:** epinephrine **does not block histamine receptors**; it acts on **α and β receptors with opposite effects** (vasoconstriction vs vasodilation; bronchodilation vs bronchoconstriction).
- **Antihistamines** only block **H1**, not leukotrienes, and are **too slow;** **corticosteroids** have a **slow onset (hours)** and only prevent further symptoms.
- ⭐ **Safety and affinity:** **metoprolol = β1-selective (not specific): relatively safer in lung disease.** **Propranolol = non-selective, equal β1 and β2 affinity → bronchoconstriction, contraindicated in asthma.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Anaphylaxis: epinephrine first (physiological antagonism).
- Propranolol is contraindicated in asthma; metoprolol is relatively safer.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is epinephrine used in anaphylaxis and what is the antagonism type?</summary>

**Answer:** It reverses hypotension, bronchospasm and laryngeal edema through α and β receptors with effects opposite to histamine, which is physiological antagonism.

**Why:** Q5 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why are antihistamines and steroids not first-line?</summary>

**Answer:** Antihistamines block only H1 effects and are too slow; corticosteroids act over hours; neither reverses life-threatening hypotension and airway obstruction quickly.

**Why:** Q5 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare metoprolol and propranolol regarding asthma.</summary>

**Answer:** Metoprolol is β1-selective with low β2 affinity, so it is relatively safer; propranolol blocks β1 and β2 equally and can cause dangerous bronchoconstriction, contraindicated in asthma.

**Why:** Q6 answer.
</details>

## 5. Patient guide

- **Take at the same time every day.** **Do not stop abruptly** (dangerous BP rise, MI); reduce **very gradually over a few weeks.**
- **Missed dose:** take when remembered; **skip if the next is nearly due; never double.**
- **Avoid OTC cold/flu drugs with pseudoephedrine or phenylephrine** (raise BP).
- **Red flags:** extreme dizziness, fainting or **HR < 50-60** with feeling unwell; **severe dyspnea or wheeze;** **new ankle or leg swelling or sudden weight gain;** **masked symptoms of hypoglycemia in diabetics.**
- **Formulations:** **metoprolol tartrate tablets (25, 50, 100 mg) and IV (Betaloc);** **propranolol HCl oral solution, tablets (10, 40, 80, 160 mg), modified-release capsules (80, 160 mg).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Never stop abruptly; skip a nearly-due missed dose.
- Avoid pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why must beta-blockers not be stopped suddenly?</summary>

**Answer:** Sudden withdrawal can cause a dangerous rise in BP or heart problems such as MI; doses must be reduced gradually over weeks.

**Why:** Patient's guide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the red flags?</summary>

**Answer:** Extreme dizziness or fainting or HR below 50-60 with illness, severe breathlessness or wheeze, new swelling or sudden weight gain, and masked hypoglycemia symptoms in diabetics.

**Why:** Patient's guide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What should be avoided while taking beta-blockers?</summary>

**Answer:** OTC cold and flu medicines containing pseudoephedrine or phenylephrine, which raise BP.

**Why:** Patient's guide.
</details>
