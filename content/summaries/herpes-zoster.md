## 1. Definition, virology and pathogenesis

- ⭐ **Herpes zoster (HZ) = shingles** = the clinical syndrome caused by **reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus (VZV)** in **sensory dorsal-root or cranial-nerve ganglia**.
- **Primary VZV infection = varicella (chickenpox)**; the virus stays **latent** and can reactivate **years or decades later** as zoster. **Varicella = primary infection; zoster = reactivation.**
- **Virology:** **Herpesviridae → Alphaherpesvirinae; enveloped; double-stranded DNA; highly neurotropic.** Other clinically important herpesviruses: **HSV-1, HSV-2, EBV, CMV, HHV-6, HHV-7, HHV-8.**

### Pathogenesis
| Stage | Events |
|---|---|
| **Primary infection** | Enters via **respiratory tract or conjunctiva** → **replication in regional lymphoid tissue** → **viremia** → **dissemination to skin** → **varicella** |
| **Latency** | Virus travels to **sensory nerve endings** → latent in **dorsal-root ganglia** and **cranial-nerve sensory ganglia** |
| **Reactivation** | Years later **impaired VZV-specific cellular immunity** → **replication in the sensory ganglion** → travels **down the sensory nerve** → **pain + unilateral dermatomal vesicular eruption** |

### Why does reactivation occur? (risk factors)
- ⭐ **Major risk factor: declining cell-mediated immunity against VZV.**
- **Age:** risk **increases substantially with advancing age.**
- **Immunosuppression:** **HIV/AIDS, hematological malignancy, solid tumors, hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation, solid-organ transplantation, chemotherapy, corticosteroids, other immunosuppressive drugs.**
- **Other factors:** **severe physical or psychological stress, some chronic diseases, previous VZV infection.** Older adults and immunocompromised patients are particularly susceptible to **severe disease and complications**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Zoster = reactivation of latent VZV in sensory ganglia; varicella = primary infection.
- VZV: Herpesviridae, Alphaherpesvirinae, enveloped dsDNA, neurotropic.
- Latency in dorsal-root/cranial-nerve ganglia; reactivation when cell-mediated immunity falls (age, immunosuppression).

</div>

## 2. Clinical features

- Classically **three phases**; the slides detail **A. the prodromal phase** and **B. the rash**.

### A. Prodromal phase
- **Several days before the rash.** Symptoms: **burning pain, tingling, hyperesthesia, itching, numbness, deep neuralgic pain**; systemic: **malaise, fever, headache.**
- 🚩 **Clinical trap:** **severe localized pain before any skin lesion** can mimic **ischemic heart disease, biliary disease, renal colic, appendicitis, pleurisy, dental disease** (depending on the dermatome).

### B. Rash
- **Macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → crust.**
- **Dermatomal distribution:** usually **unilateral, painful, does not cross the midline, one or two adjacent dermatomes, lesions in crops.**
- **Heals in about 2–4 weeks** in uncomplicated disease.

### Common dermatomes
| Dermatome | Features |
|---|---|
| **Thoracic** | **Most common**; **band-like eruption** around the chest or abdomen |
| **Trigeminal V1 (ophthalmic)** | **Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO)** |
| **Cervical** | Neck, shoulder, upper limb |
| **Lumbosacral** | Buttock lesions, genital lesions, lower-limb pain |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Prodrome (days before rash): burning pain, tingling, hyperesthesia, itching, numbness ± malaise, fever, headache.
- Trap: pain before rash mimics MI, biliary disease, renal colic, appendicitis, pleurisy, dental disease.
- Rash: macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → crust; unilateral, painful, doesn't cross midline, 1–2 dermatomes, in crops; heals 2–4 weeks; thoracic most common.

</div>

## 3. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus and Ramsay Hunt syndrome

### Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO)
- **VZV affecting the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (V1).** Risks: **keratitis, uveitis, episcleritis/scleritis, retinitis, optic neuritis, visual loss.**
- ⭐ **Hutchinson sign:** **vesicles on the tip, side or root of the nose** = involvement of the **nasociliary branch of V1** → **increased risk of ocular involvement.**
- 🚩 **Any V1 zoster needs ophthalmological assessment**; an **initially normal exam does not completely exclude later ocular complications.**

### Ramsay Hunt syndrome
- **Zoster of the geniculate ganglion/facial nerve.** **Classical triad: ear pain + vesicular eruption (external auditory canal or pinna) + ipsilateral facial paralysis.** May also cause **hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- HZO = V1 zoster: keratitis, uveitis, episcleritis/scleritis, retinitis, optic neuritis, visual loss.
- Hutchinson sign (nose tip/side/root) = nasociliary branch → higher ocular risk; all V1 zoster → ophthalmology.
- Ramsay Hunt = geniculate ganglion/facial nerve: ear pain + vesicles + ipsilateral facial paralysis (± hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo).

</div>

## 4. Complications, dissemination and HIV

### Neurological complications
- ⭐ **Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN)** = the **most important long-term complication**: **persistent neuropathic pain in the affected dermatome after the acute eruption has resolved**; commonly defined as **≥ 90 days after rash onset.** Risk ↑ with **older age, severe acute pain, severe rash and ophthalmic involvement.**
- **Other:** **meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis, cerebellitis, cranial neuropathies.**
- **VZV vasculopathy:** **TIA, ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage**; may occur **weeks or months after zoster.**

### Disseminated zoster
- In **immunocompromised** patients VZV spreads beyond the primary dermatome: **numerous lesions outside the original dermatome.**
- **Visceral dissemination:** **lung (pneumonitis), liver (hepatitis), brain (encephalitis), GI tract, other organs.** A **medical emergency**, particularly in severely immunocompromised patients.

### Herpes zoster and HIV
- Zoster can occur at **any stage of HIV infection.** Suspect **significant immunosuppression** with: **recurrent zoster, multidermatomal disease, disseminated disease, severe/prolonged disease, unusual localization, visceral involvement.**
- **Younger adult with recurrent or unusually severe zoster → consider HIV testing** (and other causes of immunosuppression).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PHN: most important long-term complication; pain ≥ 90 days after rash; risk ↑ with age, severe pain/rash, ophthalmic involvement.
- Others: meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis, cerebellitis, cranial neuropathies; vasculopathy → TIA, stroke, ICH (weeks–months later).
- Disseminated zoster (immunocompromised): lung, liver, brain, GI → emergency. Recurrent/multidermatomal/severe zoster → think HIV.

</div>

## 5. Diagnosis and differential diagnosis

- **Typical cases: clinical diagnosis** = **unilateral painful vesicles in a dermatomal distribution.**
- ⭐ **PCR for VZV DNA** = preferred confirmatory test; useful in **atypical disease, immunocompromised patients** or to **differentiate from other vesicular eruptions.** **Specimen:** vesicle fluid, lesion base, scab.

| Differential | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Herpes simplex** | Can be **zosteriform**; favors HSV: **recurrent same-site lesions, often genital/orolabial, less strictly dermatomal** |
| **Contact dermatitis** | **Pruritic** rather than intensely neuropathic; **exposure-related**; not classically dermatomal |
| **Impetigo** | **Superficial bacterial infection; honey-colored crust** |
| **Insect bites** | Usually **lack the classic dermatomal distribution** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Typical zoster = clinical diagnosis (unilateral painful dermatomal vesicles).
- PCR for VZV DNA from vesicle fluid/lesion base/scab when confirmation is needed (atypical, immunocompromised).
- DDx: HSV, contact dermatitis, impetigo, insect bites.

</div>

**Why:** Slide "Diagnosis".

## 6. Treatment, infection control and prevention

### Antiviral treatment
- ⭐ **Most effective when started early, ideally within 72 hours of rash onset.**

| Drug | Oral dose |
|---|---|
| **Valacyclovir** | **1 g three times daily** |
| **Famciclovir** | **500 mg three times daily** |
| **Acyclovir** | **800 mg five times daily** |

- **Immunocompromised adults with HIV and localized dermatomal zoster:** these regimens for about **7–10 days**, longer if lesions resolve slowly. **Adjust the dose in renal impairment.**
- **After 72 hours do NOT automatically withhold treatment:** still treat if **new lesions keep appearing, lesions not fully crusted, ophthalmic involvement, neurological complications, immunocompromised patient, severe disease.** **HIV (NIH): treat as soon as possible, within 1 week of rash onset or any time before complete crusting.**

### Pain management
- **Paracetamol/acetaminophen, NSAIDs when appropriate, opioids for severe acute pain, neuropathic agents.** For neuropathic pain: **gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclic antidepressants, topical lidocaine** in selected patients. Consider **renal function, age, drug interactions, adverse effects.**

### Corticosteroids
- **Not routinely recommended** in uncomplicated zoster. With antivirals they may **reduce acute pain or improve short-term function** in selected immunocompetent patients, but **do not reliably prevent PHN**; **avoid or use very cautiously in immunocompromised patients**; not routine in HIV-associated zoster.

### Infection control
- A **susceptible person exposed to zoster lesions develops chickenpox, not shingles.** Transmission: **direct contact with vesicle fluid; aerosolization from lesions (especially disseminated disease).**
- Patients should **cover lesions, avoid scratching, perform hand hygiene, avoid susceptible pregnant women, newborns and immunocompromised individuals until lesions have crusted.**

### Prevention — Shingrix
- **RZV (Shingrix) = non-live recombinant vaccine.** **Immunocompetent adults ≥ 50 years: 2 doses (dose 1 → 2–6 months → dose 2).** **Immunocompromised adults ≥ 19 years: 2 doses.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Antivirals within 72 h: valacyclovir 1 g TID, famciclovir 500 mg TID, acyclovir 800 mg 5×/day; don't withhold late if new lesions, eye/neuro involvement, immunocompromised or severe.
- Pain: paracetamol/NSAIDs/opioids; gabapentin, pregabalin, TCAs, topical lidocaine. Steroids not routine; don't prevent PHN.
- Zoster contacts get chickenpox; cover lesions until crusted. Shingrix = non-live, 2 doses (≥ 50 y; ≥ 19 y if immunocompromised).

</div>
