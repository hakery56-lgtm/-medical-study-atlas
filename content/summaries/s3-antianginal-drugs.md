## 1. Angina, oxygen balance and types

- ⭐ **Angina pectoris** = **transient myocardial ischemia**: O2 **supply cannot meet demand**. Typical symptom: **pressure, heaviness or squeezing chest discomfort**, radiating to the **arm, jaw or neck**; fatigue, nausea or diaphoresis may occur.
- **Demand ↑:** ↑ heart rate, ↑ contractility, ↑ wall stress (hypertension, ↑ LVEDP). **Supply ↓:** coronary **stenosis** (atherosclerosis), **vasospasm**, thrombosis, reduced perfusion (**hypotension**), **decreased diastolic filling time (tachycardia)**.
- **Treatment principle:** **reduce demand and/or improve perfusion**.
- **Obstruction and symptoms:** baseline **≈ 30%** = no angina; **≈ 50%** (decreased vascular tone, e.g. vasodilators) = potential for angina; **≈ 70%** (increased tone during exercise) = **exercise-induced angina**; **≥ 90%** (frank vasospasm) = **resting angina**.

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Stable** | **Predictable exertional** pain; improves with **rest or nitroglycerin** |
| **Unstable** | **New, worsening or rest** pain; **acute coronary syndrome** |
| **Variant (Prinzmetal)** | **Episodic rest pain** due to **coronary spasm** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Angina = supply < demand; increase supply and/or decrease demand.
- Stable = exertional/predictable; unstable = ACS; Prinzmetal = spasm at rest.
- Obstruction 30% none, 70% exertional, ≥ 90% resting.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the pathophysiology of angina.</summary>

**Answer:** Atherosclerotic narrowing, coronary spasm or reduced perfusion lowers myocardial O2 supply, while a high heart rate, contractility or wall stress raises demand; when supply is less than demand, reversible myocardial ischemia occurs.

**Why:** Slides "Angina pectoris: overview" and "Pathophysiology of Angina".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the three types of angina.</summary>

**Answer:** Stable: predictable exertional pain relieved by rest or nitroglycerin. Unstable: new, worsening or rest pain, an acute coronary syndrome. Variant (Prinzmetal): episodic rest pain from coronary spasm.

**Why:** Slide "Types of angina".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does the degree of obstruction relate to symptoms?</summary>

**Answer:** About 30% obstruction gives no angina; about 50% (decreased vascular tone) gives potential for angina; about 70% with exercise gives exercise-induced angina; ≥ 90% with frank vasospasm gives resting angina.

## 2. Mechanisms of antianginal drugs

| Aim | Effect | Drugs |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Decrease O2 demand** | ↓ **heart rate**, ↓ **contractility** | **β-blockers, non-DHP CCBs** |
| | ↓ **afterload** (arterial BP) | **Nitrates, CCBs** |
| **2. Increase O2 supply** | **Coronary vasodilation**; relieve **coronary spasm** | **Nitrates, CCBs** |
| **3. Improve myocardial metabolism** | More efficient energy use; ↓ late Na⁺ current → ↓ Ca²⁺ overload | **Ranolazine** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four classes: β-blockers, CCBs, nitrates, ranolazine.
- β-blockers and non-DHP CCBs lower HR and contractility; nitrates and CCBs dilate and lower afterload.
- Ranolazine acts on metabolism through the late sodium current.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the three mechanisms of antianginal drugs and the drugs for each.</summary>

**Answer:** Decrease demand (β-blockers and non-DHP CCBs for rate and contractility; nitrates and CCBs for afterload); increase supply (nitrates and CCBs by coronary vasodilation and relief of spasm); improve metabolism (ranolazine).

**Why:** Slide "Mechanisms of Action of Antianginal Drugs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which drugs relieve coronary spasm?</summary>

**Answer:** Calcium channel blockers and nitrates.

## 3. β-blockers

- **Mechanism:** block **cardiac β1 receptors** → ↓ **heart rate, contractility and blood pressure** → ↓ O2 demand at rest and on exertion → ↑ exercise tolerance, ↓ angina frequency.
- **Examples:** **metoprolol, atenolol** (β1-selective); **propranolol** (nonselective prototype).
- **Uses:** chronic **stable angina**, especially with **tachycardia**; **recent MI** or **HFrEF**.
- **Adverse effects:** **bradycardia, fatigue, hypotension, AV conduction slowing**; **nonselective agents → bronchoconstriction**; may **mask adrenergic warning symptoms of hypoglycemia**.
- ⭐ **Avoid abrupt discontinuation** (rebound angina, hypertension, MI). **Avoid in vasospastic angina.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- β1 blockade lowers HR, contractility and BP; best with tachycardia, recent MI, HFrEF.
- AEs: bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, bronchospasm (nonselective), masked hypoglycemia.
- Do not stop abruptly; avoid in vasospastic angina.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain how β-blockers relieve angina.</summary>

**Answer:** By blocking cardiac β1 receptors they decrease heart rate, contractility and blood pressure, lowering myocardial O2 demand at rest and during exertion, which increases exercise tolerance and reduces angina frequency.

**Why:** Slide "β-blockers: Mechanism of action".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the adverse effects and cautions of β-blockers.</summary>

**Answer:** Bradycardia, fatigue, hypotension and AV conduction slowing; bronchoconstriction with nonselective agents; masking of hypoglycemia warning symptoms; rebound angina, hypertension or MI if stopped abruptly; avoided in vasospastic angina.

**Why:** Slide "β-blockers: examples and uses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give examples of selective and nonselective β-blockers.</summary>

**Answer:** Metoprolol and atenolol are β1-selective; propranolol is the nonselective prototype.

**Why:** Slide "β-blockers: examples and uses".
</details>

## 4. Calcium channel blockers (CCBs)

| Feature | **Dihydropyridines** (amlodipine, nifedipine) | **Non-dihydropyridines** (verapamil, diltiazem) |
|---|---|---|
| **Site** | **Vascular smooth muscle** L-type Ca²⁺ channels | **SA and AV nodes and myocardium** |
| **Peripheral resistance** | **↓** (marked vasodilation) | **↓** (less than DHPs) |
| **Contractility** | No effect | **↓** (negative inotropic) |
| **SA rate** | No effect | **Slows** |
| **AV conduction** | No effect | **Slows** |
| **Adverse effects** | **Peripheral edema, flushing, headache, hypotension, reflex tachycardia** | **Bradycardia, AV block, constipation (verapamil)** |

- **Amlodipine:** prominent arterial vasodilation, minimal AV-node effect. **Nifedipine:** long-acting vasodilator - ⭐ **avoid short-acting nifedipine in coronary artery disease**. DHPs are useful in **stable or vasospastic angina**.
- **Verapamil:** slows AV conduction, ↓ HR and contractility. **Diltiazem:** AV nodal slowing + coronary vasodilation. Useful when **rate control and angina relief** are both needed; **avoid in HFrEF and significant AV block**; **caution with β-blockers**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- DHPs = vasodilators (edema, flushing, headache); non-DHPs = heart rate/AV slowing (bradycardia, AV block, constipation with verapamil).
- Avoid non-DHPs in HFrEF and AV block; avoid short-acting nifedipine in CAD.
- CCBs are preferred in vasospastic angina.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare dihydropyridine and non-dihydropyridine CCBs.</summary>

**Answer:** DHPs (amlodipine, nifedipine) act on vascular smooth muscle, causing marked arterial vasodilation without effect on contractility, SA rate or AV conduction (edema, flushing, headache). Non-DHPs (verapamil, diltiazem) act on the SA/AV nodes and myocardium, decreasing heart rate, contractility and AV conduction, with less vasodilation (bradycardia, AV block, constipation).

**Why:** Slides "Calcium channel blockers" and "Dihydropyridine vs Non-Dihydropyridine".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. When should non-dihydropyridine CCBs be avoided?</summary>

**Answer:** In HFrEF and significant AV block, and with caution when combined with β-blockers.

**Why:** Slide "Non-dihydropyridine CCBs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What precaution applies to nifedipine?</summary>

**Answer:** Short-acting nifedipine should be avoided in coronary artery disease; the long-acting vasodilator is used, with edema, flushing, headache and hypotension as adverse effects.

**Why:** Slide "Dihydropyridine CCBs".
</details>

## 5. Organic nitrates

- ⭐ **Mechanism:** **nitrate → nitric oxide (NO) → soluble guanylate cyclase → ↑ cGMP → myosin light-chain dephosphorylation → vascular smooth muscle relaxation.** **Venodilation ↓ preload and wall stress**; **coronary dilation relieves vasospasm**.
- **Nitroglycerin (GTN):** **sublingual tablet or spray** = rapid relief; **bypasses hepatic first-pass**; used for an **acute stable-angina episode or before predictable exertion**; **persistent/new severe chest pain needs emergency assessment**. **Stored in dark amber glass** (light-sensitive).
- **Adverse effects (vasodilation):** cerebral arterial dilation → **throbbing headache**; venous pooling → **orthostatic hypotension/syncope**; fall in BP → **reflex tachycardia**; flushing.
- ⭐ **Safety:** **never combine with PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil)** → profound hypotension.
- **Long-acting:** **isosorbide mononitrate** (longer duration, good oral bioavailability), **isosorbide dinitrate**, **transdermal nitroglycerin** (prophylaxis, **not** acute rescue). **Rapid tolerance** with continuous exposure → **10-12-hour nitrate-free interval** restores sensitivity.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- NO → guanylate cyclase → cGMP → relaxation; venodilation lowers preload.
- Sublingual GTN for acute attacks; keep in dark glass; patches/ISMN for prevention only.
- Contraindicated with sildenafil; nitrate-free interval prevents tolerance.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanism of nitrates.</summary>

**Answer:** Nitrates release nitric oxide, which activates soluble guanylate cyclase, raising cGMP and causing myosin light-chain dephosphorylation and smooth muscle relaxation; venodilation reduces preload and wall stress and coronary dilation relieves spasm.

**Why:** Slide "Organic nitrates".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the adverse effects of nitrates and the major contraindication.</summary>

**Answer:** Throbbing headache, flushing, orthostatic hypotension/syncope, reflex tachycardia and rapid tolerance; contraindicated with PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil because of profound hypotension.

**Why:** Slides "Nitrate adverse effects" and "Nitrates: adverse effects and safety".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How are nitrates used for acute relief and for prevention?</summary>

**Answer:** Sublingual nitroglycerin gives rapid relief for an acute stable attack or before exertion (stored in dark amber glass); long-acting forms (isosorbide mononitrate, dinitrate, transdermal nitroglycerin) are for prophylaxis with a daily nitrate-free interval of 10-12 hours to limit tolerance.

**Why:** Slides "Nitroglycerin: acute relief" and "Long-acting nitrates".
</details>

## 6. Ranolazine, onset of action and choosing the drug

- ⭐ **Ranolazine:** **inhibits the late sodium current (INa,L)** → ↓ intracellular Na⁺ → ↓ reverse-mode **Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange** → ↓ **Ca²⁺ overload** → **improved diastolic relaxation** (less wall tension) and **better myocardial energy efficiency (↓ O2 demand)**. **Add-on chronic angina prevention.**

| Drug / route | Onset | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitroglycerin IV / sublingual** | **1-3 min** | **Acute relief** |
| Nitroglycerin patch; **isosorbide mononitrate** oral | ~30-60 min | Prevention |
| **β-blockers** oral | ~1-2 h | Prevention |
| **Verapamil / diltiazem** oral | ~1-2 h | Prevention |
| **Amlodipine** oral | Gradual, ~6-12 h | Long-term prevention |
| **Ranolazine** (ER) | Days; steady state ~3 days | Add-on prevention |

| Condition | β-blockers | CCBs | Long-acting nitrates | Ranolazine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Recent MI** | **Preferred** | Neutral | Neutral | Neutral |
| **Vasospastic angina** | **Avoid** | **Preferred** | Neutral | - |
| **Hypertension** | **Preferred** | **Preferred** | Neutral | Neutral |
| **Hypotension** | Caution | Caution | Caution | **Preferred** |
| **HFrEF** | **Preferred** | DHP neutral; **non-DHP avoid** | **Preferred** | Neutral |
| **Diabetes** | Caution | **Preferred** | **Preferred** | Neutral |
| **Asthma** | **Nonselective: avoid**; selective: caution | **Preferred** | **Preferred** | Neutral |
| **COPD** | Caution | **Preferred** | **Preferred** | Neutral |

- Colour key: **green = preferred, yellow = neutral, orange = caution/close monitoring, red = generally avoid.**
- Note: the onset table carries asterisks (*) that the slide does not explain; treat the onset times as approximate.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Ranolazine blocks the late Na⁺ current: less Ca²⁺ overload, better relaxation and energy use; add-on therapy.
- Only sublingual/IV nitroglycerin acts within minutes; the rest are preventive.
- Choose by comorbidity: β-blocker for post-MI/HFrEF/hypertension; CCB for vasospasm/asthma/COPD; ranolazine for hypotension.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the mechanism of ranolazine.</summary>

**Answer:** It inhibits the late sodium current, so intracellular sodium and calcium overload during ischemia fall; diastolic relaxation improves (less wall tension) and myocardial energy use becomes more efficient, lowering O2 demand.

**Why:** Slide "Ranolazine: late sodium current".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which antianginal drugs act within minutes and which are preventive?</summary>

**Answer:** IV or sublingual nitroglycerin acts within 1-3 minutes for acute relief; patches, isosorbide mononitrate, β-blockers, verapamil/diltiazem, amlodipine and ranolazine are preventive, with onsets from about 30 minutes to days.

**Why:** Slide "How quickly do antianginal drugs work?".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Choose antianginal therapy in vasospastic angina, asthma and HFrEF.</summary>

**Answer:** Vasospastic angina: CCBs preferred, β-blockers avoided. Asthma: CCBs and long-acting nitrates preferred, nonselective β-blockers avoided. HFrEF: β-blockers and long-acting nitrates preferred, non-DHP CCBs avoided.

**Why:** Slide "Treatment of Angina in Patients with Concomitant Diseases".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Only sublingual/IV nitroglycerin is for acute relief; patches and isosorbide are NOT rescue.**
- **Nitrates + sildenafil (PDE5 inhibitors) = never.**
- **β-blockers: not in vasospastic angina, not stopped abruptly, nonselective ones avoided in asthma.**
- **Non-DHP CCBs: avoid in HFrEF and AV block; short-acting nifedipine avoided in CAD.**
- **Nitrate tolerance is prevented by a 10-12-hour nitrate-free interval; GTN tablets go in dark amber glass.**
