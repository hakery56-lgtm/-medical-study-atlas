## 1. Erb's point and the two trunk injuries

- ⭐ **Erb's point** = the meeting point of **six nerves** on the upper trunk:
  - the **ventral rami of C5 and C6**,
  - **two branches** of the upper trunk: the **suprascapular nerve** and the **nerve to subclavius**,
  - the **two divisions** of the upper trunk (anterior and posterior).
- Two key clinical patterns:

| Injury | Roots | Name |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper trunk** | **C5–C6** | **Erb's (Erb–Duchenne) palsy** |
| **Lower trunk** | **C8–T1** | **Klumpke's palsy** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Erb's point = C5 + C6 rami + suprascapular + nerve to subclavius + 2 divisions (six nerves).
- Upper trunk = C5–C6 = Erb's; lower trunk = C8–T1 = Klumpke's.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is Erb's point and which structures meet there?</summary>

**Answer:** It is the meeting point of six nerves on the upper trunk of the brachial plexus: the ventral rami of C5 and C6, the two branches of the upper trunk (suprascapular nerve and nerve to subclavius) and the two divisions of the upper trunk.

**Why:** Direct definition from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which roots form the upper and lower trunks?</summary>

**Answer:** The upper trunk is formed by C5 and C6; the lower trunk by C8 and T1.

**Why:** The root level tells you which palsy you are dealing with.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which two palsies are the main brachial plexus injuries of the lecture?</summary>

**Answer:** Upper trunk injury (Erb–Duchenne palsy) and lower trunk injury (Klumpke's palsy).

**Why:** Every deformity in the lecture comes back to these two.
</details>

## 2. Upper trunk injury (Erb–Duchenne paralysis)

- **Damage:** the **C5 and C6 fibers** of the superior trunk.
- **Mechanism:** **stretching or tearing** of the upper plexus in the **posterior triangle of the neck**:
  - a **fall on the shoulder** in which the **head and shoulder are forced apart**,
  - **obstructed labour** (obstetric traction).
- **Nerves affected:** **musculocutaneous, axillary, suprascapular** and the **nerve to subclavius**.
- **Motor:** paralysis of the **abductors of the shoulder, flexors of the elbow and supinators** → **flat shoulder** + ⭐ **policeman's (waiter's) tip deformity**: **arm adducted, forearm extended and pronated**. **Wasting** of the scapular muscles and the front of the arm.
- **Sensory loss:** lower half of the **deltoid**, **lateral arm, lateral forearm, lateral hand and lateral 2 fingers**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- C5–C6; fall with head/shoulder separation, or obstructed labour.
- Policeman's tip = adducted arm, extended and pronated forearm.
- Lateral-side sensory loss (lateral arm, forearm, 2 fingers).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanism of an upper trunk injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Excessive stretching or tearing of the upper brachial plexus in the posterior triangle of the neck, either in a fall on the shoulder where the head and shoulder are forced apart, or in obstructed labour.

**Why:** Two classic causes asked together.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does the arm take the policeman's tip position?</summary>

**Answer:** The shoulder abductors, elbow flexors and supinators are paralyzed, so the arm hangs adducted with the forearm extended and pronated (policeman's / waiter's tip), with a flat shoulder.

**Why:** Link muscle loss with posture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which nerves are affected and what is the sensory loss in Erb's palsy?</summary>

**Answer:** Musculocutaneous, axillary, suprascapular and the nerve to subclavius. Sensory loss over the lower half of the deltoid, the lateral arm, lateral forearm, lateral hand and the lateral 2 fingers.

**Why:** Motor nerves + sensory map.
</details>

## 3. Lower trunk injury (Klumpke's palsy)

- **Damage:** the **C8 and T1 fibers** of the lower trunk.
- **Causes:** the upper limb is **suddenly pulled upward and away from the body**; **obstetric injury (shoulder dystocia)**.
- **Muscles affected:**
  - **ulnar-supplied intrinsic muscles**: **interossei and the medial two lumbricals**,
  - the **thenar muscles** (C8–T1 fibers of the **median nerve**).
- **Motor:** ⭐ **combined claw hand + some degree of ape hand**; weakness of the thenar muscles. In the claw: **MCP joints hyperextended, IP joints flexed**.
- **Sensory loss:** the **medial border of the forearm** and the **medial 1½ digits**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- C8–T1; sudden upward pull or shoulder dystocia.
- Ulnar + median intrinsics → combined claw with some ape hand.
- Sensory loss on the medial side (medial forearm, 1½ fingers).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain why Klumpke's palsy gives a combined claw hand.</summary>

**Answer:** C8–T1 lesions paralyze the interossei and medial two lumbricals (ulnar nerve) and weaken the thenar muscles (median nerve), so the MCP joints hyperextend and the IP joints flex, with some ape-hand features.

**Why:** Both nerves share the C8–T1 fibers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the causes of a lower trunk injury?</summary>

**Answer:** A sudden pull of the upper limb upward and away from the body, and obstetric injury such as shoulder dystocia.

**Why:** Opposite direction of force to Erb's palsy.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the sensory loss of Erb's and Klumpke's palsies.</summary>

**Answer:** Erb's palsy: lateral side (lower deltoid, lateral arm and forearm, lateral hand and 2 fingers). Klumpke's palsy: medial side (medial border of the forearm and medial 1½ digits).

**Why:** Lateral = C5–C6, medial = C8–T1.
</details>

## 4. Root avulsion and Horner's syndrome

- **Pre-ganglionic (intraforaminal)** avulsion → accompanied by ⭐ **Horner's syndrome** (**T1** involvement): **ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis**.
- **Post-ganglionic (extraforaminal)** injury → **potentially repairable**.
- The finding that indicates a **T1 root avulsion** is **Horner's syndrome**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pre-ganglionic = intraforaminal = Horner's.
- Post-ganglionic = extraforaminal = may be repaired.
- Horner's = ptosis + miosis + anhidrosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate pre-ganglionic from post-ganglionic root injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Pre-ganglionic (intraforaminal) avulsion is proximal to the dorsal root ganglion and is associated with Horner's syndrome; post-ganglionic (extraforaminal) injury is potentially repairable.

**Why:** Decides the prognosis.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What finding indicates a T1 root avulsion?</summary>

**Answer:** Horner's syndrome: ptosis, miosis and anhidrosis on the affected side.

**Why:** Slide quiz Q3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the features of Horner's syndrome.</summary>

**Answer:** Drooping eyelid (ptosis), constricted pupil (miosis) and lack of sweating on the face (anhidrosis).

**Why:** Classic triad.
</details>

## 5. Axillary nerve injury

- **Root:** **C5–C6**; a branch of the posterior cord.
- **Common sites:** ⭐ **fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus** and **inferior dislocation of the shoulder**.
- **Muscles:** **deltoid and teres minor**.
- **Motor:** loss of abduction from **15° to 110°**; **atrophy of the deltoid** → loss of the rounded shoulder contour (flat shoulder); **weak lateral rotation**.
- **Sensory:** skin over the **cap of the shoulder** (via the **upper lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm**).
- **Test:** **resisted abduction** of the shoulder.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Surgical neck fracture or inferior dislocation.
- Deltoid + teres minor; abduction 15°–110° lost.
- Flat shoulder; sensory loss over the shoulder cap.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the common sites of axillary nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus and inferior dislocation of the shoulder joint.

**Why:** The nerve winds around the surgical neck and lies below the joint.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the motor and sensory deficits.</summary>

**Answer:** Loss of abduction between 15° and 110° and weak lateral rotation, deltoid atrophy with loss of the rounded shoulder contour, and sensory loss over the cap of the shoulder.

**Why:** Deltoid + teres minor + upper lateral cutaneous nerve.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do you test the deltoid?</summary>

**Answer:** By resisted abduction of the shoulder.

**Why:** Deltoid is the main abductor from 15° to 110°.
</details>

## 6. Suprascapular nerve injury

- **Root:** **C5–C6**.
- **Site:** **entrapment** as the nerve crosses the **superior border of the scapula in the scapular (suprascapular) notch**, under the transverse scapular ligament.
- **Muscles:** **supraspinatus and infraspinatus**.
- **Motor:** weak **initiation of the first 15° of abduction** and some weakness of **external rotation**.
- **Sensory:** ⭐ **none**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Notch entrapment; supraspinatus + infraspinatus.
- First 15° of abduction + external rotation weak.
- No sensory loss.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Where is the suprascapular nerve injured and which muscles are affected?</summary>

**Answer:** It is entrapped as it crosses the superior border of the scapula in the suprascapular notch; supraspinatus and infraspinatus are affected.

**Why:** Classic entrapment site.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What functional deficits result?</summary>

**Answer:** Weak initiation of the first 15° of abduction and some weakness of external rotation of the shoulder.

**Why:** Supraspinatus starts abduction; infraspinatus rotates laterally.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is there no sensory loss?</summary>

**Answer:** The nerve supplies only muscles (supraspinatus and infraspinatus) and carries no cutaneous branch, so the deficit is purely motor.

**Why:** Differs from the axillary nerve, which has a skin patch.
</details>

## 7. Long thoracic nerve injury

- **Root:** **C5, C6, C7**.
- **Course/site:** runs on the **superficial surface of serratus anterior**; injured by **trauma to the lateral chest wall** or **mastectomy** (cut at the lateral aspect of the mammary gland).
- **Muscle:** **serratus anterior**.
- **Motor:** difficulty in **overhead abduction**, weak **protraction**, and the sign ⭐ **winged scapula**.
- **Sensory:** none.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- C5–C7; serratus anterior.
- Mastectomy or lateral chest wall trauma.
- Winged scapula; no sensory loss.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. When is the long thoracic nerve injured?</summary>

**Answer:** In trauma to the lateral chest wall or during mastectomy, because it lies superficially on serratus anterior at the lateral aspect of the mammary gland.

**Why:** Surgical relation.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the signs of long thoracic nerve palsy?</summary>

**Answer:** Winged scapula, difficulty in overhead abduction and weak protraction of the scapula; no sensory loss.

**Why:** Serratus anterior holds the scapula against the chest wall.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the root values and the muscle supplied.</summary>

**Answer:** C5, C6 and C7; serratus anterior.

**Why:** Often asked directly.
</details>

## 8. Deformity ↔ nerve table

| Deformity | Nerve / level |
|---|---|
| Flattening of the shoulder contour | **Axillary** |
| Wrist drop | **Radial** |
| Partial claw hand | **Ulnar** |
| Benediction hand | **Median** |
| Winging of the scapula | **Long thoracic** |
| Ape-like hand | **Median** |
| Waiter's tip position | **Upper trunk** (Erb–Duchenne) |
| Combined claw hand | **Lower trunk** (Klumpke) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Memorize this table: one deformity, one nerve.
- Median gives both ape and benediction; ulnar gives partial claw; lower trunk gives combined claw.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Which nerve causes flattening of the shoulder, which wrist drop and which winged scapula?</summary>

**Answer:** Axillary nerve (flat shoulder), radial nerve (wrist drop) and long thoracic nerve (winged scapula).

**Why:** Three most frequent table rows.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate partial claw hand from combined claw hand.</summary>

**Answer:** Partial claw hand (4th and 5th fingers) is an ulnar nerve lesion; combined claw hand with some ape hand is a lower trunk (C8–T1) lesion affecting both ulnar and median intrinsics.

**Why:** Number of nerves involved.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which deformities are produced by median nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Ape-like hand and benediction hand.

**Why:** Two rows of the table share the same nerve.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Upper trunk = C5–C6 (Erb's, policeman's tip); lower trunk = C8–T1 (Klumpke, combined claw)** — don't swap them.
- **Erb's palsy sensory loss is lateral; Klumpke's is medial.**
- **Axillary nerve** loses abduction **15°–110°**; the **first 15°** belong to the **suprascapular nerve** (supraspinatus).
- **Suprascapular and long thoracic nerves have no sensory loss**; the axillary nerve does (shoulder cap).
- **Horner's syndrome** points to **pre-ganglionic (T1) avulsion**, which is **not** repairable; post-ganglionic injuries are.
- **Winged scapula = long thoracic nerve** (serratus anterior), not the axillary nerve.

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. **Erb's point:** C5 + C6 rami, suprascapular, nerve to subclavius, 2 divisions (six nerves).
2. **Upper trunk (C5–C6) → Erb's:** policeman's tip, lateral sensory loss; causes: fall with head/shoulder separation, obstructed labour.
3. **Lower trunk (C8–T1) → Klumpke:** combined claw + ape hand, medial sensory loss; causes: sudden upward pull, shoulder dystocia.
4. **Horner's (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis)** = pre-ganglionic T1 avulsion; post-ganglionic = repairable.
5. **Axillary (C5–C6):** surgical neck fracture / inferior dislocation; deltoid + teres minor; flat shoulder; shoulder-cap sensory loss.
6. **Suprascapular (C5–C6):** notch entrapment; first 15° abduction + external rotation; no sensory loss.
7. **Long thoracic (C5–C7):** mastectomy; serratus anterior; winged scapula.
8. **Table:** axillary = flat shoulder, radial = wrist drop, ulnar = partial claw, median = ape/benediction, long thoracic = winging.
