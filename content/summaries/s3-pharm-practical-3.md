# Pharmacology Practical 3: Drug Absorption (Quiz 2) — Exam Night Review

The third pharmacology practical (pharmacokinetics — **absorption**), built around **Clinical problem No. 6: migraine treated with paracetamol + metoclopramide**. **Quiz no. two = 10 marks:** clinical scenario and relevance **2**, pharmacological questions **4**, patient's guide **3**, correct spelling of drug names **1**. After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

> **The case:** a **25-year-old woman** with repeated **migraine** gets **no relief after two paracetamol 500 mg tablets**. The physician prescribes **metoclopramide 10 mg half an hour before paracetamol**.

---

## 1. Clinical relevance: migraine and gastroparesis

| Feature | **Migraine** | **Tension-type headache** |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | **Throbbing / pulsating** | Constant, dull, **pressure-like** |
| Side | Usually **unilateral**, **can be bilateral** (common in children) | — |
| Intensity | **Moderate to severe**, disabling | — |
| Duration | **4–72 hours** if untreated | — |
| Associated | **Nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia** | Less common |
| Activity | **Aggravated** by routine activity (stairs) | Little effect |

- **Aura** in about **25–30%**: develops over **5–20 min**, lasts **< 60 min** — **visual** (flickering lights, zigzag lines, blind spots) is most common, also sensory ("pins and needles") and speech disturbances.
- **Prodrome** (hours to 1–2 days before): mood change, yawning, fatigue, food craving or loss of appetite, **neck stiffness**, increased urination.
- **Triggers:** light and sound make it worse; foods (**aged cheese, processed meats, chocolate, MSG, alcohol/red wine**), hormonal changes, stress, sleep change, skipped meals, strong smells, weather.
- **GI manifestations:** **nausea (commonest), vomiting**, abdominal discomfort, loss of appetite, occasionally diarrhea, and ⭐ **gastroparesis** (delayed gastric emptying) → **impaired absorption of oral drugs**. Another cause of gastroparesis: **diabetes mellitus** (vagus nerve damage).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Migraine = throbbing, 4–72 h, nausea, photophobia/phonophobia, worse with activity; may be bilateral.
- Aura in 25–30%; prodrome precedes by hours/days.
- Gastroparesis delays absorption of oral drugs (also seen in diabetes).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does migraine differ from tension-type headache?</summary>

**Answer:** Migraine is throbbing, moderate to severe, lasts 4–72 hours, is aggravated by routine activity and is accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia; tension headache is a constant dull pressure-like ache with little effect from activity and fewer associated symptoms.

**Why:** First question of the clinical relevance section.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is an aura and what are prodromal symptoms?</summary>

**Answer:** An aura is a transient neurological disturbance (mostly visual, also sensory or speech) in 25–30% of patients, developing over 5–20 minutes and lasting less than an hour. Prodromal symptoms (mood change, yawning, food cravings, neck stiffness, increased urination) appear hours to a day or two before the headache and are not the same as an aura.

**Why:** Often confused.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is gastroparesis important in this case?</summary>

**Answer:** Migraine slows gastric emptying, so oral paracetamol stays in the stomach and is poorly absorbed in the small intestine; this is why the patient got no relief. Diabetes mellitus is another cause of gastroparesis.

**Why:** Links the clinical story to absorption.
</details>

## 2. Q1: where and why drugs are absorbed

- ⭐ The **main site of absorption** is the **small intestine (duodenum and jejunum)**.
- Acidic drugs are mostly **unionized in the acidic stomach**, so absorption *begins* there, but the stomach is **not the main site** because:
  - its **surface area is small**,
  - the **intestine has a huge surface area (villi and microvilli)**,
  - **gastric transit is short**, while **intestinal transit is longer and more consistent**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Small intestine = main site (surface area, long transit).
- Stomach: favourable pH for acidic drugs but small area and short contact.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the main site of drug absorption in the GIT?</summary>

**Answer:** The small intestine, mainly the duodenum and jejunum.

**Why:** Direct recall.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the stomach not the main absorption site even for acidic drugs?</summary>

**Answer:** Although the acidic pH favours the unionized form, the stomach has a small surface area and a short transit time, while the small intestine has villi and microvilli giving a very large surface and a longer, more consistent transit.

**Why:** Surface area and time.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which structures give the intestine its large surface area?</summary>

**Answer:** Villi and microvilli.

**Why:** Key anatomical reason.
</details>

## 3. Q2: factors affecting absorption, bioavailability

| Group | Factors |
|---|---|
| **Drug physicochemical** | Lipid solubility, **degree of ionization (pKa and pH)**, molecular size, dissolution rate |
| **Patient / physiological** | GI pH, surface area, **blood flow**, **gastric emptying time**, intestinal motility, **food** |
| **Dosage form** | Solution, tablet, capsule, **sustained-release** |
| **First-pass metabolism** | Liver and gut wall metabolism before reaching the circulation |

- ⭐ **Bioavailability (F)** = the **fraction of the administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged**.
- **F = AUC oral ÷ AUC IV** (AUC = area under the plasma concentration–time curve); **IV bioavailability = 100%**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four groups of factors: drug, patient, dosage form, first-pass.
- F = AUC(oral)/AUC(IV); IV = 100%.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the factors that affect drug absorption from the GIT.</summary>

**Answer:** Physicochemical properties (lipid solubility, ionization, molecular size, dissolution), physiological factors (GI pH, surface area, blood flow, gastric emptying, motility, food), dosage form (solution, tablet, capsule, sustained release) and first-pass metabolism.

**Why:** Grouped answer is easier to remember.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define bioavailability and show how it is calculated.</summary>

**Answer:** The fraction (percentage) of the administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. F = AUC after oral dose ÷ AUC after IV dose, because the IV route has 100% bioavailability.

**Why:** Definition + formula.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is first-pass metabolism?</summary>

**Answer:** Metabolism of the drug in the liver and gut wall before it reaches the systemic circulation, which lowers the bioavailability of oral drugs.

**Why:** One of the four factors.
</details>

## 4. Q3: gastric emptying and food

- **Increase gastric emptying (prokinetics):** **metoclopramide, domperidone, erythromycin**.
- **Delay gastric emptying:** **opioids** (morphine, codeine), **anticholinergics** (atropine), **tricyclic antidepressants** (amitriptyline).
- **Food can DECREASE absorption** — take on an **empty stomach** (≥ **1 h before** or ≥ **2 h after** a meal): e.g. **ampicillin, tetracyclines** (chelation with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺/Al³⁺ in milk and antacids → insoluble complexes).
- **Food can INCREASE absorption** — take with food: **fat-soluble drugs** such as **griseofulvin and isotretinoin** (a high-fat meal may double or triple absorption).
- ⭐ **Grapefruit juice** inhibits intestinal **CYP3A4** → higher levels and toxicity of some **statins (atorvastatin, simvastatin)** and **calcium channel blockers (felodipine, nifedipine)**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Prokinetics: metoclopramide, domperidone, erythromycin; delayers: opioids, anticholinergics, TCAs.
- Empty stomach = 1 h before / 2 h after a meal.
- Grapefruit → CYP3A4 inhibition.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give examples of drugs that increase and decrease gastric emptying.</summary>

**Answer:** Increase: metoclopramide, domperidone, erythromycin. Decrease: opioids (morphine, codeine), anticholinergics (atropine) and tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline).

**Why:** Drug lists asked directly.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does food affect drug absorption? Give examples.</summary>

**Answer:** It can decrease absorption (ampicillin, tetracyclines chelated by calcium in milk, so take on an empty stomach) or increase it (fat-soluble griseofulvin and isotretinoin with a fatty meal). Grapefruit juice inhibits intestinal CYP3A4 and raises levels of some statins and calcium channel blockers.

**Why:** Slide Q6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does "empty stomach" mean?</summary>

**Answer:** One hour or more before a meal, or two hours or more after a meal.

**Why:** Exact definition used on the slide.
</details>

## 5. Q4: pharmacology of paracetamol and metoclopramide

### Paracetamol (acetaminophen)
- **Centrally acting analgesic and antipyretic**; believed to **inhibit COX (COX-3) in the CNS**, reducing prostaglandins; **minimal peripheral anti-inflammatory effect**.

### Metoclopramide (Primperan)
- ⭐ **In migraine — dual mechanism:**
  1. **D2 receptor antagonist** in the **chemoreceptor trigger zone** → **antiemetic** (nausea/vomiting).
  2. **Prokinetic** → faster gastric emptying, so **co-administered paracetamol/NSAIDs** move from the sluggish stomach into the small intestine and are **absorbed better**.
- **Why "prokinetic"?** It stimulates motility of the upper GI tract by **antagonizing inhibitory dopamine receptors** (dopamine inhibits ACh release) and **enhancing acetylcholine release**, which acts on muscarinic receptors (an **indirect** effect, not a muscarinic agonist).
- **Side effects:** ⭐ **extrapyramidal symptoms** (dystonia, parkinsonism, akathisia), **drowsiness/fatigue**, **hyperprolactinemia** (galactorrhea, menstrual irregularity, infertility), diarrhea.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Paracetamol: central COX inhibition; weak anti-inflammatory.
- Metoclopramide: D2 blocker (antiemetic) + prokinetic (↑ACh release).
- Main side effects: EPS, drowsiness, hyperprolactinemia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does metoclopramide help a migraine patient who does not respond to paracetamol?</summary>

**Answer:** It blocks D2 receptors in the CTZ, relieving nausea and vomiting, and speeds gastric emptying (prokinetic), so paracetamol reaches the small intestine and is absorbed properly despite migraine-related gastroparesis. It is given 30 minutes before paracetamol.

**Why:** Core of the case.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is metoclopramide called a prokinetic drug?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it stimulates upper GI motility: it blocks inhibitory dopamine receptors in the gut and enhances acetylcholine release, which stimulates muscarinic receptors (indirect action).

**Why:** Mechanism of the term.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the main side effects of metoclopramide.</summary>

**Answer:** Extrapyramidal symptoms (dystonia, parkinsonism, akathisia), drowsiness and fatigue, hyperprolactinemia (galactorrhea, menstrual disturbance, infertility) and diarrhea.

**Why:** Due to central D2 blockade.
</details>

## 6. Q5: transport mechanisms across membranes

| | **Passive diffusion** | **Facilitated diffusion** | **Active transport** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction | **Down** the gradient | **Down** the gradient | **Against** the gradient |
| Carrier | **No** | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Energy (ATP) | No | No | **Yes** |
| Saturable | No | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Selectivity | Low | High | High |
| Example | **Most drugs, e.g. paracetamol** | **Glucose via GLUT** | **Levodopa** via amino acid transporters |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Passive: no carrier, no energy, not saturable.
- Facilitated: carrier, no energy, saturable.
- Active: carrier + ATP, against gradient, saturable.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare passive, facilitated and active transport.</summary>

**Answer:** Passive diffusion goes down the gradient with no carrier or energy and is not saturable. Facilitated diffusion goes down the gradient with a carrier, needs no energy and is saturable. Active transport goes against the gradient with a carrier and ATP and is saturable.

**Why:** Table from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give an example of each mechanism.</summary>

**Answer:** Passive: paracetamol (most drugs). Facilitated: glucose through GLUT transporters. Active: levodopa into the brain via amino acid transporters.

**Why:** Examples are asked with the comparison.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which transport types are saturable?</summary>

**Answer:** Facilitated diffusion and active transport (both depend on carriers).

**Why:** Passive diffusion is not saturable.
</details>

## 7. Patient's guide

### Paracetamol
1. **Dose correctly:** 500–1000 mg every **4–6 hours**; **never more than 8 × 500 mg (4 g) a day** — more, or high doses for long, can **damage the liver**.
2. **Check labels** of other medicines (cold, flu, sinus, joint products) for **paracetamol/acetaminophen** — easy to exceed the dose unknowingly.
3. **Avoid alcohol** (both damage the liver).
4. Take **with or without food** (with food if it upsets the stomach).

### Metoclopramide
1. Relieves **nausea and vomiting** and helps the stomach **empty faster**.
2. Take about **30 minutes before meals and at bedtime**.
3. **Stop and seek urgent help** for **involuntary movements, parkinsonism-like symptoms or restlessness (akathisia)** — risk higher at the extremes of age, with longer use and higher total doses.
4. May cause **drowsiness** (careful when driving/operating machinery) and **diarrhea**.

### Formulations to know (names only, no brands or doses)
- **Paracetamol:** IV solution, drops, syrup, suspension, tablet, suppository.
- **Metoclopramide:** tablet, syrup, injection, suppository.
- Spell correctly: **paracetamol (acetaminophen)** and **metoclopramide**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Paracetamol max 4 g/day; avoid alcohol; check other products.
- Metoclopramide: 30 min before meals; stop if movement disorders.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What would you tell the patient about paracetamol?</summary>

**Answer:** Take 500–1000 mg every 4–6 hours and never more than 4 g (8 × 500 mg tablets) in a day; check other medicines for hidden paracetamol; avoid alcohol; it can be taken with or without food (with food if it upsets the stomach); overdose damages the liver.

**Why:** Patient's guide = 3 marks.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What would you tell the patient about metoclopramide?</summary>

**Answer:** It relieves nausea and vomiting and helps the stomach empty; take it about 30 minutes before meals and at bedtime; stop and seek urgent help if involuntary movements, parkinsonism-like symptoms or restlessness occur; it can cause drowsiness and diarrhea.

**Why:** Includes the red-flag side effects.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why must the patient check the labels of other medicines?</summary>

**Answer:** Many over-the-counter cold, flu, sinus and joint medicines also contain paracetamol, so the patient may exceed the safe daily dose without realizing it.

**Why:** Common cause of accidental overdose.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Main absorption site = small intestine**, not the stomach.
- **F = AUC oral ÷ AUC IV** (not the reverse); **IV = 100%**.
- **Metoclopramide is a D2 *antagonist* and prokinetic** — it is *not* a muscarinic agonist; it works by **enhancing ACh release**.
- **Opioids, anticholinergics and TCAs delay** gastric emptying; **metoclopramide, domperidone, erythromycin speed it**.
- **Facilitated diffusion needs no ATP**; **active transport does**.
- **Paracetamol max = 4 g/day**; avoid alcohol.
- **Tetracyclines + milk/antacids = chelation**; **grapefruit = CYP3A4 inhibition**.
- Migraine **can be bilateral**.
