## 1. Morphologic patterns of acute inflammation

| Pattern | Exudate / definition | Key points & examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Serous** | outpouring of **watery, relatively protein-poor fluid** | **not infected**, **few leukocytes**; in body cavities the fluid comes from **plasma (↑ permeability)** or **mesothelial secretions (irritation)**; collection in a cavity = **effusion**; **skin blister of a burn or viral infection** |
| **Fibrinous** | **severe injury → ↑ permeability** lets **large molecules (fibrinogen)** pass | outcomes: **resolution** (fibrinolysis + macrophages clear debris) **or organization and scarring** (ingrowth of fibroblasts and vessels); typical in the **lining of body cavities: meninges, pericardium** |
| **Suppurative (purulent) / abscess** | large amounts of **pus = neutrophils + necrotic cells + edema fluid** | caused by **pyogenic** organisms (e.g. **staphylococci**); **abscess = localized collection of pus buried in a tissue, organ or confined space**, produced by **seeding of pyogenic bacteria** |
| **Ulcer** | **local defect/excavation of a surface** from **necrosis and sloughing** of necrotic and inflammatory tissue | only when **necrosis + inflammation are on or near a surface**; **mucosa of mouth, stomach, intestine, GU tract**; **skin/subcutis of the lower limbs in older people with ischemic necrosis**; e.g. **peptic ulcer** |

- **Effusions in non-inflammatory conditions:** **↓ blood outflow in heart failure**; **↓ plasma proteins in kidney and liver disease**.
- **Ulcer: acute and chronic inflammation coexist** — **acute stage:** intense **PMN infiltration** and **vascular dilation** at the margins; **with chronicity:** **scarring of the margins and base** with **lymphocytes, macrophages and plasma cells**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Serous = watery, protein-poor, not infected (effusion, burn blister); fibrinous = fibrinogen leak, meninges/pericardium, resolves or scars.
- Suppurative = pus (neutrophils + necrotic cells + edema), pyogenic staphylococci, abscess = localized pus.
- Ulcer = surface defect from necrosis and sloughing, needs necrosis near a surface; peptic ulcer = acute + chronic features.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe serous inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** It is the outpouring of a watery, relatively protein-poor fluid that is typically not infected and has few leukocytes. In body cavities the fluid derives from plasma (increased permeability) or mesothelial secretions (local irritation), and its accumulation is an effusion; a burn or viral skin blister is a classic example. Effusions also occur in non-inflammatory conditions such as heart failure and kidney or liver disease with low plasma proteins.

**Why:** Slides "Serous inflammation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare fibrinous and suppurative inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** Fibrinous inflammation follows more severe injury with greater permeability so that fibrinogen leaks out; the exudate either resolves by fibrinolysis and macrophage clearance or is organized by fibroblasts and vessels, leaving a scar, and is typical of the meninges and pericardium. Suppurative inflammation produces pus (neutrophils, necrotic cells, edema fluid), usually from pyogenic organisms such as staphylococci, and when localized in a tissue it forms an abscess.

**Why:** Patterns II and III.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define an ulcer and give its features.</summary>

**Answer:** An ulcer is a local defect of the surface of an organ or tissue produced by necrosis and sloughing of necrotic and inflammatory tissue; it occurs only when necrosis and inflammation are on or near a surface (mucosa of the mouth, stomach, intestine, genitourinary tract, or the skin of the lower limbs in older people with ischemia). In a peptic ulcer, acute inflammation (PMNs, vascular dilation) coexists with chronic features (scarring, lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells).

**Why:** Slide "An ulcer".
</details>

## 2. Effects and outcomes of acute inflammation

| Beneficial effects | Harmful effects |
|---|---|
| **dilution of toxins** by edema fluid | **swelling and edema** — e.g. **acute epiglottitis** (laryngeal edema, congestion and narrowing of the laryngeal orifice, may be life-threatening) |
| **production of protective antibodies** and promotion of immunity | **↑ tissue pressure** contributing to **tissue necrosis** |
| **fibrin meshwork** — scaffold for cell migration and **limits spread of infection** | **digestion of adjacent viable tissue** |
| **cell nutrition** | **severe damaging allergic reaction**; **generalized ↑ permeability → shock (anaphylaxis)** |

### Three outcomes
1. ⭐ **Complete resolution** — injury **limited or short-lived**, **little tissue destruction**, damaged parenchymal cells **can regenerate**.
2. ⭐ **Healing by fibrosis** — **extensive tissue destruction**, tissues **incapable of regeneration**, or **abundant fibrin exudation**.
3. ⭐ **Progression to chronic inflammation** — the response persists because the **injurious agent persists** or **healing is interfered with**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Beneficial: dilution of toxins, antibodies, fibrin scaffold that limits spread, nutrition.
- Harmful: edema (epiglottitis), ↑ tissue pressure/necrosis, digestion of tissue, allergic damage, shock.
- Outcomes: resolution, fibrosis, chronic inflammation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the beneficial and harmful effects of acute inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** Beneficial: dilution of toxins by edema fluid, production of protective antibodies and immunity, a fibrin meshwork that supports cell migration and limits infection, and cell nutrition. Harmful: swelling and edema (e.g. life-threatening epiglottitis), rise in tissue pressure causing necrosis, digestion of adjacent viable tissue, severe allergic reactions, and shock from generalized increased permeability (anaphylaxis).

**Why:** Slide "Effects of acute inflammation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Mention the three outcomes of acute inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** (1) Complete resolution when the injury is limited, tissue destruction is small and cells can regenerate; (2) healing by fibrosis after extensive destruction, in non-regenerating tissues or with abundant fibrin exudation; (3) progression to chronic inflammation when the agent persists or healing is interfered with.

**Why:** Slide "Outcomes of acute inflammation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When does acute inflammation become chronic?</summary>

**Answer:** When the acute response persists because of persistence of the injurious agent or some interference with the normal healing process.

**Why:** Third outcome.
</details>

## 3. Chronic inflammation: causes and features

- **Chronic inflammation** underlies tissue damage in **rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, tuberculosis and chronic lung diseases**.
- It often starts **from the outset as an insidious, low-grade response (chronic inflammation ab initio)**, or **complicates acute inflammation, almost always the suppurative type (pus, abscess)**.

### Causes of chronic inflammation ab initio
1. **Persistent infections** — **tubercle bacilli, *Treponema pallidum*, certain viruses, fungi, parasites**: **low toxicity**, evoke **delayed hypersensitivity**, sometimes a specific pattern = **granulomatous inflammation**.
2. **Prolonged exposure to toxic agents** — **exogenous** (**inhaled silica**) or **endogenous** (**toxic plasma lipids → atherosclerosis**, a chronic inflammatory process of the arterial wall).
3. **Hypersensitivity diseases.**

### Morphologic features
1. **Infiltration with mononuclear cells:** **macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells**.
2. **Tissue destruction** by the **persistent agent or the inflammatory cells**.
3. **Attempts at healing by fibrosis** — **angiogenesis** and **fibroblast** proliferation.
- Example in lung: chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate + **destruction of normal alveoli** + **fibrosis**; compare **acute bronchopneumonia** with **neutrophils filling the alveoli** and congested vessels. **Chronic cholecystitis** shows a **fibrotic thickened wall**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Chronic: RA, atherosclerosis, TB, chronic lung disease; ab initio or after suppurative acute inflammation.
- Causes: persistent low-toxicity infection (delayed hypersensitivity), prolonged toxic exposure (silica, lipids), hypersensitivity.
- Features: mononuclear infiltrate, tissue destruction, fibrosis (angiogenesis + fibroblasts).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Mention the causes of chronic inflammation ab initio.</summary>

**Answer:** Persistent infections by low-toxicity organisms (tubercle bacilli, Treponema pallidum, some viruses, fungi, parasites) that evoke delayed hypersensitivity and may produce granulomatous inflammation; prolonged exposure to exogenous (silica) or endogenous (toxic plasma lipids in atherosclerosis) toxic agents; and hypersensitivity diseases.

**Why:** Slides "Causes of chronic inflammation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the morphologic features of chronic inflammation?</summary>

**Answer:** Infiltration with mononuclear cells (macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells); tissue destruction by the persistent agent or the inflammatory cells; and attempts at healing by fibrosis through angiogenesis and fibroblast proliferation.

**Why:** Slide "Morphologic features".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does chronic inflammation of the lung differ from acute bronchopneumonia?</summary>

**Answer:** In chronic inflammation there is a chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate, destruction of normal alveoli and replacement by fibrosis, whereas in acute bronchopneumonia the alveolar spaces and vessels are filled with neutrophils and congestion.

**Why:** Lung figures.
</details>

## 4. Cells of chronic inflammation

### Macrophage — the dominant cell
- Tissue forms: **Kupffer cells (liver)**, **sinus histiocytes (spleen, lymph nodes)**, **alveolar macrophages (lung)**.
- **Blood monocyte half-life ≈ 1 day**; **tissue macrophages live months to years**; two activation pathways: **classical** and **alternative**.
- Persistence is due to **recruitment of monocytes**, **local proliferation**, and **immobilization** at the site.
- **Products causing tissue injury:** **toxic O₂ species, NO, proteases**; **chemoattractants**; **growth factors** → **fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, angiogenesis**; **cytokines (TNF, IL-1, chemokines)**.
- Macrophages **present antigens to T cells** and respond to their signals → a **feedback loop** that sustains chronic inflammation.

### Other cells
| Cell | Role |
|---|---|
| **Lymphocytes** | interact **bidirectionally** with macrophages; **activated T cells make IFN-γ** (the **major macrophage activator**); **CD4⁺ subsets:** **TH1 → IFN-γ → classical macrophage activation; TH2 → IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 → eosinophils, allergy, alternative activation; TH17 → IL-17 → chemokines that recruit neutrophils** |
| **Plasma cells** | from **activated B cells**; **antibodies against persistent antigen** |
| **Eosinophils** | **IgE-mediated reactions and parasitic infections**; recruited by **eotaxin**; granules contain **major basic protein (toxic to parasites)** |
| **Mast cells** | widely distributed in connective tissue; **IgE receptor on the surface**; in acute and chronic reactions; cytokines may contribute to **fibrosis** |
| **Neutrophils** | persist with **persistent microbes** or **macrophage/T-cell mediators**; e.g. **chronic osteomyelitis (months)**, **smoking-induced lung damage** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Macrophage = dominant cell; Kupffer (liver), sinus histiocytes (spleen/LN), alveolar (lung); monocyte 1 day, tissue macrophage months–years.
- IFN-γ (TH1) → classical activation; IL-4/5/13 (TH2) → eosinophils, alternative; IL-17 (TH17) → neutrophils.
- Plasma cells = antibodies; eosinophils = eotaxin + major basic protein; mast cells = IgE; neutrophils persist in osteomyelitis and smoking.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the role of the macrophage in chronic inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** It is the dominant cell (Kupffer cells, sinus histiocytes, alveolar macrophages) derived from monocytes that live about a day in blood and months to years in tissue. Macrophages accumulate by recruitment, local proliferation and immobilization; they present antigen to T cells and secrete cytokines (TNF, IL-1, chemokines), toxic oxygen species, NO, proteases and growth factors that cause tissue injury but also fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition and angiogenesis, forming a feedback loop with T cells.

**Why:** Slides "Mononuclear cell infiltration — macrophage".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the three CD4⁺ T-cell subsets.</summary>

**Answer:** TH1 cells produce IFN-γ, which activates macrophages by the classical pathway; TH2 cells secrete IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13, recruiting and activating eosinophils (parasites, allergy) and driving the alternative macrophage pathway; TH17 cells secrete IL-17, inducing chemokines that recruit neutrophils.

**Why:** Slide "Lymphocytes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the roles of plasma cells, eosinophils, mast cells and neutrophils.</summary>

**Answer:** Plasma cells (from activated B cells) make antibody against the persistent antigen. Eosinophils appear in IgE-mediated and parasitic reactions, are recruited by eotaxin and contain major basic protein toxic to parasites. Mast cells bear IgE receptors, take part in acute and chronic reactions and may make cytokines contributing to fibrosis. Neutrophils persist with microbes or macrophage/T-cell mediators, e.g. chronic osteomyelitis and smoking-induced lung damage.

**Why:** Slides IV–VI.
</details>

## 5. Granulomatous inflammation

- **Granulomatous inflammation** = a distinctive chronic pattern with **focal accumulations of activated macrophages that develop an epithelioid appearance**.
- **Causes:** **tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, cat-scratch disease, lymphogranuloma inguinale, leprosy, brucellosis, syphilis, fungal infections, berylliosis, reactions to irritant lipids**.
- ⭐ **Granuloma** = **microscopic aggregation of macrophages transformed into epithelioid cells, surrounded by a collar of mononuclear leukocytes (mainly lymphocytes, occasionally plasma cells)**.
- **Epithelioid cells:** **pale pink granular cytoplasm, indistinct borders, oval or elongate vesicular nucleus**; **older granulomas get a rim of fibroblasts and connective tissue**.
- **Giant cells** form by **fusion of epithelioid cells** (**40–50 µm**, **≥ 20 nuclei**): **Langhans type = nuclei peripheral**; **foreign body type = nuclei haphazard**.

### Caseating vs non-caseating
| | **Caseating** | **Non-caseating** |
|---|---|---|
| Disease | **tuberculosis** (*M. tuberculosis*) | **Crohn disease, sarcoidosis, foreign body reactions** |
| Center | **caseous necrosis**: **granular, cheesy** grossly; **amorphous, structureless, eosinophilic granular debris** microscopically | no necrotic center |
- **Healing of granulomas → fibrosis, possibly extensive.**
- **Typical TB granuloma:** central **caseation** surrounded by **epithelioid cells, multinucleated giant cells, lymphocytes and fibroblasts**.

### Types
| | **Foreign body granuloma** | **Immune granuloma** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | material **too large to be phagocytosed by one macrophage**: **talc, sutures, fibers** | **insoluble, poorly degradable particles — typically microbes** that induce a **cell-mediated immune response** |
| Findings | epithelioid and giant cells **around/including the foreign body**, **refractile under polarized light** (talc crystals in IV drug users; suture strand) | macrophages **process the agent and present it to T cells**; T cells release **IL-2** (activates other T cells) and **IFN-γ** (activates macrophages → **epithelioid cells and giant cells**) |
| Example | suture or talc granuloma | **TB — the tubercle** with **central caseous necrosis** |
- **Identifying the agent:** **acid-fast stain, culture, PCR (TB); serology (syphilis)**; in **sarcoidosis the cause is unknown — diagnosis by exclusion** (non-caseating epithelioid granulomas with occasional Langhans giant cells and lymphocytes).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Granuloma = epithelioid macrophages + lymphocyte collar (+ giant cells); caseating in TB, non-caseating in sarcoid/Crohn/foreign body.
- Langhans = peripheral nuclei; foreign body type = haphazard nuclei; giant cell 40–50 µm.
- Foreign body: talc, sutures, fibers, polarized light; immune: T cells → IL-2 + IFN-γ → epithelioid cells; tubercle = TB.
- Sarcoidosis: unknown cause, diagnosis by exclusion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define granuloma and describe its cellular composition.</summary>

**Answer:** A granuloma is a focus of chronic inflammation made of a microscopic aggregation of macrophages transformed into epithelioid cells (pale pink granular cytoplasm, vesicular nucleus) surrounded by a collar of lymphocytes and occasional plasma cells; epithelioid cells may fuse into giant cells (Langhans or foreign-body type), and older granulomas have a fibroblast rim.

**Why:** Slides "Granuloma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate caseating from non-caseating granulomas.</summary>

**Answer:** Caseating granulomas (e.g. tuberculosis) have a central zone of caseous necrosis — grossly granular and cheesy, microscopically amorphous eosinophilic granular debris. Non-caseating granulomas (Crohn disease, sarcoidosis, foreign body reactions) lack a necrotic center.

**Why:** Slide "Granuloma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate foreign body from immune granulomas and state the role of IL-2 and IFN-γ.</summary>

**Answer:** Foreign body granulomas form around material that a single macrophage cannot phagocytose (talc, sutures, fibers), the material is seen in the center and is refractile in polarized light. Immune granulomas are caused by poorly degradable microbes that induce a cell-mediated response: macrophages present the antigen to T cells, which release IL-2 (activates other T cells) and IFN-γ (activates macrophages and transforms them into epithelioid and giant cells); TB is the typical example.

**Why:** Slides "Foreign body granulomas / Immune granulomas".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Serous = protein-poor and not infected; fibrinous = fibrinogen (more severe injury); suppurative = pus (neutrophils).**
- **Fibrinous inflammation: lining of body cavities (meninges, pericardium).**
- **Ulcer needs necrosis on or near a surface; abscess is buried pus.**
- **Outcomes of acute inflammation: resolution, fibrosis, chronic inflammation — chronic when the agent persists or healing is impaired.**
- **Blood monocyte half-life ≈ 1 day; tissue macrophage = months to years.**
- **TH1 → IFN-γ (classical); TH2 → IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 (eosinophils, alternative); TH17 → IL-17 (neutrophils).**
- **Eosinophils: eotaxin + major basic protein; mast cells: IgE receptor; plasma cells: antibodies.**
- **Langhans giant cell = peripheral nuclei; foreign body giant cell = haphazard nuclei.**
- **Caseating = TB; non-caseating = Crohn, sarcoidosis, foreign body.**
- **IL-2 activates T cells; IFN-γ activates macrophages (→ epithelioid and giant cells).**
- **Sarcoidosis: cause unknown, diagnosis of exclusion.**
