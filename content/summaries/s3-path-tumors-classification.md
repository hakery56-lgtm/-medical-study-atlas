## 1. Definitions, benign vs malignant, parts of a tumor

- ⭐ **Neoplasm** = **new growth** (= tumor, swelling); **oncology** = study of neoplasms. A neoplasm is an **abnormal proliferation exceeding normal tissue that continues after the cause is removed**, with a degree of **autonomy**.
- **Cancer** = genetic disorder from **DNA mutations** (mutagens or spontaneous with aging); alterations are **heritable**; cells with a growth advantage **outcompete neighbors**; together the properties are the **cancer hallmarks**. Cancer is the **2nd leading cause of death** in the Western world.
- **Benign** = innocent, **localized, removable, patients survive**. **Malignant** = **invades, destroys, metastasizes, causes death**; malignant tumors = **cancers**. The division is a **judgment of clinical behavior**.
- **Two components of every tumor:** **parenchyma** (neoplastic cells) and **stroma** (host connective tissue, blood vessels, inflammatory cells).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Neoplasm = growth persisting after the cause is removed; autonomous.
- Benign = localized; malignant = invades and metastasizes.
- Parenchyma = neoplastic cells; stroma = host support.

</div>

## 2. Benign tumor nomenclature

- Benign tumors: **suffix -oma** on the cell of origin. **Mesenchymal:** **fibroma** (fibroblasts), **chondroma** (cartilage; lobules of benign chondrocytes), **osteoma** (osteoblasts).
- **Benign epithelial** tumors are named by **cell of origin** and **micro/macroscopic appearance**:

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Adenoma** | Benign epithelial tumor forming **glandular structures** (also used for glandular tumors without glands) | **Thyroid follicular adenoma** (colloid-filled follicles, thin fibrous capsule) |
| **Papilloma** | **Finger-like (warty) projections** from epithelial surfaces | **Squamous cell papilloma** of skin and larynx |
| **Cystadenoma** | Adenoma with **large cystic space(s)** | **Ovarian cystadenoma** (unilocular, thin-walled); **papillary** type has yellowish warty projections |
| **Polyp** | Benign mass **projecting above a mucosal surface** or skin | **Adenomatous polyp** of colon (closely packed glands) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Benign = -oma; epithelial ones: adenoma, papilloma, cystadenoma, polyp.
- Glandular tumors without glands are still called adenomas.

</div>

## 3. Malignant, mixed and germ cell tumors

- **Sarcoma** = malignant **mesenchymal** tumor: **fibrosarcoma, liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma**.
- **Carcinoma** = malignant **epithelial** tumor from **any germ layer** (epidermis = ectoderm; renal tubules = mesoderm; GI/respiratory lining = endoderm). **Adenocarcinoma** = glandular pattern; **squamous cell carcinoma** = recognizable squamous cells (skin, esophagus, cervix); **undifferentiated (anaplastic)** = origin not recognizable.
- ⭐ **Mixed tumor** (one cell line, divergent differentiation): **pleomorphic adenoma of the parotid**: glands/tubules in **myxoid stroma**, may show squamous nests, **cartilage and bone**; from **epithelial/myoepithelial cells**; presents as **preauricular swelling**; malignancy excluded **only microscopically**.
- **Lymphomas and leukemias** = hematopoietic cells. **Germ cell tumors** = **totipotent** cells; **teratoma** = tissues from **more than one germ layer** (skin, muscle/fat, gut epithelium); **mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst)** may contain **teeth**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sarcoma = mesenchymal; carcinoma = epithelial (any germ layer).
- Pleomorphic adenoma = epithelial + myxoid/cartilage; confirm benign only by microscopy.
- Teratoma = more than one germ layer (totipotent cells).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate sarcoma from carcinoma.</summary>

**Answer:** Sarcomas are malignant tumors of mesenchymal cells (e.g., fibrosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma); carcinomas are malignant tumors of epithelial cells from any germ layer (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma).

**Why:** Slide 12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe pleomorphic adenoma of the parotid.</summary>

**Answer:** A mixed tumor of epithelial/myoepithelial cells forming glands and tubules in a myxoid stroma, sometimes with squamous nests, cartilage or bone; presents as a preauricular swelling; diagnosis and exclusion of malignancy need microscopy.

**Why:** Slides 13 to 16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a teratoma?</summary>

**Answer:** A germ cell tumor from totipotent cells containing several cell types from more than one (usually all three) germ layers, e.g., skin, muscle and fat, gut epithelium; the mature ovarian type is a dermoid cyst that may contain teeth.

**Why:** Slides 13, 17.
</details>

## 4. Special nomenclature and the tumor table

- ⭐ **Malignant tumors that sound benign:** **lymphoma, mesothelioma, melanoma, seminoma, astrocytoma/glioma, hepatoma, blastomas** (immature or nervous tissue: **medulloblastoma, retinoblastoma, nephroblastoma**).
- **Non-tumors that sound like tumors:** **hamartoma** = disorganized growth of normal tissues of an organ from **faulty development** (lung hamartoma: cartilage, vessels, bronchial structures, lymphoid tissue); **choristoma** = **normal tissue in an abnormal location** (ectopic pancreas in stomach/duodenum/jejunum; adrenal rest under kidney capsule; Brunner's gland rest).

| Tissue | Benign | Malignant |
|---|---|---|
| Hematopoietic / lymphoid | — | **Leukemia, lymphoma** |
| Smooth / striated muscle | **Leiomyoma / rhabdomyoma** | **Leiomyosarcoma / rhabdomyosarcoma** |
| Stratified squamous | **Squamous cell papilloma** | **Squamous cell carcinoma** |
| Basal cells of skin | — | **Basal cell carcinoma** |
| Glands or ducts | **Adenoma, cystadenoma** | **Adenocarcinoma, cystadenocarcinoma** |
| Epithelial lining | **Papilloma** | **Papillary carcinoma** |
| Respiratory passages | **Bronchial adenoma** | **Bronchogenic carcinoma** |
| Neuroectoderm | **Nevus** | **Malignant melanoma** |
| Renal epithelium | **Renal tubular adenoma** | **Renal cell carcinoma** |
| Liver cells | **Liver cell adenoma** | **Hepatocellular carcinoma** |
| Transitional epithelium | **Transitional cell papilloma** | **Transitional cell carcinoma** |
| Placental trophoblast | **Hydatidiform mole** | **Choriocarcinoma** |
| Testicular germ cells | — | **Seminoma** |
| Salivary gland | **Pleomorphic adenoma** | **Malignant mixed tumor** |
| Breast / renal anlage | **Fibroadenoma** | **Malignant cystosarcoma phyllodes / Wilms tumor** |
| Totipotential cells | **Mature teratoma, dermoid cyst** | **Immature teratoma, teratocarcinoma** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Misnomers: lymphoma, mesothelioma, melanoma, seminoma, glioma, hepatoma, blastomas are malignant.
- Hamartoma = disorganized native tissue; choristoma = normal tissue in the wrong place.

</div>

## 5. Differentiation and anaplasia

- Malignant tumors differ from benign by **differentiation and anaplasia, local invasion, distant metastases**.
- ⭐ **Differentiation** = extent to which neoplastic cells **resemble comparable normal cells**. Grades: **well, moderately, poorly, undifferentiated (anaplastic)**.
- **Benign** = well differentiated (leiomyoma: interlacing bundles of benign smooth muscle). **Well-differentiated cancer** resembles normal cells (diagnosis can be difficult; e.g., squamous cell carcinoma with **intercellular bridges and keratin pearls**). **Poorly differentiated** barely resembles origin (focal resemblance only). **Anaplasia** = **total lack** of differentiation, primitive cells.
- **Anaplastic features:** **pleomorphism**; **hyperchromatism**; **high N/C ratio (up to 1:1 vs 1:4-1:6)**; variable nuclear shape; **coarse clumped chromatin**; **large nucleoli**; **frequent mitoses, atypical ones more important**; **loss of polarity**; **tumor giant cells**; **ischemic necrosis** (poor prognostic sign, outgrows blood supply). Normal nuclei are **vesicular**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Differentiation = resemblance to normal cells; anaplasia = none.
- Atypical mitoses matter more than mitotic count.
- Necrosis in a malignant tumor = aggressive, poor prognosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define differentiation and list its degrees.</summary>

**Answer:** The extent to which neoplastic cells resemble comparable normal cells; degrees are well, moderately, poorly differentiated and undifferentiated (anaplastic).

**Why:** Slide 26.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the morphologic features of anaplastic cells.</summary>

**Answer:** Pleomorphism, hyperchromatic enlarged nuclei with high N/C ratio, abnormal chromatin clumping, large nucleoli, frequent atypical mitoses, loss of polarity, tumor giant cells and ischemic necrosis.

## 6. Dysplasia and carcinoma in situ

- ⭐ **Dysplasia** = **disordered growth**, mainly in **epithelia** (cervix, skin, metaplastic bronchial mucosa): **pleomorphism and loss of orientation, hyperchromatic large nuclei, abundant mitoses** (usually **normal bipolar** but in **abnormal locations**).
- Graded **low-grade** and **high-grade**. **Severe dysplasia through the full epithelial thickness** = **carcinoma in situ** (pre-invasive). **Breaching the basement membrane** = **invasive carcinoma**.
- Cervix example: failure of normal differentiation, marked pleomorphism, mitoses extending toward the surface (abnormal and normal mitoses).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Dysplasia = disordered epithelial growth with normal-pattern mitoses.
- Full-thickness severe dysplasia = carcinoma in situ.
- Basement membrane breached = invasive.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define dysplasia and where it occurs.</summary>

**Answer:** Disordered growth seen principally in epithelial membranes such as cervical or skin squamous epithelium and metaplastic bronchial mucosa.

**Why:** Slide 36.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the features of dysplasia?</summary>

**Answer:** Pleomorphism with loss of orientation, hyperchromatic large nuclei and abundant mitoses that are usually normal in pattern but in abnormal locations.

**Why:** Slide 36.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is carcinoma in situ and when does it become invasive?</summary>

**Answer:** Severe dysplasia involving the whole epithelial thickness (pre-invasive neoplasm); it is invasive carcinoma once cells breach the basement membrane.

**Why:** Slides 36, 37.
</details>

## 7. Local invasion

- **Benign:** **slow growth**, **cohesive masses**, usually (not always) a **fibrous capsule** (compressed connective tissue), confined to the site of origin.
- **Malignant:** mostly **invasive**, penetrate and destroy tissue, **no capsule**; invasiveness makes **resection difficult**. **Fixation of a breast mass** suggests malignancy.
- **Breast carcinoma:** infiltrating, **hard, gritty white** area (**scirrhous**): **desmoplastic reaction** (abundant collagen) and focal **dystrophic calcification**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Benign: slow, cohesive, capsulated.
- Malignant: infiltrates, no capsule, hard to resect.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the local growth of a benign tumor.</summary>

**Answer:** Slow, cohesive, usually with a rim of compressed connective tissue (fibrous capsule); it stays at its site of origin without invading.

**Why:** Slide 38.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is surgical removal of cancers difficult?</summary>

**Answer:** Malignant tumors invade and destroy underlying tissue and lack a capsule.

## 8. Metastasis

- ⭐ **Metastasis** = spread to **physically discontinuous** sites; it **unequivocally marks malignancy**. More likely with **more anaplastic and larger** tumors.
- **Exceptions (rarely metastasize):** **most malignant gliomas** and **most basal cell carcinomas** (**rodent ulcer**: destructive, slow, mainly head and neck, sun exposure; early excision).
- **Three pathways:** **seeding** (body cavities; typical of **ovarian** cancer; more typical of carcinomas), **lymphatic** (carcinomas), **hematogenous** (sarcomas; carcinomas too).
- **Lymphatic:** lung to bronchial then tracheobronchial and hilar nodes; **breast** (upper outer quadrant) to **axillary** nodes, medial lesions to **internal mammary** nodes, then supra/infraclavicular. **Sentinel node** = first regional node (blue dye or radiotracer) used to avoid full axillary clearance.
- **Hematogenous:** follows venous drainage, **first capillary bed**; **liver and lungs** most common; **thyroid and prostate** carcinomas via **paravertebral plexus**; **renal cell carcinoma** grows up the **renal vein and IVC**, even to the right heart.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Metastasis = hallmark of malignancy; exceptions: gliomas, basal cell carcinoma.
- Carcinoma: lymphatic; sarcoma: hematogenous; ovary: seeding.
- Sentinel node biopsy guides treatment with less morbidity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define metastasis and name the exceptions.</summary>

**Answer:** Spread of tumor to physically discontinuous sites, which marks a tumor as malignant; most malignant gliomas and most basal cell carcinomas rarely metastasize.

**Why:** Slides 41, 42.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the pathways of spread with an example each.</summary>

**Answer:** Seeding in body cavities (ovarian cancer over the peritoneum), lymphatic spread (breast cancer to axillary nodes), hematogenous spread (liver and lungs; renal cell carcinoma up the IVC).

**Why:** Slides 43 to 46.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a sentinel lymph node and why is it used?</summary>

**Answer:** The first regional node receiving lymph from the primary tumor, found by blue dye or radiotracer; its biopsy shows the extent of spread and plans treatment while avoiding the morbidity of full axillary dissection.

**Why:** Slide 45.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Adenoma does not require visible glands** in some thyroid adenomas; the name follows the glandular tissue of origin.
- **Carcinoma = epithelial from ANY germ layer** (renal cell carcinoma is mesodermal but still a carcinoma).
- **Melanoma, lymphoma, seminoma, glioma, hepatoma, mesothelioma are malignant** despite the benign-sounding names.
- **Hamartoma is disorganized native tissue; choristoma is normal tissue in the wrong place.**
- **Dysplasia mitoses are usually normal but abnormally located; atypical mitoses mark malignancy.**
- **Basal cell carcinoma invades destructively but rarely metastasizes.**
- **Lymphatic spread favors carcinomas; hematogenous favors sarcomas.**
- **Note:** the slide table lists a dash for some benign counterparts (leukemia, lymphoma, seminoma, basal cell carcinoma), meaning no benign counterpart is given.
