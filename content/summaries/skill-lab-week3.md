## 1. Radial nerve: anatomy and injury sites

- **Objectives:** **(1) manage a patient with arm trauma, (2) examine the radial nerve, (3) prescribe analgesics**.
- **Radial nerve** = **posterior cord, C5, 6, 7, 8 (T1)**.

| Level | Muscles supplied |
|---|---|
| **Upper arm** | **triceps** |
| **In front of the elbow** | **brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus, brachialis**; the **posterior interosseous branch** (before the supinator tunnel) supplies **extensor carpi radialis brevis and part of supinator** |
| **Supinator tunnel** | **rest of the supinator** — lesions here may cause **local tenderness** |
| **After leaving the supinator** | **extensor digitorum communis, extensors digiti minimi and indicis, extensor carpi ulnaris, abductor pollicis longus, extensors pollicis longus and brevis** |

- **Sensory:** **superficial radial nerve → radial side of the back of the hand**; **posterior cutaneous branch → variable area of the back of the arm and forearm**.
- **Common injury sites:** **(A) axilla** (crutches, back of a chair = **"Saturday night" palsy**), **(B) mid-humerus** (**fractures, tourniquet palsy**), **(C) at/below the elbow** (**dislocation, Monteggia fracture, ganglions, surgical trauma**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Posterior cord, C5–T1; triceps in the arm; brachioradialis/ECRL/brachialis at the elbow; posterior interosseous nerve for the forearm extensors.
- Sensory: radial side of the dorsum of the hand (superficial radial) + posterior cutaneous branch.
- Injury sites: axilla (Saturday night palsy), mid-humerus (fracture), elbow region (dislocation, Monteggia).

</div>

## 2. Radial nerve: examination steps

1. **Inspect:** **obvious wrist drop?** **wasting of forearm muscles?** **wasting of the triceps → high (proximal) lesion**.
2. **Wrist and fingers extensors:** **elbow flexed, hand pronated**; support the wrist, ask the patient to **straighten the fingers then pull back the wrist**; judge strength with **counterpressure**.
3. **Triceps:** **extend the shoulder; patient extends the elbow against gravity, then against resistance.** **Weakness → mid-humeral level lesion or incomplete high lesion; total loss → high (plexus) lesion.**
4. **Brachioradialis:** weakness of the **elbow flexor** brachioradialis; **loss of triceps tendon jerk**.
5. **Sensation:** test the supplied areas (**dorsum of the hand**); **loss confined to the hand → lesion unlikely to be much proximal to the elbow**; analyze **motor + sensory deficits** to localize the lesion.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Wrist drop, wasting, wrist/finger extension against counterpressure, triceps against gravity then resistance, sensation of the dorsal hand.
- Triceps weak → mid-humeral or incomplete high lesion; all triceps activity lost → high (plexus) lesion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe how to examine the radial nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** Inspect for wrist drop and wasting of the forearm and triceps; test wrist and finger extensors with the elbow flexed and the hand pronated, asking the patient to straighten the fingers and pull back the wrist against counterpressure; test the triceps by extending the elbow against gravity then resistance; test brachioradialis and the triceps jerk; and test sensation over the dorsum of the hand.

**Why:** Figures 2.33–2.38.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What does triceps weakness suggest?</summary>

**Answer:** A lesion at mid-humeral level or an incomplete high lesion; loss of all triceps activity suggests a high (plexus) lesion.

**Why:** Figure 2.37.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does sensory loss confined to the hand suggest?</summary>

**Answer:** That the lesion is unlikely to be much proximal to the elbow.

**Why:** Figure 2.38.
</details>

## 3. Case scenario and humeral shaft fracture

- **Case:** **67-year-old woman, left arm pain for 3 hours** after **falling on an outstretched hand** in the garden; **sharp, severe (9/10), constant, progressive, worse with motion**; **swelling and bluish discoloration**; **hypertension × 10 years, ischemic heart disease × 3 years**; on **antihypertensive, antiplatelet and lipid-lowering drugs**; sister had **multiple myeloma**.
- **Examination:** **moderate swelling and extensive ecchymosis; arm held in flexion; mid-arm tenderness; painful, limited active shoulder movement; normal cervical spine and elbow; normal brachial and radial pulses; radial nerve palsy (cannot extend wrist and MCP joints, sensory loss over the snuffbox); median and ulnar nerves normal.**
- ⚠️ **Tutor point:** management **does not start by dealing directly with the fracture — follow ABCDE; passive movement is contraindicated; focus on the neurovascular bundle (radial nerve + deep brachial artery).**
- **Pathological anatomy:** fractures **above the deltoid insertion → proximal fragment adducted by pectoralis major**; **below → abducted by the deltoid**; **radial nerve injury is common, recovery is usual**.
- **Structures at risk:** proximal humerus — **brachial plexus, axillary nerve/artery, circumflex humeral arteries, arcuate artery**; **shaft — radial nerve, brachial artery, profunda brachii, median nerve**; distal — **radial, brachial artery, median, ulnar nerves**.
- **Analgesic question (65-year-old woman, HTN + ischemic heart disease, humerus shaft fracture):** *teaching point, not on the slide* — **paracetamol is the usual first-line choice; NSAIDs are best avoided** (hypertension, ischemic heart disease and antiplatelet therapy); stronger options (e.g. weak opioid) only if needed.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Fall on an outstretched hand → humeral shaft fracture + radial nerve palsy (wrist drop, snuffbox sensory loss).
- ABCDE first, no passive movement, check neurovascular status (radial nerve, profunda brachii).
- Above deltoid insertion: pectoralis major adducts the proximal fragment; below: deltoid abducts it.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How should the patient with arm trauma be managed first?</summary>

**Answer:** Not by dealing directly with the fracture: follow ABCDE, avoid passive movement (contraindicated) and examine the neurovascular bundle, especially the radial nerve and the deep brachial artery.

**Why:** "Dear tutors" slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the displacement of the proximal fragment in humeral shaft fractures.</summary>

**Answer:** Above the deltoid insertion the proximal fragment is adducted by pectoralis major; below the deltoid insertion it is abducted by the deltoid.

**Why:** Pathological anatomy slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which analgesic would suit a hypertensive patient with ischemic heart disease and a humeral shaft fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** Paracetamol as first line; avoid NSAIDs because of hypertension, ischemic heart disease and antiplatelet therapy (teaching point; the slide only poses the question).

**Why:** Objective 3.
</details>
