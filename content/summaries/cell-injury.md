# Cell Injury and Adaptation — Exam Night Review

This lecture is the foundation of pathology — almost everything later builds on it. We go through it from the first slide to the last, topic by topic. After each topic you get **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions. Answers are hidden: tap a question to reveal it, but try answering first.

## 1. What is pathology, and where does the story start?

**Pathology** literally means the study (**logos**) of suffering (**pathos**).

Every disease is studied from four angles — the "core of pathology":

1. **Cause (etiology)** — what caused it?
2. **Mechanisms of development (pathogenesis)** — how did it develop, step by step?
3. **Morphologic changes** — what structural alterations appear in cells and organs?
4. **Clinical significance** — what are the functional consequences for the patient?

The central storyline of the lecture:

- Cells constantly adjust their structure and function to changes in demand and external stress. This steady state of equilibrium is called **homeostasis**.
- When a cell meets **stress** — **physiological** or **pathological** — it undergoes **adaptation** to preserve its viability and function and reach a **new steady state**.
- If the adaptive capacity is exceeded, or the injury is **severe and prolonged**, **cell injury** results.
- Cell injury may be **reversible** (within limits, the cell returns to baseline) or **irreversible**, leading to **cell death**.

Think of it as a ladder: normal cell → stress → adaptation → if that fails → reversible injury → if it continues → irreversible injury → death.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pathology = study (logos) of suffering (pathos).
- Four aspects: etiology, pathogenesis, morphologic changes, clinical significance.
- Homeostasis = steady state; stress → adaptation → if exceeded → cell injury (reversible or irreversible → cell death).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Why:** These four are listed word for word in the lecture — a classic "enumerate" question. Keep them in order.
</details>

Under physiological or pathological stress the cell undergoes adaptation to preserve its viability and function and reach a new steady state; if the adaptive capacity is exceeded or the injury is severe and prolonged, cell injury results.

**Why:** The examiner wants the full sequence: homeostasis → adaptation → injury.
</details>

**Why:** The key difference is whether the cell can return to normal.
</details>

## 2. Reversible vs irreversible cell injury

1. **Reversible cell injury** — the changes **disappear** when the **injurious agent is removed**, and the cell returns to **normal function and morphology**.
2. **Irreversible cell injury** — when the injurious agent **persists**, or is **sudden and severe**, the changes do not return to normal and progress to **cell death**.

The deciding factors: was the cause removed in time, and how severe was it?

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Reversible: changes disappear when the agent is removed → normal function & morphology.
- Irreversible: agent persists or is sudden & severe → progresses to cell death.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate between reversible and irreversible cell injury.</summary>

**Answer:** In reversible injury the cellular changes disappear when the injurious agent is removed and the cell returns to normal function and morphology. In irreversible injury the agent persists, or is sudden and severe, so the changes do not return to normal and progress to cell death.

**Why:** This is the lecture's definition — note the words "persists" and "sudden and severe".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What two features of the injurious agent push a cell toward irreversible injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Persistence of the injurious agent, or an injury that is sudden and severe.

**Why:** Persistence or sudden severity prevents recovery.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What happens to a reversibly injured cell once the injurious agent is removed?</summary>

**Answer:** The changes disappear and the cell returns to its normal function and morphology (stable baseline state).

**Why:** That is exactly the definition of "reversible".
</details>

## 3. Causes of cell injury

The lecture lists eight causes — learn them as a list:

1. **Oxygen deprivation (hypoxia, ischemia)** — ⭐ **the most common cause**
2. **Chemical agents** — poisons, drugs, **carbon monoxide**, air pollution
3. **Infectious agents** — viruses, bacteria…
4. **Immunological reactions** — e.g. **anaphylaxis** and **autoimmune diseases**
5. **Genetic defects** — e.g. **Down syndrome**, **sickle cell anaemia**
6. **Nutritional imbalance** — **protein-calorie malnutrition**, specific vitamin deficiency, and also **obesity** (excess counts, not just deficiency!)
7. **Physical agents** — trauma, radiation, electric shock
8. **Aging**

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** "What is the most common cause of cell injury?" → **oxygen deprivation (hypoxia and ischemia)**. And remember that **obesity** is listed under nutritional imbalance.

- Most common cause: oxygen deprivation (hypoxia, ischemia).
- Others: chemical, infectious, immunological, genetic, nutritional (including obesity), physical agents, aging.

</div>

## 4. Mechanisms of cell injury (the big five)

This is the most important section of the lecture.

### 4.1 Depletion of ATP
When ATP falls **below 5–10% of normal**, many critical systems are affected:

- The plasma-membrane **sodium pump** (ouabain-sensitive **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase**) slows → sodium and water enter the cell.
- **Cellular energy metabolism** is altered.
- Failure of the **Ca²⁺ pump** → **influx of Ca²⁺**, which damages many cellular components.
- **Reduced protein synthesis**.
- In cells deprived of oxygen or glucose, proteins become **misfolded**, triggering the **unfolded protein response**, which may lead to injury and even death.

### 4.2 Mitochondrial damage
Mitochondria are **the target of most types of injury**; their integrity is critical for survival. They are damaged by:

1. **Increased intracellular Ca²⁺**
2. **Accumulation of free oxygen radicals**
3. **Breakdown of phospholipids by phospholipase**

### 4.3 Influx of calcium and loss of calcium homeostasis
Raised intracellular Ca²⁺ switches on destructive enzymes:

| Enzyme activated | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Phospholipases** | membrane damage |
| **Proteases** | degrade structural and membrane proteins |
| **ATPase** | further ATP depletion |
| **Endonucleases** | **fragmentation of genetic material** |

### 4.4 Oxygen-derived free radicals (oxidative stress)
- A free radical has a **single unpaired electron in an outer orbital** → extremely **unstable** and highly reactive.
- **Reactive oxygen species (ROS)** are normal by-products of **mitochondrial respiration** — **superoxide (O₂•⁻)**, **hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)**, **hydroxyl radical (OH•)** — normally removed by defence mechanisms.
- When production exceeds removal → **oxidative stress**.
- Seen in **ischemia-reperfusion injury**, chemical and radiation injury, O₂ toxicity, cellular aging, and tissue injury by inflammatory cells.
- **Three damaging reactions**:
  1. **Membrane lipid peroxidation**
  2. **DNA fragmentation**
  3. **Cross-linking of proteins**

### 4.5 Defects in membrane permeability
- **Direct damage:** bacterial toxins, viral proteins, complement components.
- **Indirect (secondary):** ↓**ATP synthesis** and **calcium-mediated phospholipase activity**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Calcium appears in several mechanisms — it damages mitochondria, activates enzymes, and causes membrane damage via phospholipase. For "role of calcium in cell injury", write the four-enzyme table.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five mechanisms: ATP depletion, mitochondrial damage, Ca²⁺ influx, free radicals (oxidative stress), membrane permeability defects.
- ATP < 5–10% → ↓Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, Ca²⁺ influx, ↓protein synthesis, unfolded protein response.
- Mitochondria damaged by ↑Ca²⁺, free radicals, phospholipase.
- Ca²⁺ activates phospholipases, proteases, ATPase, endonucleases.
- Free radicals → lipid peroxidation, DNA fragmentation, protein cross-linking.

</div>

**Answer:** When ATP falls below 5–10% of normal: reduced activity of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump; altered cellular energy metabolism; failure of the Ca²⁺ pump with influx of Ca²⁺ damaging cellular components; reduced protein synthesis; and in cells deprived of oxygen or glucose, misfolded proteins trigger the unfolded protein response, which may lead to injury and death.

**Why:** Five points straight from the lecture, plus the 5–10% figure examiners love.
</details>

</details>

**Answer:** Oxidative stress is the condition in which production of reactive oxygen species (by-products of mitochondrial respiration such as O₂•⁻, H₂O₂ and OH•) exceeds the cell's defence mechanisms. Free radicals injure cells by membrane lipid peroxidation, DNA fragmentation and cross-linking of proteins.

**Why:** Definition + three reactions is a classic short-essay question.
</details>

## 5. Factors affecting severity & morphology of reversible injury

### What determines how severe the injury is?
1. The **type, duration and severity** of the injurious agent.
2. The **type, state, adaptability and genetic make-up** of the cell. Key example: **neurons** are very susceptible (**3–5 minutes** of ischemia is enough), while **skeletal muscle** and **fibroblasts** tolerate **hours**.

### Light-microscopic patterns of reversible injury

**1) Cellular swelling** — ⭐ **the first manifestation of almost all forms of injury**
- **Grossly** (when all cells of an organ are affected): **pallor**, **increased turgor**, **increased weight**.
- **Microscopically**: **small clear cytoplasmic vacuoles** = **distended segments of endoplasmic reticulum** → called **hydropic change** or **vacuolar degeneration**.
- Example from the slides: **swollen hepatocytes in viral hepatitis**.

**2) Fatty change**
- Occurs in **hypoxic**, toxic and metabolic injury.
- Seen as **lipid vacuoles in the cytoplasm**.
- Mainly in **hepatocytes** and **myocardial cells**.

In the kidney images, early (reversible) ischemic injury shows **surface blebs**, **increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia** and swelling of some cells; necrosis shows **loss of nuclei**, fragmentation and leakage of contents.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Severity depends on the agent (type, duration, severity) and the cell (type, state, adaptability, genetics); neurons die in 3–5 min, skeletal muscle/fibroblasts need hours.
- Cellular swelling = first sign of almost all injury; hydropic change / vacuolar degeneration (distended ER).
- Fatty change: lipid vacuoles, mainly hepatocytes and myocardium.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What factors determine the severity of cell injury? Give an example.</summary>

**Answer:** 1) The type, duration and severity of the injurious agent. 2) The type, state, adaptability and genetic make-up of the affected cell — e.g. neurons are injured after 3–5 minutes of ischemia, whereas skeletal muscle and fibroblasts need hours.

**Why:** The neuron example is what makes the answer complete.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the gross and microscopic features of cellular swelling.</summary>

**Answer:** It is the first manifestation of almost all forms of injury. Grossly (when all cells of an organ are affected): pallor, increased turgor and increased weight. Microscopically: small clear cytoplasmic vacuoles representing distended segments of endoplasmic reticulum — hydropic change or vacuolar degeneration.

**Why:** Gross + microscopic + both alternative names.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is fatty change and where is it mainly seen?</summary>

**Answer:** A pattern of reversible injury seen in hypoxic, toxic and metabolic injury, appearing as lipid vacuoles in the cytoplasm, mainly in hepatocytes and myocardial cells.

**Why:** Name the three causes and the two organs.
</details>

## 6. Cellular adaptations — atrophy, hypertrophy, hyperplasia, metaplasia

Cells counter changes in their environment (physiological or pathological) with **adaptive responses**. There are four:

### 6.1 Atrophy
- **Shrinkage in cell size** caused by **loss of cell substance**; if enough cells are involved, the whole organ shrinks.
- ⭐ Atrophic cells are **not dead** (still viable).
- **Six causes:** 1) decreased workload (e.g. limb immobilization), 2) loss of innervation, 3) decreased blood supply, 4) inadequate nutrition, 5) loss of endocrine stimulation, 6) aging.
- **Lipofuscin**: a **finely granular yellow-brown pigment** in the cytoplasm (e.g. myocardial fibres) — **indigestible residues of autophagic vacuoles** formed during aging and atrophy.
- Example: **brain atrophy** (aging + atherosclerosis) → **narrowed gyri, widened sulci**.

### 6.2 Hypertrophy
- **Increase in cell size** → organ enlarges.
- Usually in **non-dividing cells**.
- Caused by **hormonal stimulation** or **increased functional demand**.
- **Physiologic**: skeletal muscle in weight lifters. **Pathologic**: **myocardial hypertrophy**.

### 6.3 Hyperplasia
- **Increase in the number of cells** (usually **dividing cells**) → organ enlarges.
- Hypertrophy and hyperplasia often occur together.
- **Physiological:**
  1. **Hormonal** — glandular proliferation of the **female breast in pregnancy**.
  2. **Compensatory** — when part of a tissue is removed or diseased.
- **Pathological:**
  1. **Excessive hormonal stimulation** — **endometrial hyperplasia**.
  2. **Growth-factor stimulation** — **skin wart** caused by **papilloma virus** (increased transcription factors).
- Drivers: hormonal stimulation, increased functional demand, chronic stress or injury.
- Example: hyperplasia of the cervical epithelium — more cells with **normal morphology**.

### 6.4 Metaplasia
- A **reversible** change in which one **differentiated cell type (epithelial or mesenchymal)** is replaced by another.
- **Most common**: **columnar → squamous** in the **respiratory tract** due to **chronic irritation**.
- **Squamous → columnar**: **Barrett oesophagus** — squamous epithelium replaced by **intestinal-like columnar cells** because of **refluxed gastric acid**.
- **Connective-tissue metaplasia**: cartilage, bone or fat forming where they don't belong — bone in muscle = **myositis ossificans**.
- **Mechanism**: **reprogramming of local tissue stem cells** or **colonization by differentiated cells from adjacent sites**.
- ⚠️ If persistent, it may **initiate malignant transformation**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Hypertrophy = **size**, **non-dividing** cells. Hyperplasia = **number**, **dividing** cells. The most common metaplasia is **columnar → squamous**; Barrett is the reverse (squamous → columnar).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Atrophy: ↓cell size, cells still viable; 6 causes; lipofuscin = yellow-brown wear-and-tear pigment.
- Hypertrophy: ↑cell size, non-dividing cells; physiologic (weight lifters) vs pathologic (myocardium).
- Hyperplasia: ↑cell number, dividing cells; physiologic (breast in pregnancy; compensatory) vs pathologic (endometrial hyperplasia; wart by papilloma virus).
- Metaplasia: reversible replacement of one differentiated cell type by another; columnar→squamous most common; Barrett = squamous→columnar; myositis ossificans; may become malignant.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define atrophy and list its causes.</summary>

**Answer:** Atrophy is shrinkage in cell size caused by loss of cell substance; when enough cells are involved the whole organ diminishes in size (atrophic cells are not dead). Causes: decreased workload, loss of innervation, decreased blood supply, inadequate nutrition, loss of endocrine stimulation, and aging.

**Why:** Don't forget "atrophied cells are not dead" — it distinguishes a full answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare hypertrophy and hyperplasia, with a physiological and pathological example of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Hypertrophy is an increase in cell size, usually in non-dividing cells (physiological: skeletal muscle in weight lifters; pathological: myocardial hypertrophy). Hyperplasia is an increase in cell number, usually in dividing cells (physiological: breast in pregnancy, compensatory hyperplasia; pathological: endometrial hyperplasia, skin wart by papilloma virus). Both enlarge the organ and often occur together.

**Why:** One of the most frequently repeated comparison questions.
</details>

**Answer:** Metaplasia is a reversible change in which one differentiated cell type (epithelial or mesenchymal) is replaced by another. Examples: columnar to squamous in the respiratory tract (chronic irritation) — the most common; squamous to columnar in Barrett oesophagus (gastric acid reflux); myositis ossificans (bone in muscle). It results from reprogramming of local stem cells or colonization from adjacent sites, and if persistent may initiate malignant transformation.

**Why:** The significance (malignant transformation) earns the last mark.
</details>

## 7. Irreversible cell injury & necrosis

### The point of no return
With persistent injury the cell passes a point of no return. There is no single definitive marker, but three phenomena are consistent:
1. **Inability to restore mitochondrial function**
2. **Loss of structure and function of the cell membrane**
3. **Loss of DNA and chromatin structural integrity**

Irreversible injury takes two forms: **necrosis** and **apoptosis**.

### Necrosis
- **Definition:** death of **groups of contiguous cells** in a tissue or organ.
- Causes considerable tissue dysfunction and is ⭐ **always pathologic**.
- Features: **denaturation of cellular proteins**, **leakage of cellular contents** through damaged membranes, **local inflammation**, **enzymatic digestion**.
- Leakage of intracellular proteins into the blood is the basis of tissue-specific blood tests. Best example: **cardiac troponins** appear in the blood as early as **2 hours** after myocardial necrosis — before histological evidence of infarction.

### Morphology of the necrotic cell

**1) Cytoplasmic changes**
- **Increased eosinophilia** — due to **loss of RNA (basophilia)** and increased eosin binding to denatured proteins.
- **Glassy, homogeneous** appearance — due to **loss of glycogen particles**.
- Later the cytoplasm becomes **vacuolated and "moth-eaten"** as lysosomal enzymes digest organelles.
- Finally **calcification** may follow; dead cells may be replaced by **myelin figures** (large whorled phospholipid masses).

**2) Nuclear changes** (three patterns, non-specific DNA breakdown)
- **Pyknosis**: nuclear **shrinkage** and **increased basophilia** (DNA condenses).
- **Karyorrhexis**: **fragmentation** of the pyknotic nucleus.
- **Karyolysis**: basophilia **fades** due to **DNase activity**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Remember the order: **pyknosis = shrink**, **karyorrhexis = fragment**, **karyolysis = fade**. Eosinophilia has two causes: loss of RNA + binding to denatured proteins.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Irreversibility: can't restore mitochondria, membrane damage, loss of DNA/chromatin integrity.
- Necrosis: death of contiguous cells, always pathologic, with inflammation; leakage is the basis of blood tests (troponin within 2 h).
- Cytoplasm: ↑eosinophilia, glassy, moth-eaten, calcification, myelin figures.
- Nucleus: pyknosis (shrink), karyorrhexis (fragment), karyolysis (fade, DNase).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What three phenomena characterize irreversible cell injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Inability to restore mitochondrial function; loss of structure and function of the cell membrane; and loss of DNA and chromatin structural integrity.

**Why:** These are the three the lecture calls "consistently characterized".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define necrosis and explain why troponin is useful clinically.</summary>

**Answer:** Necrosis is the death of groups of contiguous cells in a tissue or organ; it is always pathologic and features protein denaturation, leakage of cellular contents, local inflammation and enzymatic digestion. </details>

## 8. Types (patterns) of necrosis

| Type | Key feature | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Coagulative** | protein denaturation is primary; **structural outlines preserved**, nucleus lost; sudden severe ischemia | ⭐ **myocardial infarction**; **wedge-shaped** kidney infarct |
| **Gangrenous** | not a distinct form — a surgical term: coagulative necrosis of a **limb** ± superimposed bacterial infection | **dry gangrene** (frostbite, foot); **wet gangrene** (strangulated intestine, volvulus) |
| **Liquefactive** | **complete enzymatic digestion** → cyst filled with debris and fluid | **brain infarct**, **abscess** |
| **Fat** | action of **lipase** in adipose tissue; fatty acids + calcium = **calcium soaps (saponification)** → **chalky white** | **acute pancreatitis** (mesentery) |
| **Caseous** | **cheesy white**; microscopically **structureless amorphous pink debris** within **granulomatous inflammation**; **architecture completely obliterated** | **tuberculosis** (lung, lymph node) |
| **Fibrinoid** | immune complexes + leaked plasma proteins in vessel walls → **bright pink, amorphous** on H&E | immune reactions of vessels, e.g. **polyarteritis nodosa** |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** The golden contrast: **coagulative = architecture preserved**, **caseous = architecture completely obliterated**. The brain is always **liquefactive**, never coagulative.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Coagulative: preserved outlines, MI/kidney infarct.
- Gangrenous: surgical term, coagulative necrosis of a limb ± infection (dry vs wet).
- Liquefactive: brain infarcts and abscesses.
- Fat: lipase, saponification, chalky white — acute pancreatitis.
- Caseous: cheesy, TB, granuloma, architecture lost.
- Fibrinoid: immune complexes in vessel walls, bright pink.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare coagulative and caseous necrosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Coagulative necrosis: protein denaturation is the primary event, structural outlines of the cells are preserved while the nucleus is lost; caused by sudden severe ischemia (e.g. myocardial infarction, kidney). Caseous necrosis: cheesy white gross appearance, seen in the centre of tuberculous infection; microscopically structureless amorphous pink debris surrounded by granulomatous inflammation, and the tissue architecture is completely obliterated.

**Why:** Tissue architecture is the key to the comparison.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe fat necrosis and its gross appearance.</summary>

**Answer:** A specific pattern in adipose tissue caused by the action of lipase, most commonly in acute pancreatitis. Liberated fatty acids combine with calcium (saponification) to form calcium soaps, seen grossly as chalky white areas.

**Why:** Lipase + saponification + chalky white = the complete answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Where is liquefactive necrosis seen and what is its outcome? What is gangrenous necrosis?</summary>

**Answer:** Liquefactive necrosis occurs in brain infarcts and abscesses; dead cells are completely digested by enzymes and the area becomes a cyst filled with debris and fluid. Gangrenous necrosis is not a distinct form of cell death but a surgical term for coagulative necrosis of a limb, with superimposed bacterial infection in wet gangrene.

**Why:** "Not a distinctive form" is a frequent point.
</details>

## 9. Apoptosis

- **Definition:** **programmed cell death** — elimination of unwanted cells through a coordinated, **internally programmed** series of events driven by gene products (**internal suicidal program**), i.e. **cellular suicide**.
- It is **"the physiological way for a cell to die"**.

**Examples (physiological and pathological):**
1. **Embryogenesis** — shaping organs.
2. **Hormone-dependent involution** — e.g. endometrium in the menstrual cycle.
3. **Cell deletion in proliferating populations** — e.g. **intestinal crypt epithelium**.
4. **Deletion of autoreactive T cells in the thymus**.
5. **Mild injurious stimuli** (heat, radiation, drugs) causing **irreparable DNA damage**.

**Morphology:**
- Affects **single cells or small clusters**.
- Cells appear **round/oval** with **intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm**.
- **Condensed nuclear chromatin** aggregates under the nuclear membrane, then **karyorrhexis**.
- The cell **shrinks**, forms **cytoplasmic buds**, and fragments into **apoptotic bodies** → rapidly **phagocytosed**.

### Necrosis vs apoptosis (from the lecture)

| | Necrosis | Apoptosis |
|---|---|---|
| Cells involved | groups of contiguous cells | single cells / small clusters |
| Nature | always pathologic | physiological (and some pathological) |
| Inflammation | local inflammation | none — fragments are quickly phagocytosed |
| Mechanism | protein denaturation, leakage, enzymatic digestion | internal suicidal program |
| Cell size | swelling, leakage | shrinkage → apoptotic bodies |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Necrosis is **always** pathologic; apoptosis can be physiological or pathological. Karyorrhexis occurs in **both**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Apoptosis = programmed cell death (internal suicidal program), the physiological way to die.
- Examples: embryogenesis, endometrial involution, intestinal crypts, autoreactive T cells in thymus, irreparable DNA damage.
- Morphology: single cells, eosinophilic, condensed peripheral chromatin, karyorrhexis, shrinkage, apoptotic bodies, phagocytosis, no inflammation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define apoptosis and list its physiological and pathological examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Apoptosis is programmed cell death eliminating unwanted cells by activating a coordinated, internally programmed series of events (internal suicidal program). Examples: embryogenesis; hormone-dependent involution (endometrium in the menstrual cycle); cell deletion in proliferating populations (intestinal crypts); deletion of autoreactive T cells in the thymus; mild injurious stimuli causing irreparable DNA damage.

**Why:** Definition plus all five examples.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the morphology of apoptosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Affects single cells or clusters, which appear as round/oval masses with intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm and condensed chromatin aggregating at the periphery under the nuclear membrane, followed by karyorrhexis. The cell shrinks, forms cytoplasmic buds and fragments into apoptotic bodies that are rapidly phagocytosed.

**Why:** The sequence (condensation → karyorrhexis → shrinkage → apoptotic bodies → phagocytosis) is what's asked.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate between necrosis and apoptosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Necrosis involves groups of contiguous cells, is always pathologic, with protein denaturation, leakage of contents, enzymatic digestion and local inflammation. Apoptosis involves single cells, is often physiological (internal suicidal program), and the cell shrinks and forms apoptotic bodies that are phagocytosed without inflammation.

**Why:** A comparison that appears in almost every exam.
</details>
