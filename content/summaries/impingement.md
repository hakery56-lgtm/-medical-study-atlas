# Shoulder Impingement Syndrome (SAPS) — Exam Night Review

A clinical lecture: definition, anatomy, examination, imaging and treatment. The most important parts are the **special tests** and the differences between conditions that look alike. After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

## 1. Definition and the change in terminology

- **Shoulder impingement syndrome** is a **clinical syndrome** of **shoulder pain**, particularly on **elevation of the arm**, commonly involving the **rotator-cuff tendons** and/or the **subacromial bursa**.
- ⭐ Modern sources increasingly prefer **subacromial pain syndrome (SAPS)** or **rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain**, because the pain is **multifactorial** and cannot reliably be attributed to **mechanical compression** alone.
- As a result, management has shifted toward **exercise-based rehabilitation** rather than **decompression surgery**.

**Commonly involved structures:**
- **Supraspinatus tendon** (⭐ most often)
- **Infraspinatus tendon**
- **Subacromial bursa**
- **Long head of biceps tendon**
- Other components of the rotator cuff

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Impingement = shoulder pain especially on elevation, involving rotator-cuff tendons and/or subacromial bursa.
- Preferred modern terms: subacromial pain syndrome (SAPS) / rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain — pain is multifactorial, not just compression.
- Management shifted to exercise-based rehab rather than decompression surgery.

</div>

## 2. Anatomy & biomechanics

### Rotator cuff (four muscles)
| Muscle | Main action | Nerve |
|---|---|---|
| **Supraspinatus** | **initiates abduction** | **suprascapular nerve** |
| **Infraspinatus** | **external rotation** | **suprascapular nerve** |
| **Teres minor** | **external rotation** | **axillary nerve** |
| **Subscapularis** | **internal rotation** | **upper/lower subscapular nerves** |

- The **supraspinatus tendon** passes **beneath the acromion** before inserting on the **greater tuberosity** — which is why it is the most frequently involved.
- The **subacromial bursa** lies between the **rotator cuff** and the **acromion/deltoid**.

### Normal biomechanics
- Normal elevation needs coordinated movement of the **glenohumeral joint, scapula, clavicle and thoracic spine**.
- The **rotator cuff** keeps the **humeral head centred in the glenoid** while larger muscles generate movement.
- **Scapulohumeral rhythm** ≈ **2° glenohumeral : 1° scapular** during full elevation (the ratio varies through the range).
- Abnormal scapular movement, weakness or **altered motor control** can contribute to symptoms.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Supraspinatus (initiates abduction) & infraspinatus (ER) — suprascapular n.; teres minor (ER) — axillary n.; subscapularis (IR) — subscapular nn.
- Supraspinatus tendon passes under the acromion → greater tuberosity.
- Subacromial bursa lies between cuff and acromion/deltoid.
- Rotator cuff centres the humeral head; scapulohumeral rhythm ≈ 2:1.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Tabulate the rotator cuff muscles with their actions and nerve supply.</summary>

**Answer:** Supraspinatus — initiates abduction — suprascapular nerve. Infraspinatus — external rotation — suprascapular nerve. Teres minor — external rotation — axillary nerve. Subscapularis — internal rotation — upper and lower subscapular nerves.

**Why:** The nerve table is a direct question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the scapulohumeral rhythm?</summary>

**Answer:** The coordinated movement of the glenohumeral joint and scapula during elevation: approximately 2° of glenohumeral movement for every 1° of scapular movement during full elevation, although the ratio varies through the range.

**Why:** The 2:1 ratio is what's asked.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the supraspinatus most frequently involved?</summary>

**Answer:** Its tendon passes beneath the acromion before inserting on the greater tuberosity, so it is frequently involved in rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain.

**Why:** The anatomical course is the reason.
</details>

## 3. Pathophysiology & risk factors

### The traditional model
The rotator cuff (especially **supraspinatus**) is compressed between the **humeral head ↔ acromion/coracoacromial arch** during elevation.

### Modern understanding (more complex)
Contributors:
- **Tendon overload** and **reduced tendon capacity**
- **Age-related tendon degeneration**
- **Altered load management**
- **Rotator-cuff weakness**
- **Scapular motor-control abnormalities**
- **Thoracic posture/movement**
- **Subacromial bursal nociception**
- **Central/peripheral pain sensitization**
- **Occupational or sporting repetitive loading**

⭐ Bottom line: **shoulder pain ≠ simply a narrow subacromial space**.

### Risk factors
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Occupational** | repetitive overhead work, manual labour, heavy lifting, repetitive reaching |
| **Sporting** | swimming, volleyball, tennis, baseball, throwing sports, weightlifting |
| **Patient-related** | increasing age, **smoking**, **diabetes**, metabolic disease, poor conditioning, previous injury |
| **Biomechanical** | rotator-cuff weakness, **scapular dyskinesis**, reduced thoracic mobility, repetitive overhead loading |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Old model: cuff compressed between humeral head and acromion/coracoacromial arch.
- Modern: multifactorial — overload, degeneration, weakness, scapular control, posture, bursal nociception, pain sensitization.
- Shoulder pain ≠ simply a narrow subacromial space.
- Risks: overhead work/sports, age, smoking, diabetes, scapular dyskinesis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** Traditionally, the rotator cuff (especially supraspinatus) was thought to be compressed between the humeral head and the acromion/coracoacromial arch during elevation. Modern understanding is multifactorial: tendon overload, reduced tendon capacity, age-related degeneration, altered load management, cuff weakness, scapular motor-control abnormalities, thoracic posture, subacromial bursal nociception, pain sensitization and repetitive loading — so pain is not simply a narrow subacromial space.

**Why:** Group them into four categories so nothing is forgotten.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What biomechanical factors contribute to shoulder impingement?</summary>

**Answer:** Rotator-cuff weakness, scapular dyskinesis, reduced thoracic mobility and repetitive overhead loading.

**Why:** Four short points.
</details>

## 4. Clinical presentation, history & red flags

### The typical patient
A **middle-aged adult** with **gradually progressive** shoulder pain, worse with **overhead activity**.

- **Site**: **anterolateral shoulder**, **lateral upper arm/deltoid region**, sometimes anterior.
- May radiate **toward the elbow** but ⭐ **generally not below the elbow**.
- **Aggravated by**: reaching overhead, dressing, **combing hair**, reaching behind the back, lifting, **sleeping on the affected side**.
- **Night pain**: common and may disturb sleep.

### History — ask about
- **Onset**: acute or gradual? trauma? repetitive activity?
- **Site**: anterior / lateral / superior / posterior?
- **Character**: mechanical, constant, burning, neuropathic?
- **Functional limitation**: can the patient dress, comb hair, reach a shelf, reach behind the back, lift objects?
- **Associated symptoms**: weakness, neck pain, numbness, paraesthesia, instability, fever, weight loss.

### 🚩 Red flags — don't automatically diagnose impingement
Significant trauma, **acute inability to actively elevate the arm**, severe unremitting pain, fever, constitutional symptoms, history of malignancy, neurological deficit, vascular symptoms, deformity, suspected fracture/dislocation, **significant true weakness**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Typical: middle-aged, gradual pain, worse overhead; anterolateral shoulder/deltoid region; does not go below the elbow; night pain.
- Aggravated by overhead reaching, dressing, combing hair, reaching behind back, sleeping on that side.
- Red flags: trauma, acute inability to elevate arm, fever, malignancy, neuro deficit, deformity, true weakness.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** A middle-aged adult with gradually progressive shoulder pain worse with overhead activity, located in the anterolateral shoulder or lateral upper arm/deltoid region, sometimes radiating toward but not below the elbow; aggravated by reaching overhead, dressing, combing hair, reaching behind the back, lifting and lying on the affected side; night pain is common.

**Why:** A full description of the typical patient.
</details>

</details>

**Answer:** Onset (acute/gradual, trauma, repetitive activity), site, character (mechanical, constant, burning, neuropathic), functional limitation (dressing, combing hair, reaching a shelf or behind the back, lifting) and associated symptoms (weakness, neck pain, numbness, paraesthesia, instability, fever, weight loss).

**Why:** The history structure from the lecture.

## 5. Physical examination & special tests

### Step 1 — Inspection
Muscle wasting, asymmetry, **scapular winging**, swelling, deformity, scars — **and compare both sides**.

### Range of movement
- Assess **active** and **passive** ROM (flexion, extension, abduction, IR, ER).
- ⭐ **Key distinction**: in **impingement**, **passive ROM is usually preserved**; in **adhesive capsulitis**, **active and passive ROM are both restricted**.

### Special tests
| Test | Technique | Positive / meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Painful arc** | active abduction | **pain at 60–120°**, improving beyond → subacromial pain (**not pathognomonic**) |
| **Neer** | **stabilize the scapula, internally rotate, passively elevate forward** | reproduces the patient's pain → subacromial pain (not proof of mechanical impingement) |
| **Hawkins–Kennedy** | **shoulder flexed 90° + elbow 90°**, examiner **internally rotates** | pain reproduced → supports subacromial pain |
| **Jobe / empty-can** | **90° abduction, slight forward flexion, thumb down**, resist downward pressure | tests **supraspinatus**: **pain only** → tendinopathy; **marked weakness** → **cuff tear** |
| **External rotation test** | compare ER strength both sides | **infraspinatus + teres minor**; marked weakness → structural pathology |
| **Lift-off** | hand behind the back, lift away | **subscapularis** |
| **Belly-press** | press palm into abdomen, elbow held forward | **subscapularis** |

### Tests for the differential
- **AC joint**: **cross-body adduction test** (pain localized to the AC joint).
- **Biceps**: **Speed test**, **Yergason test**.
- **Cervical spine**: neck + neurological exam + **Spurling-type testing** — **cervical radiculopathy may mimic shoulder disease**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** All impingement tests (Neer, Hawkins) **support** subacromial pain but **don't prove** a specific lesion. Empty-can: **pain = tendinopathy**, **weakness = tear**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Passive ROM preserved in impingement; restricted in adhesive capsulitis.
- Painful arc 60–120°; Neer (scapula stabilized, IR, passive forward elevation); Hawkins–Kennedy (90°/90°, IR).
- Empty-can (supraspinatus): pain → tendinopathy, weakness → tear. ER test: infraspinatus/teres minor. Lift-off & belly-press: subscapularis.
- Cross-body adduction: AC joint. Speed/Yergason: biceps. Examine the neck (radiculopathy mimics).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the Neer and Hawkins–Kennedy tests and their interpretation.</summary>

**Answer:** Neer: stabilize the scapula, internally rotate the arm and passively elevate it forward; reproduction of the patient's familiar pain is positive. Hawkins–Kennedy: shoulder and elbow flexed to about 90°, then the examiner internally rotates the shoulder; reproduction of pain is positive. Both support subacromial pain but are not proof of a specific mechanical lesion.

**Why:** Technique + the correct interpretation ("supporting", not "proof").
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the Jobe (empty-can) test and how to interpret pain versus weakness.</summary>

**Answer:** It assesses the supraspinatus: shoulder at about 90° abduction with slight forward flexion and thumb down while the patient resists downward pressure. Pain only may reflect tendinopathy/subacromial pain; marked weakness raises suspicion of a rotator-cuff tear.

**Why:** Pain vs weakness is the core of the question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is the subscapularis examined?</summary>

**Answer:** By the lift-off test (the patient places the hand behind the back and tries to lift it away) and the belly-press test (the patient presses the palm into the abdomen while maintaining elbow position).

**Why:** Both test names + technique.
</details>

## 6. Differential diagnosis (key comparisons)

### Impingement vs rotator-cuff tear — ⭐ "a major postgraduate examination issue"
| Feature | Subacromial pain | Rotator-cuff tear |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | common | common |
| Painful arc | common | may occur |
| Passive ROM | usually preserved | usually preserved initially |
| Active elevation | painful | **may be markedly impaired** |
| True weakness | usually limited by pain | **often prominent** |
| Trauma | not necessary | may be present |
| Atrophy | usually absent | may occur |
| **Drop-arm** | usually negative | **may be positive** |

⭐ **Key point**: a patient who **suddenly cannot actively elevate the arm after trauma** should be assessed for an **acute rotator-cuff tear**, not labelled "impingement".

### Impingement vs adhesive capsulitis
| Feature | Impingement | Adhesive capsulitis |
|---|---|---|
| Active ROM | painful | **restricted** |
| **Passive ROM** | **usually preserved** | **restricted** |
| **External rotation** | usually preserved | **markedly restricted** |
| **Diabetes** | possible | **strong association** |

- Classic adhesive capsulitis pattern: **external rotation > abduction > internal rotation** restriction.

### Other differentials (from the objectives)
Rotator-cuff tear, adhesive capsulitis, **cervical radiculopathy**, **AC-joint disease**, **glenohumeral arthritis**, **calcific tendinopathy**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cuff tear: marked active-elevation impairment, prominent true weakness, trauma, atrophy, positive drop-arm.
- Acute traumatic inability to elevate → assess for acute cuff tear, not "impingement".
- Adhesive capsulitis: active AND passive ROM restricted, ER markedly restricted, strong diabetes link; ER > abduction > IR.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate subacromial pain syndrome from a rotator-cuff tear.</summary>

**Answer:** Both cause pain and passive ROM is usually preserved (initially in tears). In a tear, active elevation may be markedly impaired, true weakness is often prominent, trauma may be present, atrophy may occur and the drop-arm test may be positive; in subacromial pain, weakness is usually limited by pain, trauma is not necessary, atrophy is absent and drop-arm is negative. Sudden inability to elevate the arm after trauma should be assessed as an acute cuff tear.

**Why:** The lecturer calls this "a major examination issue".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate impingement from adhesive capsulitis.</summary>

**Answer:** In impingement, active ROM is painful but passive ROM and external rotation are usually preserved. In adhesive capsulitis, both active and passive ROM are restricted, external rotation is markedly restricted, there is a strong association with diabetes, and the classic pattern of restriction is external rotation > abduction > internal rotation.

**Why:** Passive ROM is the key.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the differential diagnoses of subacromial pain syndrome.</summary>

**Answer:** Rotator-cuff tear, adhesive capsulitis, cervical radiculopathy, AC-joint disease, glenohumeral arthritis and calcific tendinopathy.

**Why:** Six diagnoses from the learning objectives.
</details>

## 7. Imaging

Imaging **complements** clinical assessment — it doesn't replace it.

| Modality | When | Shows / advantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Plain X-ray** | trauma, persistent symptoms, suspected arthritis or **calcific tendinopathy**, older patient with unexplained symptoms, alternative diagnosis | AC arthritis, glenohumeral OA, **calcific deposits**, fracture, **acromial morphology**, **high-riding humeral head** (advanced cuff disease) |
| **Ultrasound** | cuff tears, tendinopathy, bursitis, biceps, **dynamic assessment** | **no radiation**, **inexpensive**, **dynamic**, compares both shoulders; **operator-dependent** |
| **MRI** | significant weakness (tear?), trauma, failed conservative care, surgery planned, uncertain diagnosis, intra-articular pathology | tendinopathy, partial/full tear, **atrophy**, **fatty infiltration**, bursitis, labral and AC pathology |

⭐ **Important postgraduate point**: MRI abnormalities are **common, especially with age** → **"Do not treat the MRI; treat the patient"** — correlate imaging with symptoms and examination.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- X-ray: trauma, persistent symptoms, arthritis, calcific tendinopathy; shows calcific deposits, acromial morphology, high-riding humeral head.
- Ultrasound: tears, tendinopathy, bursitis; no radiation, cheap, dynamic; operator-dependent.
- MRI: weakness/trauma/failed conservative care/surgery planning; shows tears, atrophy, fatty infiltration.
- MRI abnormalities are common with age — treat the patient, not the MRI.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the indications and findings of plain X-ray in shoulder pain?</summary>

**Answer:** Indications: trauma, persistent symptoms, suspected arthritis or calcific tendinopathy, older patient with unexplained symptoms, suspected alternative diagnosis. Findings: AC arthritis, glenohumeral OA, calcific deposits, fracture, acromial morphology and a high-riding humeral head in advanced cuff disease.

**Why:** Two lists: when, and what it shows.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the advantages of ultrasound in shoulder assessment?</summary>

**Answer:** It is very useful for cuff tears, tendinopathy, bursitis and biceps pathology; it has no radiation, is relatively inexpensive, allows dynamic assessment and comparison of both shoulders; however, operator experience matters.

**Why:** Advantages plus its one drawback.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain the statement "Do not treat the MRI; treat the patient".</summary>

**Answer:** MRI abnormalities (such as tendinopathy or partial tears) are common, especially with increasing age, even without symptoms; therefore imaging findings must be correlated with the patient's symptoms and examination before deciding treatment.

**Why:** An explicit postgraduate point in the lecture.
</details>

## 8. Management

### Treatment hierarchy (in order)
**Education + activity modification → exercise-based rehabilitation → analgesia/NSAID when appropriate → corticosteroid injection (selected patients) → reassess diagnosis & adherence → specialist if persistent/structural**

### Education
- The condition is **usually not dangerous**.
- **Pain doesn't necessarily mean progressive structural damage**.
- **Complete rest is generally unnecessary**; **gradual loading** is important.
- Recovery may take **weeks to months**; exercise adherence is central.

### Activity modification
Temporarily avoid repetitive painful overhead lifting and heavy loads, **but avoid prolonged immobilization**. The objective: ⭐ **"Modify load, not eliminate movement."**

### Exercise therapy — ⭐ **the cornerstone**
- **Rotator cuff**: ER and IR strengthening, supraspinatus loading, progressive resistance.
- **Scapular muscles**: **serratus anterior**, **middle and lower trapezius**, **rhomboids**.
- **Mobility**: posterior shoulder, thoracic mobility, stretching.
- **Progression**: load → repetitions → resistance → functional activity.

### Analgesics
- **Paracetamol** when appropriate.
- **NSAIDs**: **short-term** benefit; consider contraindications — **peptic ulcer, CKD, heart failure, significant CVD, anticoagulation, NSAID hypersensitivity**. ⭐ **NSAIDs support rehabilitation rather than replace it**.

### Corticosteroid injection
- Considered when pain is substantial and **prevents rehabilitation**.
- Benefit: **rapid short-term relief** — an **adjunct**, not definitive treatment.
- ⭐ One trial: injection + exercise was better short-term, but **at 12 weeks there was no significant difference** vs exercise alone.
- Be careful with **repeated injections**, especially with a **cuff tear** or possible surgery.

### Physiotherapy modalities
Manual therapy, taping, education, mobility work — but the core stays **progressive exercise**; evidence for passive modalities is weaker.

### Surgery
- Historically: **arthroscopic subacromial decompression / acromioplasty**.
- ⭐ Current evidence **does not support routine decompression** for uncomplicated SAPS. A **BMJ guideline strongly recommended against it** because trials showed no clinically important improvement in pain, function or quality of life vs non-operative/placebo approaches.

### When to refer
Acute traumatic weakness, suspected **full-thickness cuff tear**, persistent severe symptoms despite rehab, **progressive weakness**, significant disability, diagnostic uncertainty, suspected cervical/neurological disease, suspected arthritis/structural disease.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hierarchy: education + activity modification → exercise → analgesia → injection (selected) → reassess → specialist.
- "Modify load, not eliminate movement"; complete rest unnecessary.
- Exercise is the cornerstone: rotator cuff + scapular muscles (serratus anterior, middle/lower trapezius, rhomboids) + mobility.
- NSAIDs short-term; they support, not replace, rehab.
- Steroid injection: short-term adjunct; no difference vs exercise alone at 12 weeks.
- Routine subacromial decompression NOT recommended (BMJ guideline).

</div>

## 9. Practical algorithm

1. **Trauma?** → exclude fracture/dislocation/acute cuff tear.
2. **Red flags?** → investigate appropriately.
3. **Neck/neurological symptoms?** → examine the cervical spine and nervous system.
4. **Passive ROM restricted?** → think **adhesive capsulitis** or glenohumeral pathology.
5. **Passive ROM preserved + painful elevation?** → **rotator-cuff-related pain / SAPS**.
6. **Marked weakness?** → investigate for a **cuff tear**.
7. **No major structural concern** → education + progressive exercise ± analgesia.
8. **Severe pain prevents rehab** → selected subacromial **corticosteroid injection**.
9. **Persistent symptoms** → reassess diagnosis, adherence and loading; consider imaging/referral.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Trauma → exclude fracture/dislocation/tear; red flags → investigate; neck symptoms → examine spine.
- Passive ROM restricted → adhesive capsulitis/GH pathology; preserved + painful elevation → SAPS; marked weakness → cuff tear.
- Otherwise: education + exercise ± analgesia; injection if pain blocks rehab; reassess if persistent.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does passive range of motion guide diagnosis in shoulder pain?</summary>

**Answer:** Restricted passive ROM suggests adhesive capsulitis or glenohumeral pathology; preserved passive ROM with painful elevation suggests rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain/SAPS.

**Why:** Steps 4 and 5 of the algorithm.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. A patient has shoulder pain with marked weakness. What is the next step?</summary>

**Answer:** Investigate for a rotator-cuff tear (e.g. ultrasound or MRI) rather than treating it as simple impingement.

**Why:** Marked weakness = suspect a tear.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What should be done when symptoms persist despite treatment?</summary>

**Answer:** Reassess the diagnosis, exercise adherence and loading, and consider imaging and specialist referral.

**Why:** The last step of the algorithm.
</details>
