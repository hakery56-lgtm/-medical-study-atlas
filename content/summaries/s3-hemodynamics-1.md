## 1. Hyperemia and congestion

| | **Hyperemia** | **Congestion** |
|---|---|---|
| **Process** | **Active**: arteriolar dilation, increased inflow | **Passive**: impaired venous outflow |
| **Examples** | **Inflammation, exercising muscle** | **Cardiac failure** (systemic), **venous obstruction** (local) |
| **Color** | **Red** (oxygenated blood) | **Blue-red / cyanosis** (deoxygenated Hb) |

- **Chronic congestion** → hypoxia, **parenchymal cell death, fibrosis, edema, capillary rupture and focal hemorrhage.** Cut surfaces **feel wet and ooze blood.**
- **Pulmonary congestion** (↑ left atrial pressure: **left ventricular failure, mitral stenosis**): **acute** = engorged capillaries, septal edema, intra-alveolar hemorrhage; **chronic** = thickened fibrotic septa and **hemosiderin-laden macrophages ("heart failure cells").**
- **Hepatic congestion** (commonest cause: **right heart disease**): **acute** = distended central vein and sinusoids, **centrilobular necrosis**, periportal fatty change; **chronic** = **nutmeg liver** (red-brown depressed centers on tan fatty background), centrilobular necrosis, hemorrhage, hemosiderin macrophages; long-standing → **cardiac cirrhosis.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hyperemia = active, red; congestion = passive, blue-red.
- Lung: heart failure cells. Liver: nutmeg liver, cardiac cirrhosis.
- Chronic congestion → hypoxia, fibrosis, edema, hemorrhage.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Distinguish hyperemia from congestion.</summary>

**Answer:** Hyperemia is an active process of arteriolar dilation and increased inflow producing red tissue; congestion is a passive process of impaired venous outflow producing blue-red cyanotic tissue.

**Why:** Slides "Hyperemia and congestion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe chronic pulmonary congestion.</summary>

**Answer:** Thickened fibrotic septa and alveolar spaces containing hemosiderin-laden macrophages (heart failure cells), caused by raised left atrial pressure (left heart failure, mitral stenosis).

**Why:** Slide "Pulmonary congestion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is nutmeg liver?</summary>

**Answer:** Chronic passive hepatic congestion: red-brown, slightly depressed central lobular regions against tan fatty uncongested liver, with centrilobular necrosis, hemorrhage and hemosiderin macrophages.

**Why:** Slide "Hepatic congestion".
</details>

## 2. Edema

- ⭐ **Edema** = accumulation of **interstitial fluid**; effusions in cavities (**hydrothorax, hydropericardium, hydroperitoneum/ascites**); **anasarca** = severe generalized edema. Water = 60% of body weight (⅔ intracellular, ~5% plasma).
- **Starling forces:** **vascular hydrostatic pressure** vs **plasma colloid osmotic pressure.**

| Cause | Example |
|---|---|
| **↑ Hydrostatic pressure** | **Heart failure** |
| **↑ Vascular permeability** | **Inflammation** |
| **↓ Colloid osmotic pressure** (low albumin) | Decreased synthesis: **liver disease, protein malnutrition**; increased loss: **nephrotic syndrome** |
| **Lymphatic obstruction** | **Inflammation, neoplasia** |
| **Sodium retention** | **Renal failure** |

### Transudate versus exudate
| | **Transudate** | **Exudate** |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | ↑ hydrostatic / ↓ colloid pressure (non-inflammatory) | ↑ permeability (inflammation) |
| **Protein** | **Low** (< 1 g/dl) | **High** (2.5-3.5 g/dl) |
| **Cells / LDH** | Few cells; low LDH | Many cells; high LDH |
| **pH, glucose** | pH > 7.3; glucose = plasma | pH < 7.3; low glucose |
| **Example** | **Edema of heart failure** | **Pus** |

- **Morphology:** **subcutaneous** (dependent edema: legs when standing, sacrum when recumbent; **pitting**; **periorbital** in renal disease/nephrotic syndrome), **lungs** (2-3 × normal weight, frothy blood-tinged fluid), **brain** (narrow sulci, flattened gyri).
- **Clinical:** cardiac = gravitational pitting; renal = periorbital; pulmonary = dyspnea; brain = headache, vomiting, convulsion; serous cavities = hydrothorax, ascites; skin stretched and shiny.
- **Generalized:** cardiac, renal, famine, allergic. **Localized:** venous obstruction, lymphatic obstruction, inflammatory. **All pit except lymphatic obstruction (non-pitting).**
- Note: the slide text gives specific-gravity cut-offs of < 1.012 / > 1.020 while the table shows < 1.015 / > 1.018; learn the protein, cell and LDH differences instead.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five causes: ↑ hydrostatic, ↑ permeability, ↓ colloid osmotic, lymphatic obstruction, Na retention.
- Transudate = protein-poor; exudate = protein-rich with many cells.
- Lymphatic edema is non-pitting.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the five causes of edema with an example of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Increased hydrostatic pressure (heart failure), increased permeability (inflammation), reduced colloid osmotic pressure (liver disease, malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome), lymphatic obstruction (inflammation, neoplasia) and sodium retention (renal failure).

**Why:** Slide "Edema may be caused by".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare transudate and exudate.</summary>

**Answer:** Transudate: protein-poor, few cells, low LDH, pH > 7.3, non-inflammatory (e.g. cardiac failure). Exudate: protein-rich, many cells, high LDH, pH < 7.3, low glucose, due to inflammation (e.g. pus).

**Why:** Slide "Exudate vs Transudate".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the morphology of edema in subcutaneous tissue, lung and brain.</summary>

**Answer:** Subcutaneous: dependent, pitting, periorbital in renal disease. Lung: 2-3 times normal weight with frothy fluid. Brain: narrowed sulci and flattened gyri.

**Why:** Slides "Morphology of edema".
</details>

## 3. Hemorrhage

- ⭐ **Hemorrhage** = **extravasation of blood** from vessels: vessel damage or defective clotting. **Causes:** capillary bleeding in chronic congestion, trauma, atherosclerosis, inflammation, neoplastic erosion.
- **Hemorrhagic diatheses:** defects in **vessel wall, platelets or coagulation factors.**
- **Forms:** external; internal **hematoma** (bruise → fatal retroperitoneal); cavities: **hemothorax, hemopericardium, hemoperitoneum, hemarthrosis.** Extensive bleeding → **jaundice.**

| Term | Size | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Petechiae** | **1-2 mm** | **Thrombocytopenia, defective platelet function, vitamin C deficiency** |
| **Purpura** | **3-5 mm** | Same + **trauma, vasculitis, fragility** |
| **Ecchymoses** | **1-2 cm** | Subcutaneous hematomas (bruises) |

- **Bruise colors:** hemoglobin (red-blue) → **bilirubin (blue-green)** → **hemosiderin (golden-brown).**
- **Significance depends on** volume, rate, site (e.g. brain) and chronic/recurrent loss (peptic ulcer, menstrual).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Petechiae 1-2 mm < purpura 3-5 mm < ecchymoses 1-2 cm.
- Bruise: hemoglobin → bilirubin → hemosiderin.
- Importance = volume, rate, site, recurrence.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define petechiae, purpura and ecchymoses.</summary>

**Answer:** Hemorrhages of 1-2 mm (petechiae), 3-5 mm (purpura) and 1-2 cm (ecchymoses, subcutaneous bruises).

**Why:** Slide "Petechiae, purpura, ecchymoses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the color changes of a bruise.</summary>

**Answer:** Macrophages convert hemoglobin (red-blue) to bilirubin (blue-green) and then hemosiderin (golden-brown).

**Why:** Same slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What determines the clinical significance of a hemorrhage?</summary>

**Answer:** The volume lost, the rate of bleeding, the site (e.g. brain) and chronic or recurrent blood loss.

**Why:** Slide "The clinical significance of any particular hemorrhage".
</details>

## 4. Thrombosis: Virchow's triad

- ⭐ **Thrombosis** = formation of a solid/semisolid mass from blood constituents **within the vascular system during life.**
- ⭐ **Virchow's triad:** **1. endothelial injury, 2. abnormal blood flow (stasis/turbulence), 3. hypercoagulability.**
- **Endothelial injury:** exposes **vWF and tissue factor**; inflammation causes endothelial activation; degeneration: atherosclerosis, aneurysm, varicose veins.
- **Abnormal flow:** **turbulence → arterial and cardiac thrombi; stasis → venous thrombi.** Both promote endothelial activation, platelet/leukocyte contact, slower washout of clotting factors and impeded inhibitor inflow.
- **Hypercoagulability:** abnormally high tendency to clot (alterations in coagulation factors).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Virchow: endothelial injury + stasis/turbulence + hypercoagulability.
- Turbulence → arterial; stasis → venous.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. State Virchow's triad.</summary>

**Answer:** Endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow (stasis or turbulence) and hypercoagulability.

**Why:** Slide "Thrombosis" / Fig. 4.12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does endothelial injury cause thrombosis?</summary>

**Answer:** Mechanical injury exposes von Willebrand factor and tissue factor; inflammation and degenerative disease (atherosclerosis, aneurysm, varicose veins) cause endothelial activation or dysfunction.

**Why:** Slide "I. Endothelial injury".

**Answer:** They activate endothelium, bring platelets and leukocytes into contact with it, slow the washout of activated clotting factors and impede the inflow of inhibitors.

</details>

## 5. Thrombus morphology

- Thrombi **propagate toward the heart**: **arterial** thrombi grow **retrograde**; **venous** thrombi extend **with blood flow.** The propagating end is poorly attached → **embolus.**
- ⭐ **Lines of Zahn** = alternating pale platelet-fibrin and dark red-cell layers; **only in thrombi formed in flowing blood** → separate **antemortem** thrombi from **postmortem clots.**
- **Mural thrombi** (heart chambers, aortic lumen): abnormal contraction (**arrhythmia, dilated cardiomyopathy, MI**), endomyocardial injury (**myocarditis, catheter**); ulcerated atheroma and aneurysms promote aortic thrombi.
- **Vegetations** on valves: **infective endocarditis**, sterile **non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis**, **Libman-Sacks** (SLE).

### Thrombus versus clot
| | **Thrombus** | **Clot** |
|---|---|---|
| **Formed** | In circulating blood **during life** | In stagnant blood **after death** |
| **Composition** | **Platelets** + fibrin, RBC, WBC | Fibrin + RBC + WBC, **no platelets** |
| **Attachment** | **Firmly attached** | Usually not attached |
| **Lines of Zahn** | **Present** | Absent |
| **Consistency** | Granular, dry, friable | Rubbery, gelatinous, moist |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Lines of Zahn prove the thrombus formed in flowing blood (antemortem).
- Postmortem clot = gelatinous, "chicken fat" top, unattached.
- Thrombi propagate toward the heart.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are lines of Zahn and why are they important?</summary>

**Answer:** Alternating pale platelet-fibrin and darker red-cell layers found only in thrombi formed in flowing blood, so they distinguish antemortem thrombi from postmortem clots.

**Why:** Slide "Lines of Zahn".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare a thrombus with a clot.</summary>

**Answer:** A thrombus forms in circulating blood during life, contains platelets, is firmly attached and laminated; a clot forms after death or in stagnant blood, lacks platelets, is unattached and gelatinous.

**Why:** Slide "Thrombus vs Clot".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are mural thrombi and what causes them?</summary>

**Answer:** Thrombi in heart chambers or the aorta caused by abnormal myocardial contraction (arrhythmias, dilated cardiomyopathy, MI) or endomyocardial injury, and promoted by ulcerated plaques and aneurysms.

**Why:** Slide "Mural thrombi".
</details>

## 6. Arterial versus venous thrombi and fate of a thrombus

| | **Arterial** | **Venous (phlebothrombosis)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Site** | Endothelial injury or turbulence | **Stasis / hypercoagulable state** |
| **Direction** | **Retrograde** from attachment | **With blood flow** |
| **Attachment** | Occlusive, **well attached** | Occlusive, **loosely attached** (embolize) |
| **Color** | **Pale, platelet-rich** | **Red, RBC/fibrin-rich** |
| **Treatment** | **Antiplatelets** | **Fibrinolytics** |
| **Example** | **MI** (on ruptured plaque) | **DVT** (lower limb ≈ 90%) |

- ⭐ **Fate of a thrombus:** **1. propagation, 2. embolization, 3. dissolution** (fibrinolysis, new thrombi), **4. organization and recanalization**, **5. infection and mycotic aneurysm.**
- **At autopsy:** postmortem clots are **gelatinous with dark red dependent part and yellow "chicken fat"**, not attached; red thrombi are **firm, attached, with gray fibrin strands.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Arterial = pale, retrograde, antiplatelets; venous = red, with flow, fibrinolytics.
- Five fates: propagation, embolization, dissolution, organization/recanalization, infection.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare arterial and venous thrombi.</summary>

**Answer:** Arterial thrombi form at endothelial injury/turbulence, are pale and platelet-rich, well attached, grow retrograde and are treated with antiplatelets (e.g. MI). Venous thrombi form in stasis, are red and loosely attached, extend with flow and are treated with fibrinolytics (e.g. DVT of the leg).

**Why:** Slide "Arterial thrombosis vs Venous thrombosis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the fates of a thrombus.</summary>

**Answer:** Propagation, embolization, dissolution, organization with recanalization, and infection with mycotic aneurysm formation.

**Why:** Slide "Fate of thrombus".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do you tell a postmortem clot from a red thrombus at autopsy?</summary>

**Answer:** Postmortem clots are gelatinous with a dark red dependent part and a yellow "chicken fat" top and are not attached; red thrombi are firm, focally attached and contain gray fibrin strands.

**Why:** Slide "At autopsy".
</details>
