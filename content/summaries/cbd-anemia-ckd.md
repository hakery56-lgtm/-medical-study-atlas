## 2. Erythropoiesis, EPO and hypoxia-inducible factor

- ⭐ **Hypoxia** (low O2 in the kidney) → **erythropoietin (EPO)** → acts on the **bone marrow** → **erythropoiesis** → more **red cells** → **increased oxygenation**.
- **EPO** promotes production of **mature red blood cells** in the bone marrow. More RBCs → better oxygenation and **lower hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)** → **suppressed EPO** (negative feedback).
- **HIF is degraded under normal oxygen tension**; in **anemia or hypoxia** it **promotes gene transcription of EPO**, needed for maturation of red cells. HIF also promotes **iron absorption and transport**; **iron** is necessary for RBC production.
- **Standard teaching (not on the slides):** EPO is made by **peritubular interstitial cells of the renal cortex**; in CKD the damaged kidneys make **less EPO**, so the marrow is under-stimulated.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hypoxia → kidney EPO → marrow → more RBCs → more O2 (negative feedback).
- HIF is degraded at normal O2 and drives EPO transcription (and iron absorption) in hypoxia.
- Iron is also required for RBC production.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain how tissue hypoxia regulates red cell production through EPO.</summary>

**Answer:** Hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor, which promotes transcription of the EPO gene; EPO acts on the bone marrow to produce mature red cells, which raises oxygenation, lowers HIF and suppresses EPO again.

**Why:** Slide "Erythropoiesis: a homeostatic system".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What happens to HIF in normal oxygen and in hypoxia?</summary>

**Answer:** It is degraded at normal oxygen tension; in anemia or hypoxia it promotes transcription of EPO and also promotes iron absorption and transport.

**Why:** Slide "Erythropoiesis: a homeostatic system".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does CKD cause anemia? (standard teaching)</summary>

**Answer:** The damaged kidneys produce less EPO, so the bone marrow is not stimulated enough to make red cells; iron deficiency and other factors add to it.

**Why:** Standard teaching, supported by the slide diagram.
</details>

## 3. Hemoglobin versus hematocrit

| | **Hemoglobin (Hgb)** | **Hematocrit (Hct)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Measures** | Amount of hemoglobin in blood | **% of blood volume made up of RBCs** |
| **Unit** | **g/dL** | **%** |
| **Function** | **Carries O2** | Reflects RBC concentration |
| **Low →** | Anemia / blood loss | Anemia / blood loss |
| **High →** | Dehydration / erythrocytosis | Dehydration / erythrocytosis |
| **Quick relationship** | ⭐ **Hct ≈ 3 × Hgb** (Hgb 10 g/dL → Hct ≈ 30%) | same |

- Each RBC contains **several hundred million hemoglobin molecules**; **oxygen binds to heme**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hgb = amount (g/dL); Hct = % of blood volume that is RBCs.
- Hct ≈ 3 × Hgb.
- Low = anemia/blood loss; high = dehydration/erythrocytosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate hemoglobin from hematocrit.</summary>

**Answer:** Hemoglobin is the amount of O2-carrying protein in blood (g/dL); hematocrit is the percentage of blood volume made of red cells (%). Both fall in anemia or blood loss and rise in dehydration or erythrocytosis.

**Why:** Slide 9 (Hgb vs Hct table).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the quick relationship between Hct and Hgb?</summary>

**Answer:** Hct ≈ 3 × Hgb; for example Hgb 10 g/dL corresponds to Hct ≈ 30%.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does each red cell contain and where does O2 bind?</summary>

**Answer:** Several hundred million hemoglobin molecules; oxygen binds to the heme of each hemoglobin molecule.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

## 4. Anemia in CKD: etiologies, iron homeostasis and treatment

- ⭐ **Definition (infographic):** **Hgb < 12.0 g/dL in women; Hgb < 13.0 g/dL in men.**
- **Etiologies:** **impaired EPO production\***, **iron deficiency\***, decreased RBC survival, blood loss, severe hyperparathyroidism, inflammatory conditions, folate and B12 deficiency (**\*most common causes in CKD**).
- **Iron homeostasis:** dietary iron is absorbed in the **duodenum**, binds **soluble transferrin** (plasma Fe-Tf), and is stored in the **bone marrow and spleen**; **macrophages** take up iron from RBC destruction. **Hepcidin** (liver) **decreases iron uptake from the gut and decreases mobilization of iron from stores**. **EPO** induces RBC production and mobilization of iron from the bone marrow.
- **Treatment slide:** **recombinant human erythropoietin injection (4000 IU)**, for **subcutaneous and intravenous** use. **Standard teaching (not on the slides):** erythropoiesis-stimulating agents plus iron as needed.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Anemia in CKD: Hgb < 12 (women), < 13 (men).
- Commonest causes: impaired EPO production and iron deficiency.
- Hepcidin reduces gut iron uptake and mobilization of stores; recombinant EPO is given SC or IV.

</div>
