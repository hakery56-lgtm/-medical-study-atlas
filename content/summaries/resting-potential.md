## 1. The resting membrane potential (RMP)

- **Membrane potential** = the **voltage difference across the cell membrane** = difference between the **electrical potential in the cytoplasm** and that in the **extracellular space**.
- A cell **at rest** has the **inside negative relative to the outside**: the **resting membrane potential (RMP)** — "at rest" = **not receiving or transmitting any signals**.
- ⭐ **RMP of neurons ≈ −70 mV** (the inside is 70 mV more negative than the outside).
- It is caused by the **different distribution of ions** inside and outside the membrane.

### Ionic distribution (Table 1)
| Ion | Extracellular | Intracellular |
|---|---|---|
| **Na⁺** | **142 mEq/L** | 10 mEq/L |
| **K⁺** | 4 mEq/L | **140 mEq/L** |
| **Cl⁻** | **103 mEq/L** | 4 mEq/L |
| **Phosphates** | 4 mEq/L | **75 mEq/L** |
| **Proteins** | 30 mg/dL | **200 mg/dL** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Membrane potential = charge separation across the membrane; RMP of a neuron ≈ −70 mV (inside negative).
- Outside: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ high. Inside: K⁺, phosphates and proteins high.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is membrane potential and what is the resting membrane potential?</summary>

**Answer:** The membrane potential is the voltage difference across the cell membrane, between the electrical potential in the cytoplasm and in the extracellular space, produced by separation of positive and negative charges. The resting membrane potential is this difference in a cell at rest (not receiving or transmitting signals), with the inside negative relative to the outside — about −70 mV in neurons.

**Why:** Definition + sign + value.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is it called the "resting" membrane potential?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the neuron is inactive — it is neither receiving nor transmitting any signals.

**Why:** A direct statement from the lecture.
</details>

**Why:** The distribution explains the potential.
</details>

## 2. Causes of the resting membrane potential

### 1) Active transport — the Na⁺-K⁺ pump
- Present in **all cell membranes**; continually moves **Na⁺ out** and **K⁺ in**.
- ⭐ **3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in** → a **net deficit of positive ions inside** → **negative potential inside** (it is *electrogenic*).
- Also creates the **large concentration gradients** of Na⁺ and K⁺.

### 2) Continuous passive diffusion of K⁺ out of the cell
- ⭐ This **diffusion potential is the major factor responsible for the RMP**.
- Both K⁺ and Na⁺ can cross the membrane and move along their **chemical driving forces** (**K⁺ out, Na⁺ in**).
- But **outward K⁺ movement exceeds inward Na⁺ movement** because the membrane is **more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺** (resting K⁺ leak channels).
- Result: **net outward movement of positive charge → negative membrane potential**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Two causes: Na⁺-K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in → net loss of positive charge) and K⁺ diffusion out (major factor).
- K⁺ leaves more than Na⁺ enters because resting permeability is greater to K⁺.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the causes of the resting membrane potential.</summary>

**Answer:** (1) The Na⁺-K⁺ pump, present in all cell membranes, transports three Na⁺ out for every two K⁺ in, leaving a net deficit of positive ions inside and creating the large Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients. (2) Continuous passive diffusion of K⁺ out of the cell, the major factor: K⁺ moves out and Na⁺ in along their chemical gradients, but the membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺, so there is a net outward movement of positive charge and the inside becomes negative.

**Why:** Pump + leak; K⁺ diffusion is the major one.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does the cell membrane's outward K⁺ movement exceed inward Na⁺ movement at rest?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the resting membrane permeability to K⁺ is greater than to Na⁺, so more positive charge leaves than enters.

**Why:** Permeability, not concentration, decides.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the contribution of the Na⁺-K⁺ pump to the RMP?</summary>

**Answer:** It pumps 3 Na⁺ to the outside for 2 K⁺ to the inside, so each cycle removes one net positive charge from the cell, contributing to the negative potential, and it maintains the Na⁺ and K⁺ concentration gradients needed for diffusion potentials.

**Why:** Direct (electrogenic) + indirect (gradient) effect.
</details>

## 3. Graded potentials

- **Small changes in membrane potential** that occur when **ion channels open or close in response to a stimulus**.
- Occur at the **dendrites and soma** of neurons, when **gated ion channels** open in response to stimuli such as **neurotransmitters**.
- They **summate** to bring the **axon hillock** to threshold (see below).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Graded potentials = small, local changes in membrane potential at dendrites/soma, produced by gated channels (e.g. neurotransmitters).
- They can reach threshold and trigger an action potential.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are graded potentials and where do they occur?</summary>

**Answer:** Small changes in membrane potential caused by opening or closing of ion channels in response to a stimulus; they occur at the dendrites and soma of neurons when gated channels open in response to stimuli such as neurotransmitters.

**Why:** Definition + site.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the relationship between graded potentials and action potentials?</summary>

**Answer:** Action potentials occur in excitable membranes in response to graded potentials that reach threshold.

**Why:** Graded → threshold → AP.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What triggers the opening of ion channels in a graded potential?</summary>

**Answer:** A stimulus, such as a neurotransmitter binding, opening (or closing) the gated ion channels.

**Why:** Chemical (ligand) gating.
</details>

## 4. The action potential & its three phases

- Nerve and muscle are **excitable cells**; **action potentials** occur in their membranes in response to graded potentials that **reach threshold**.
- When a **sufficient stimulus** (**electrical, chemical, physical** or other) is applied, **the negativity is reversed** and the **inside becomes positive** = **action potential**.
- **Response:** nerve cell → **transmission of the action potential (nerve impulse)**; muscle cell → **contraction**.

### Three phases
| Phase | Membrane potential | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **1. Rapid depolarization** | **−70 mV → +30 mV** | **sudden, dramatic ↑ in Na⁺ permeability** → **Na⁺ rushes in** down its electrochemical gradient |
| ⭐ **2. Repolarization** | **+30 mV → back to −70 mV** | within **1 ms**, **Na⁺ permeability falls rapidly** (↓ Na⁺ inflow) while **K⁺ permeability rises** → **K⁺ moves out** |
| ⭐ **3. After-hyperpolarization** | goes **below** the RMP | **K⁺ permeability stays elevated for 5–15 ms** after reaching the RMP |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- AP phases: rapid depolarization (Na⁺ in; −70 → +30 mV), repolarization (Na⁺ permeability falls, K⁺ out; +30 → −70 mV), after-hyperpolarization (K⁺ permeability stays high 5–15 ms).
- Response: nerve → nerve impulse; muscle → contraction.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the phases of the action potential.</summary>

**Answer:** (1) Rapid depolarization — membrane potential rises from −70 mV to +30 mV because of a sudden, dramatic increase in sodium permeability and inflow of Na⁺ down its electrochemical gradient. (2) Repolarization — within 1 ms Na⁺ permeability decreases rapidly and K⁺ permeability increases, so K⁺ moves out and the potential returns from +30 mV to −70 mV. (3) After-hyperpolarization — K⁺ permeability remains elevated for 5–15 ms after the resting level is reached, so the potential falls below the RMP.

**Why:** Three phases with numbers and ions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is an action potential and how do nerve and muscle cells respond?</summary>

**Answer:** When a sufficient electrical, chemical or physical stimulus is applied to an excitable membrane, the negativity is reversed and the inside becomes positive; this change in membrane potential is the action potential. In a nerve cell the response is transmission of the nerve impulse and in a muscle cell it is contraction.

**Why:** Definition + the two responses.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What causes repolarization?</summary>

**Answer:** The rapid decrease in sodium permeability (reducing Na⁺ inflow) together with the increase in potassium permeability, so K⁺ flows out of the cell down its electrochemical gradient and restores the resting membrane potential.

**Why:** Two events: Na⁺ off, K⁺ on.
</details>

## 5. Properties of the action potential

- ⭐ **Threshold (firing) potential** = membrane potential at which the AP starts; it must be reached at the **axon initial segment (axon hillock / trigger zone)**.
- **Threshold stimulus:** raises the membrane potential **15 mV above RMP (−70 → −55 mV)**; the AP phases then start and **do not stop until the cycle is complete**.
- **How threshold is reached:** graded potentials depolarize the membrane at the **axon hillock**, where **voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated**; at **−55 mV the activation gates of Na⁺ channels open rapidly** → further depolarization → **positive feedback** → AP begins. Hillock Na⁺ channels have **higher kinetics** than those along the axon.
- **Subthreshold stimuli:** weak stimuli that **do not cause an AP**; they may cause **local responses** that **increase or decrease with stimulus strength**, **subside after removal** of the stimulus and **do not travel (no propagation)**.
- **Supramaximal stimuli:** same effect as threshold stimuli — **do not change the shape** of the AP curve.
- ⭐ **All-or-none principle:** if depolarization reaches threshold or more, **the AP amplitude is the same**; if not, **no AP occurs**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Threshold = −55 mV (15 mV above RMP) at the axon hillock (trigger zone), rich in voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
- Subthreshold: local responses, no propagation; supramaximal: same AP as threshold.
- All-or-none: AP amplitude is independent of stimulus strength once threshold is reached.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the threshold potential and explain how it is reached.</summary>

**Answer:** The threshold (firing) potential is the membrane potential at which an action potential starts, reached at the axon initial segment (hillock), about 15 mV above rest (−70 → −55 mV). Graded potentials depolarize the hillock, where voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated; at −55 mV the activation gates of Na⁺ channels open rapidly, causing further depolarization (positive feedback) and the action potential begins.

**Why:** Value + site + positive feedback.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare subthreshold, threshold and supramaximal stimuli.</summary>

**Answer:** A subthreshold stimulus causes only a local response that varies with stimulus strength, does not propagate and subsides when the stimulus is removed. A threshold stimulus raises the membrane potential by 15 mV and triggers a complete action potential. A supramaximal stimulus produces the same action potential as a threshold stimulus without changing its shape.

**Why:** Three stimulus levels.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. State the all-or-none principle.</summary>

**Answer:** If the membrane is depolarized to threshold or beyond, an action potential of the same amplitude occurs; if depolarization does not reach threshold, no action potential occurs.

**Why:** Stronger stimulus ≠ bigger AP (it changes the frequency).
</details>

## 6. The refractory period

- During and just after an AP the membrane is **less excitable** — the **refractory period**, divided into two phases:

| Phase | Extent | A second AP can be produced? |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Absolute refractory period** | **all of depolarization + most of repolarization (until ⅔ repolarization)** | **No — whatever the stimulus strength** |
| ⭐ **Relative refractory period** | **from ⅔ repolarization to the end of repolarization** | **Yes — but only with a stimulus stronger than threshold** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Absolute refractory period: from the start of depolarization to ⅔ repolarization — no second AP possible.
- Relative refractory period: ⅔ repolarization to the end — a second AP needs a stronger-than-threshold stimulus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the refractory period and its two phases.</summary>

**Answer:** The refractory period is the period of reduced excitability during and immediately after an action potential. In the absolute refractory period (all of depolarization plus most of repolarization, up to two-thirds) no second action potential can be generated whatever the strength of the stimulus. In the relative refractory period (from two-thirds repolarization to the end) a second action potential can be generated only by a stimulus stronger than the normal threshold stimulus.

**Why:** Two phases with their limits.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why can an action potential not be generated during the absolute refractory period?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the membrane is still depolarized/repolarizing and the Na⁺ channels are open or inactivated, so a second stimulus, however strong, cannot trigger another action potential.

**Why:** Na⁺ channel inactivation (standard physiology explaining the lecture's statement).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the importance of the refractory period?</summary>

**Answer:** It limits how frequently action potentials can be generated and ensures that the impulse travels in one direction along the axon, from the point of origin onward (standard physiological significance).

**Why:** Extension of the lecture, flagged as standard physiology.
</details>
