## 2. Chondrosarcoma

- **One of the commonest malignant tumours originating in bone**; **M > F**.
- **Slow-growing**: **dull ache** or a **gradually enlarging lump**.
- **Staging:** full staging; ⭐ **CT and MRI must be carried out before performing a biopsy**.
- **Treatment:** slow-growing and **metastasizes late** → **wide excision and prosthetic replacement**; **amputation** in some cases; **isolated pulmonary metastases can sometimes be resected**.
- ⭐ **The tumour does not respond to radiotherapy or chemotherapy.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Slow-growing, late metastases, M > F.
- CT and MRI before biopsy.
- Wide excision and prosthesis; no response to radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical features of chondrosarcoma.</summary>

**Answer:** One of the commonest malignant bone tumours, commoner in males; slow-growing, presenting with a dull ache or a gradually enlarging lump.

**Why:** Slide "Primary malignant bone tumours – chondrosarcoma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What staging is needed and when?</summary>

**Answer:** Full staging procedures; CT scans and MRI must be carried out before performing a biopsy.

**Why:** Slide "Chondrosarcoma – staging".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is chondrosarcoma treated?</summary>

**Answer:** Wide excision with prosthetic replacement (amputation in some cases); isolated pulmonary metastases can sometimes be resected; it does not respond to radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

**Why:** Slide "Chondrosarcoma – treatment".
</details>

## 3. Osteosarcoma

- **Highly malignant tumour arising within the bone**, spreading rapidly outwards to the **periosteum and soft tissues**; predominantly **children and adolescents**.
- **Pain** is the first symptom: **constant, worse at night, gradually increasing**. **ESR raised** and **serum alkaline phosphatase increased**.
- **X-ray:** cortex breached, tumour extends into adjacent tissues; ⭐ **'sunburst' effect** (streaks of new bone radiating from the cortex); **Codman's triangle** (reactive new bone at the angles of periosteal elevation).
- **Staging:** chest X-ray, and **pulmonary CT** (much more sensitive for lung metastases).
- **Treatment:** **multiagent neoadjuvant chemotherapy for 8–12 weeks**, then **wide resection**; chemotherapy continues for **another 6–12 months**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Children and adolescents; constant night pain; ↑ ESR and ↑ ALP.
- Sunburst effect and Codman's triangle.
- Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (8–12 weeks) → wide resection → chemotherapy 6–12 months.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical features of osteosarcoma.</summary>

**Answer:** A highly malignant tumour arising within bone that spreads rapidly to the periosteum and soft tissues, mainly in children and adolescents; the first symptom is constant pain, worse at night and increasing in severity, with raised ESR and increased alkaline phosphatase.

**Why:** Slide "Osteosarcoma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the X-ray signs and staging imaging of osteosarcoma?</summary>

**Answer:** Cortex breached with sunburst streaks of new bone and Codman's triangle at the angle of periosteal elevation; staging with chest X-ray and pulmonary CT, which is more sensitive for lung metastases.

**Why:** Slide "Osteosarcoma – imaging".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is osteosarcoma treated?</summary>

**Answer:** Multiagent neoadjuvant chemotherapy for 8–12 weeks, then wide resection, then chemotherapy for another 6–12 months.

**Why:** Slide "Osteosarcoma – treatment".
</details>

## 4. Ewing's sarcoma

- **Age 10–20 years**; usually a **tubular bone**, especially the **tibia, fibula or clavicle**.
- **Throbbing pain** and **swelling**; **generalized illness, pyrexia, warm tender swelling and raised ESR** may suggest **osteomyelitis**.
- **X-ray:** bone destruction **predominantly in the mid-diaphysis** (unlike osteosarcoma); new bone may extend along the shaft as fusiform layers – ⭐ **'onion-peel' effect**. **Sunray appearance and Codman's triangles** are usually associated with osteosarcoma but are **just as common in Ewing's sarcoma**.
- **CT and MRI** reveal the **large extraosseous component**.
- **Treatment: all three methods** – neoadjuvant chemotherapy; then **wide excision** (favourable site) or **radiotherapy followed by local excision** (less accessible); then chemotherapy for **1 year**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 10–20 years; tibia, fibula, clavicle; mimics osteomyelitis.
- Mid-diaphysis destruction and onion-peel layering.
- Chemotherapy + surgery or radiotherapy + chemotherapy for 1 year.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical presentation of Ewing's sarcoma and what it can mimic.</summary>

**Answer:** Ages 10–20, usually in a tubular bone (tibia, fibula, clavicle), with throbbing pain and swelling; generalized illness, fever, a warm tender swelling and raised ESR may suggest osteomyelitis.

**Why:** Slide "Ewing's sarcoma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the X-ray and CT/MRI findings of Ewing's sarcoma?</summary>

**Answer:** Bone destruction predominantly in the mid-diaphysis (unlike osteosarcoma), with onion-peel layers of new bone; sunray and Codman's triangles occur just as commonly as in osteosarcoma; CT and MRI show the large extraosseous component.

**Why:** Slide "Ewing's sarcoma – X-rays".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is Ewing's sarcoma treated?</summary>

**Answer:** A combination of neoadjuvant chemotherapy; then wide excision (favourable site) or radiotherapy followed by local excision (less accessible site); then chemotherapy for 1 year.

**Why:** Slide "Ewing's sarcoma – treatment".
</details>

## 5. Multiple myeloma

- **Malignant B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder of the marrow, with plasma cells predominating.** Bone effects: **marrow cell proliferation and increased osteoclastic activity** → **osteoporosis** and **discrete lytic lesions** throughout the skeleton.
- **Age 45–65**; **weakness, backache, bone pain or pathological fracture**; **hypercalcaemia** → thirst, polyuria, abdominal pain.
- **X-ray:** **osteoporosis** (one of the commonest causes of osteoporosis and vertebral compression fracture in men over 45); ⭐ **'classical' lesions = multiple punched-out defects with 'soft' margins (lack of new bone)** in the **skull, pelvis and proximal femur**, a **crushed vertebra**, or a **solitary lytic tumour in a large-bone metaphysis**.
- **Investigations:** **mild anaemia, high ESR, hypercalcaemia**; **over half have Bence Jones protein in the urine**; **sternal marrow puncture** shows **plasmacytosis** with typical 'myeloma' cells.
- **Treatment:** **pain control**; **supportive measures** (fluid balance, hypercalcaemia); **internal fixation** of limb and spine fractures; **specific therapy**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Plasma-cell marrow disorder; osteoclast activation → lytic lesions and osteoporosis.
- Punched-out skull lesions, crushed vertebra; Bence Jones protein in over half.
- Supportive care, internal fixation, specific therapy.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is multiple myeloma and how does it affect bone?</summary>

**Answer:** A malignant B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder of the marrow with plasma cells predominating; marrow cell proliferation and increased osteoclastic activity cause osteoporosis and discrete lytic lesions throughout the skeleton.

**Why:** Slide "Multiple myeloma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the classical X-ray findings of myeloma?</summary>

**Answer:** Osteoporosis, and multiple punched-out defects with soft margins (no new bone) in the skull, pelvis and proximal femur, a crushed vertebra, or a solitary lytic tumour in a large-bone metaphysis.

**Why:** Slide "Myeloma – X-rays".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which investigations support the diagnosis?</summary>

**Answer:** Mild anaemia, high ESR, hypercalcaemia, Bence Jones protein in the urine (over half of patients) and plasmacytosis with typical myeloma cells on sternal marrow puncture.

**Why:** Slide "Myeloma – investigations".
</details>

## 6. Metastatic bone disease

- The **skeleton is one of the commonest sites of secondary cancer**. ⭐ **Commonest source = carcinoma of the breast**; next **prostate, kidney** and others; in about **10%** no primary is found.
- **Commonest sites:** **vertebrae, pelvis, proximal half of the femur, humerus**.
- Metastases are usually **osteolytic**; **osteoblastic** lesions are uncommon and usually occur in **prostatic carcinoma**.
- Patient usually **50–70 years**; **pain** is the commonest symptom; **hypercalcaemia** symptoms may occur; **radioscintigraphy (bone scan)**.
- **Treatment:** prognosis for survival is **poor** → keep the patient **comfortable**, control pain and metastatic activity; **radiotherapy** (unless contraindicated) controls pain and reduces growth; **hormone therapy** for breast or prostate deposits; treat **hypercalcaemia**; **internal fixation** of limb fractures; **brace or operative stabilization** for spinal disease.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Breast is the commonest source; vertebrae, pelvis, proximal femur and humerus the commonest sites.
- Usually osteolytic; osteoblastic mainly from prostate.
- Palliative treatment: radiotherapy, hormones, fixation, bracing.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the commonest sources and sites of bone metastases?</summary>

**Answer:** Sources: breast carcinoma first, then prostate, kidney and others (about 10% no primary found). Sites: vertebrae, pelvis, proximal half of the femur and humerus.

**Why:** Slide "Metastatic bone disease".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the usual nature of bone metastases?</summary>

**Answer:** Usually osteolytic; osteoblastic lesions are uncommon and usually occur in prostatic carcinoma.

**Why:** Slide "Metastatic bone disease".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is metastatic bone disease managed?</summary>

**Answer:** Prognosis is poor, so aim for comfort: control of pain and metastatic activity with radiotherapy (unless contraindicated), hormone therapy for breast or prostate deposits, treatment of hypercalcaemia, internal fixation of limb fractures and a brace or operative stabilization for spinal disease.

**Why:** Slides "Metastatic bone disease – treatment" (two slides).
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Chondrosarcoma: NO response to radiotherapy or chemotherapy; wide excision with prosthesis; CT/MRI BEFORE biopsy.**
- **Osteosarcoma = sunburst + Codman's triangle; Ewing's = onion-peel, mid-diaphysis, but sunray and Codman's are just as common in Ewing's.**
- **Ewing's sarcoma can mimic osteomyelitis (fever, warm tender swelling, raised ESR).**
- **Myeloma = punched-out lytic lesions with soft margins (no new bone); Bence Jones protein; hypercalcaemia.**
- **Bone metastases are usually osteolytic; osteoblastic = prostate; commonest source = breast.**
- **Over 70, metastases outnumber primary bone tumours** (Lecture 1).
