## 1. Non-depolarizing blockers: mechanism and effects

- **Neuromuscular blockers block muscular nAChRs → skeletal muscle relaxation; adjunct to anesthesia (with artificial ventilation available).**
- ⭐ **Two categories:** **non-depolarizing (majority; nAChR antagonists)** and **depolarizing (nAChR agonists).**
- **Curare** = plant alkaloid arrow poison; **tubocurarine** = main component, **rarely used now.** All are **quaternary ammonium compounds → poorly absorbed, rapidly excreted, don't cross the placenta (usable in obstetrics).**
- ⭐ **Mechanism:** **competitive antagonists at endplate nAChRs → no depolarization → flaccid paralysis**; **70-80% of receptors must be blocked** before transmission fails.
- ⭐ **Order of paralysis:** **extrinsic eye muscles (double vision) and facial muscles → fingers → limbs → neck and trunk → intercostals → diaphragm.** **Respiratory muscles are last affected and first to recover.**
- **Pharmacokinetics:** **IV; onset and recovery differ;** most **metabolized by the liver or excreted unchanged by the kidney;** **exceptions: atracurium (spontaneous plasma hydrolysis) and mivacurium (plasma cholinesterase).** Duration **15 min to 1-2 h.** **Renal or hepatic impairment prolongs paralysis, except atracurium.**
- **Unwanted effects:** **tubocurarine: hypotension (ganglion block) and histamine release (hypotension, bronchospasm; not nAChR-mediated);** atracurium and mivacurium release less histamine; **gallamine, and less so pancuronium, block cardiac mAChRs → tachycardia.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Competitive nAChR block; flaccid paralysis; eye muscles first, diaphragm last.
- Atracurium: spontaneous hydrolysis (safe in renal/hepatic failure).
- Histamine release: tubocurarine > atracurium, mivacurium.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanism and course of paralysis of non-depolarizing blockers.</summary>

**Answer:** They competitively block endplate nAChRs, preventing depolarization and causing flaccid paralysis (70-80% of receptors must be blocked); paralysis starts in the extrinsic eye and facial muscles, then fingers, limbs, neck and trunk, and finally intercostals and diaphragm, which recover first.

**Why:** Slides "Mechanism of action" and "Effects".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which agents are not eliminated by liver or kidney?</summary>

</details>

## 2. Classification of non-depolarizing agents

| Group | Drug | Onset | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Isoquinoline** | **Tubocurarine** | **Slow (> 5 min)** | **Long (1-2 h)** | **Hypotension, bronchoconstriction (histamine); liver and kidney; rarely used** |
| | **Atracurium** | **Intermediate (2-3 min)** | **Intermediate (< 30 min)** | **Transient hypotension (histamine); spontaneous degradation; widely used** |
| | **Cisatracurium** | **Intermediate** | **Intermediate (< 30 min)** | **Less histamine than atracurium; spontaneous hydrolysis** |
| | **Doxacurium** | **Intermediate** | **Long** | **Less histamine; kidney** |
| | **Mivacurium** | **Fast (~2 min)** | **Short (~15 min)** | **Histamine; pseudocholinesterase (longer in deficiency)** |
| **Steroid** | **Pancuronium** | **Intermediate** | **Long** | **Slight tachycardia, no hypotension; kidney; widely used** |
| | **Vecuronium** | **Intermediate** | **Intermediate (30-40 min)** | **Few side effects; liver; widely used** |
| | **Rocuronium** | **Fastest of all** | Similar to vecuronium | |
| **Other** | **Gallamine** | | | **First synthetic alternative; blocks M2 → tachycardia; rarely used** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Isoquinolines release histamine; steroids (pancuronium, vecuronium) do not.
- Rocuronium: fastest onset; mivacurium: shortest duration.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify non-depolarizing blockers with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Isoquinolines (tubocurarine, atracurium, cisatracurium, doxacurium, mivacurium), steroids (pancuronium, vecuronium, rocuronium) and others (gallamine).

**Why:** Slides "Classification".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare atracurium and cisatracurium.</summary>

**Answer:** Both have intermediate onset and duration and spontaneous hydrolysis; cisatracurium is a derivative that releases less histamine.

</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which blocker has the fastest onset and which is inactivated by pseudocholinesterase?</summary>

**Answer:** Rocuronium has the fastest onset; mivacurium is inactivated by pseudocholinesterase (prolonged in genetic deficiency).

**Why:** Classification table.
</details>

## 3. Depolarizing blockers: succinylcholine

- **Decamethonium** = first depolarizing agent (long duration). **Succinylcholine (suxamethonium)** = **the only one in clinical use;** **two ACh molecules linked**; **hydrolyzed quickly by plasma cholinesterase.**
- ⭐ **Phase I:** **Na⁺ channels open → depolarization → fasciculations,** then endplate excitability is lost. **Phase II:** **desensitization → flaccid paralysis** (membrane repolarized, receptor desensitized).
- ⭐ **Compared with non-depolarizing:** **neostigmine reverses non-depolarizing block, not depolarizing** (phase I is augmented, phase II may be antagonized); **depolarizing block gives fasciculations and postoperative muscle pain.**
- **Uses:** **IV, fast onset, short duration; brief procedures (tracheal intubation, ECT).** Prolonged in **liver disease or genetic plasma cholinesterase deficiency.**
- ⭐ **Dangers:** **bradycardia** (muscarinic; **prevented by atropine**); **hyperkalemia** (burns, trauma, denervation → arrhythmia, cardiac arrest); **↑ intraocular pressure** (extraocular contracture); **prolonged paralysis** (**scoline apnea ~1 in 2000**, anticholinesterases such as echothiophate, procaine, neonates, liver disease); **malignant hyperthermia.**
- ⭐ **Malignant hyperthermia:** **mutation of the SR Ca²⁺ release channel;** intense spasm, high temperature, **~65% mortality;** with **succinylcholine and halothane;** **treat with cooling and dantrolene (blocks SR Ca²⁺ release).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Succinylcholine = nAChR agonist; fasciculation then paralysis; not reversed by neostigmine.
- Dangers: hyperkalemia, bradycardia, ↑ IOP, scoline apnea, malignant hyperthermia (dantrolene).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe phase I and phase II of depolarizing block.</summary>

**Answer:** Phase I: Na⁺ channel opening gives depolarization and fasciculations that subside as excitability is lost; phase II: persistent binding desensitizes the receptor, causing flaccid paralysis with a repolarized membrane.

**Why:** Slides "Depolarizing blocking drugs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the dangers of succinylcholine.</summary>

**Answer:** Bradycardia (prevented by atropine), hyperkalemia (burns, trauma, denervation), increased intraocular pressure, prolonged paralysis (cholinesterase deficiency, liver disease, anticholinesterases) and malignant hyperthermia.

**Why:** Slides "Unwanted effects and dangers of succinylcholine".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is malignant hyperthermia and how is it treated?</summary>

**Answer:** A rare inherited SR Ca²⁺ channel mutation causing intense muscle spasm and a dramatic temperature rise with succinylcholine or halothane; treated by cooling and dantrolene.

**Why:** Slide "Malignant hyperthermia".
</details>

## 4. Drugs acting on autonomic ganglia

| | **Ganglion stimulants** | **Ganglion blockers** |
|---|---|---|
| **Drugs** | **Nicotine, DMPP** | **Hexamethonium, trimetaphan** |
| **Action** | **Stimulate sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia** | **Block all autonomic and enteric ganglia** |
| **Effects** | **Tachycardia, ↑ BP, variable GI motility, ↑ bronchial, salivary, sweat secretions** | **Hypotension, loss of CV reflexes, ↓ secretions, GI paralysis, impaired micturition** |
| **Use** | **None, except nicotine for smoking cessation** | **Obsolete; trimetaphan occasionally for controlled hypotension in anesthesia** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Stimulants: effects are complex (both divisions).
- Blockers: hypotension, GI paralysis, impaired micturition; obsolete.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give examples and effects of ganglion stimulants.</summary>

**Answer:** Nicotine and DMPP stimulate both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia causing tachycardia, raised BP, variable GI effects and increased bronchial, salivary and sweat secretions; the only use is nicotine for smoking cessation.

**Why:** Slide "Ganglion-stimulating drugs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give examples and effects of ganglion blockers.</summary>

**Answer:** Hexamethonium and trimetaphan block all autonomic and enteric ganglia causing hypotension, loss of cardiovascular reflexes, reduced secretions, GI paralysis and impaired micturition.

**Why:** Slide "Ganglion-blocking drugs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the remaining use of trimetaphan?</summary>

**Answer:** Occasionally to produce controlled hypotension in anesthesia; the class is otherwise clinically obsolete.

**Why:** Slide "Ganglion-blocking drugs".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Non-depolarizing = competitive antagonists (reversed by neostigmine); depolarizing = agonists (not reversed).**
- **Atracurium and mivacurium: not liver/kidney eliminated.**
- **Respiratory muscles: last to be paralyzed, first to recover.**
- **Succinylcholine: hyperkalemia in burns; atropine prevents bradycardia; dantrolene for malignant hyperthermia.**
- **Gallamine and pancuronium: tachycardia (M blockade).**
