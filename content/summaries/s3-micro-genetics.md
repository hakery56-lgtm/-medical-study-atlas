## 1. Importance and basic concepts

- **Objectives:** understand the principles of bacterial genetics, the mechanisms of **genetic variation**, and how they influence **pathogenicity, antibiotic resistance and clinical applications**; explain the role of **DNA, plasmids, operons and horizontal gene transfer (HGT)**.
- **Importance:** key to understanding **infectious diseases, antimicrobial resistance and biotechnology**; the foundation for **novel antibiotics, vaccines and diagnostics**.
- Bacteria typically have **a single circular DNA molecule** with the genes for essential functions; genetic material exists in **chromosomes and plasmids**; genes are organized into **operons**.

### Bacterial chromosome
- Usually **single, double-stranded, circular DNA**, compacted in the **nucleoid without a nuclear membrane**.
- Carries **essential genes for survival, metabolism and reproduction**.
- **No histones** — **nucleoid-associated proteins** package the DNA and regulate gene expression.

### Plasmids
- **Small, circular, double-stranded DNA independent of the chromosome**; **replicate autonomously**; carry **selective-advantage genes** (**antibiotic resistance, toxin production**); key in **horizontal gene transfer**.

### Transposons ("jumping genes")
- **Mobile DNA sequences** that move **within and between genomes**; ↑ genetic diversity; can **disrupt or activate genes**; some carry **antibiotic-resistance genes**.

### Operon
- ⭐ **A cluster of genes under the control of a single promoter, transcribed together as ONE mRNA** (prokaryotes); genes usually encode proteins with **related functions** → coordinated regulation.
- Controlled by **promoters, operators, repressors/activators**; lets the cell switch many genes on/off together.
- **Example: the *lac* operon in *E. coli* (lactose metabolism).**

### Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes
- **Highly conserved** DNA regions producing **rRNA** for protein synthesis; used in **bacterial identification and phylogenetic studies** (sequence differences classify bacteria).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Chromosome: single circular dsDNA in the nucleoid; no histones (nucleoid-associated proteins).
- Plasmids: autonomous extra DNA (resistance, toxins). Transposons: jumping genes, may carry resistance.
- Operon: genes under one promoter → one mRNA (lac operon).
- rRNA genes: conserved; used for identification and phylogeny.

</div>

## 2. Mutations and horizontal gene transfer

### Mutations
- **Heritable changes in DNA sequence**, **spontaneous or caused by mutagens**.
- **Types:** **point mutations, insertions, deletions, frameshifts**.
- Drive **evolution and adaptation**; may contribute to **antibiotic resistance**.

### Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)
- **Movement of genetic material between organisms without reproduction**; introduces new traits rapidly; spreads **resistance and virulence genes**.
- ⭐ **Three mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | What happens |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Transformation** | **uptake of free DNA fragments from the environment** by a **competent** bacterium; used in cloning and genetic engineering |
| ⭐ **Transduction** | transfer of bacterial DNA **via bacteriophages**; **generalized** and **specialized** forms; affects toxin production and drug resistance |
| ⭐ **Conjugation** | **direct DNA transfer through physical contact, mediated by a pilus**; **F (fertility) plasmids** are transferred; **major driver of resistance spread** |

- **Plasmid-mediated resistance:** resistance plasmids spread by conjugation → **multi-drug-resistant strains**.
- **Pathogenicity islands:** **large genetic regions acquired by HGT encoding virulence factors** (toxin production, adhesion, immune evasion).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Mutation types: point, insertion, deletion, frameshift.
- HGT: transformation (free DNA, competent cell), transduction (bacteriophage; generalized/specialized), conjugation (pilus, F plasmid).
- Resistance plasmids spread by conjugation → MDR; pathogenicity islands acquired by HGT.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the types of mutations.</summary>

**Answer:** Point mutations, insertions, deletions and frameshifts; they may arise spontaneously or due to mutagens.

**Why:** Mutations are heritable DNA changes.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the three mechanisms of horizontal gene transfer.</summary>

**Answer:** Transformation: uptake of free environmental DNA by competent bacteria. Transduction: bacteriophage-mediated transfer of bacterial DNA (generalized or specialized). Conjugation: direct cell-to-cell DNA transfer through a pilus, transferring F plasmids; it is the major driver of resistance gene spread.

**Why:** Three HGT mechanisms.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does plasmid-mediated resistance spread and why is it important?</summary>

**Answer:** Plasmids carrying resistance genes spread rapidly by conjugation, creating multi-drug-resistant strains and contributing to the global rise of untreatable infections.

**Why:** Critical for antimicrobial therapy design.
</details>

## 3. Applications of bacterial genetics

- **Recombinant DNA technology:** genes inserted into **bacterial plasmids** for expression → **insulin, vaccines, therapeutic proteins**; also agriculture, environmental clean-up, synthetic biology.
- **Diagnostics:** **PCR (based on bacterial enzymes)** detects pathogens rapidly; **genome sequencing** tracks outbreaks, studies resistance genes and improves patient management.
- **Vaccines:** identify **virulence genes and antigenic targets**; **engineered bacteria** produce safe vaccine components; understanding genetic variability improves vaccine design.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Recombinant DNA in plasmids → insulin, vaccines, therapeutic proteins.
- PCR and genome sequencing → diagnosis, outbreak tracking, resistance genes.
- Genetics guides vaccine design (virulence genes, antigens).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is recombinant DNA technology applied using bacteria?</summary>

**Answer:** Genes are inserted into bacterial plasmids so that bacteria express them, producing insulin, vaccines and other therapeutic proteins; GM bacteria are also used in agriculture, environmental clean-up and synthetic biology.

**Why:** Plasmids as vectors.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the role of bacterial genetics in diagnostics?</summary>

**Answer:** PCR (based on bacterial enzymes) detects pathogens quickly and accurately, and genome sequencing tracks outbreaks, studies resistance genes and improves patient management.

**Why:** Revolutionized clinical microbiology.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does bacterial genetics help vaccine development?</summary>

**Answer:** It identifies virulence genes and antigenic targets, allows genetically engineered bacteria to produce vaccine components and helps design vaccines that cover genetic variability and emerging strains.

**Why:** Vaccine design relies on genetic information.
</details>
