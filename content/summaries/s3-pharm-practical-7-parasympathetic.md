## 1. The case and glaucoma

- **Case:** 60-year-old man, **open-angle glaucoma**, on **latanoprost** (prostaglandin analog) + **oral acetazolamide** (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor); **IOP 24 mmHg in both eyes (target 18)**, occasional headaches, red eyes, visual disturbance → ophthalmologist adds **pilocarpine 2% eye drops, 1 drop in each eye three times daily** → **IOP 18 mmHg**, better vision, fewer headaches.
- **Glaucoma:** group of eye disorders with **progressive optic nerve damage**, usually with **raised IOP**; gradual, **irreversible** vision loss. ⭐ **Primarily an optic nerve disease**; raised IOP is the main risk factor, but it can occur with **normal IOP (normal-tension glaucoma).**
- **Latanoprost:** **prostaglandin F₂α analog** → **↑ uveoscleral outflow** → ↓ IOP.

| Feature | Open (wide)-angle glaucoma | Closed (narrow)-angle glaucoma |
|---|---|---|
| **Angle** | **Open**; outflow ↓ due to **trabecular meshwork dysfunction** | **Physically obstructed (often by the iris)** |
| **Course** | **Chronic, painless**, gradual vision loss | **Sudden ↑ IOP**: acute pain, blurred vision, halos, headache, nausea |
| **Urgency** | Chronic | **Ophthalmic emergency** |

| Feature | Glaucoma | Cataract |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem** | **Optic nerve disease** | **Lens opacity** |
| **Vision** | **Peripheral loss first**; permanent blindness if untreated | Cloudy vision; **restored by surgery** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Glaucoma = optic nerve disease; raised IOP is the main risk factor.
- Open-angle: chronic and painless; closed-angle: emergency.
- Glaucoma is more dangerous than cataract because the loss is irreversible.
- Latanoprost ↑ uveoscleral outflow.

</div>

## 2. Aqueous humor and autonomic innervation of the eye

- **Production:** **ciliary body epithelium**, in the **posterior chamber**.
- **Drainage:**
  1. **Conventional (trabecular):** trabecular meshwork → **canal of Schlemm** → episcleral veins.
  2. **Uveoscleral:** ciliary muscle → suprachoroidal space → scleral veins.
- **Parasympathetic (3rd cranial / oculomotor nerve):** **sphincter pupillae contraction (miosis)** and **ciliary muscle contraction (accommodation for near vision).**
- **Sympathetic:** **α₁ → dilator pupillae contraction (mydriasis)**; **β₂ on ciliary epithelium → ↑ aqueous humor production.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Aqueous made by ciliary epithelium; two outflow routes.
- Parasympathetic = miosis + near accommodation; sympathetic α₁ = mydriasis.
- Sympathetic β₂ ↑ aqueous production.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Where is aqueous humor produced and how does it drain?</summary>

**Answer:** Produced by the ciliary body epithelium in the posterior chamber; drains via the trabecular meshwork → canal of Schlemm → episcleral veins (conventional) and via ciliary muscle → suprachoroidal space → scleral veins (uveoscleral).

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the parasympathetic innervation of the eye.</summary>

**Answer:** Via the oculomotor nerve: contraction of the sphincter pupillae (miosis) and ciliary muscle (accommodation for near vision).

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the sympathetic effects in the eye.</summary>

**Answer:** α₁ contracts the dilator pupillae (mydriasis); β₂ on the ciliary epithelium increases aqueous humor production.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

## 3. Pilocarpine

- **Class:** **direct-acting muscarinic (M) agonist**; **cholinomimetic alkaloid.**
- **Mechanism:** stimulates **M₃ receptors** on the **iris sphincter (miosis)** and **ciliary muscle (contraction).**
- ⭐ **How it lowers IOP:** ciliary muscle contraction → **opens trabecular meshwork** → ↑ aqueous outflow through the **conventional pathway** → ↓ IOP.
- **Side effects:** blurred vision (**spasm of accommodation**), **brow ache**, **lacrimation, salivation, sweating** (systemic if absorbed), **reduced night vision** (miosis).
- **Uses:** **glaucoma (acute angle-closure)**, **reversal of mydriasis** after eye examination, occasionally **xerostomia** (e.g., Sjögren's syndrome).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pilocarpine = direct M₃ agonist → miosis + ciliary contraction.
- ↑ conventional outflow via trabecular meshwork.
- Side effects: blurred vision, brow ache, poor night vision.
- Other uses: reversing mydriasis, xerostomia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is pilocarpine and what is its mechanism?</summary>

**Answer:** A direct-acting muscarinic agonist (cholinomimetic alkaloid) that stimulates M₃ receptors on the iris sphincter and ciliary muscle.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does pilocarpine lower IOP?</summary>

**Answer:** Ciliary muscle contraction opens the trabecular meshwork and increases aqueous outflow through the conventional pathway.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the side effects and other uses of pilocarpine.</summary>

**Answer:** Side effects: blurred vision, brow ache, lacrimation, salivation, sweating, reduced night vision. Uses: glaucoma, reversal of mydriasis after examination, xerostomia (e.g., Sjögren's).

**Why:** Slide 4.

## 4. Drugs that raise IOP and other miotics

- **Antimuscarinics** (parasympatholytics) **raise IOP:** block **M₃** on iris sphincter → **mydriasis** → **iris thickens at the iridocorneal angle and blocks drainage** → ↑ IOP, **especially dangerous in narrow-angle glaucoma.**
- **Contraindicated:** **atropine, tropicamide, cyclopentolate, scopolamine**; also **antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, antiparkinsonian drugs** (additional anticholinergic effects).
- **Other miotics that ↓ IOP:** **carbachol** (direct muscarinic agonist); **physostigmine, echothiophate** (indirect, **AChE inhibition**) → pupil constriction + ciliary contraction → ↑ outflow.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Antimuscarinic → mydriasis → angle blockage → ↑ IOP.
- Avoid atropine-like drugs in narrow-angle glaucoma.
- Other miotics: carbachol, physostigmine, echothiophate.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why do antimuscarinic drugs increase IOP?</summary>

**Answer:** They block M₃ receptors on the iris sphincter, causing mydriasis; the thickened iris blocks the drainage angle, raising IOP, especially in narrow-angle glaucoma.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give examples of drugs that are contraindicated in glaucoma.</summary>

**Answer:** Atropine, tropicamide, cyclopentolate, scopolamine; also antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics and antiparkinsonian drugs because of their anticholinergic effects.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which drugs besides pilocarpine cause miosis and lower IOP?</summary>

**Answer:** Carbachol (direct agonist), physostigmine and echothiophate (AChE inhibitors); they constrict the pupil, contract the ciliary muscle and increase outflow.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

## 5. AChE inhibitors and organophosphate poisoning

- **Therapeutic uses of AChE inhibitors:** **myasthenia gravis** (neostigmine, pyridostigmine); **glaucoma** (physostigmine, echothiophate); **Alzheimer's disease** (donepezil, rivastigmine); **reversal of competitive (non-depolarizing) neuromuscular block.**
- **Poisoning:** **organophosphate (OP)** insecticides (also nerve agents) **irreversibly inhibit AChE** → ACh accumulates → **cholinergic crisis** (nicotinic + muscarinic overstimulation). **SLUDGE:** salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI upset, emesis + **bradycardia, miosis, bronchospasm.**
- ⭐ **Treatment:** **atropine alone is not enough** (blocks muscarinic receptors only; does not reactivate AChE). **Second antidote: oximes** (**pralidoxime/2-PAM**, obidoxime) **reactivate phosphorylated AChE** by removing the OP group (**effective if given early**), restoring **nicotinic and muscarinic** function.
- **Avoid atropine/antimuscarinics in:** **narrow-angle glaucoma**, **prostatic hypertrophy** (urinary retention), **tachyarrhythmias**, **paralytic ileus.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- AChE inhibitors: myasthenia gravis, glaucoma, Alzheimer's, reversal of NMB.
- OP = irreversible AChE inhibition → cholinergic crisis (SLUDGE).
- Atropine + oxime (pralidoxime); oximes early.
- Avoid atropine in narrow-angle glaucoma, BPH, tachyarrhythmias, paralytic ileus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the therapeutic uses of AChE inhibitors?</summary>

**Answer:** Myasthenia gravis (neostigmine, pyridostigmine), glaucoma (physostigmine, echothiophate), Alzheimer's disease (donepezil, rivastigmine) and reversal of non-depolarizing neuromuscular block.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain organophosphate poisoning and why atropine alone is insufficient.</summary>

**Answer:** OP compounds irreversibly inhibit AChE, causing ACh accumulation and cholinergic crisis (SLUDGE, bradycardia, miosis, bronchospasm). Atropine blocks only muscarinic receptors and does not reactivate AChE, so an oxime (pralidoxime) is also needed.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. In which conditions should atropine-like drugs be avoided?</summary>

**Answer:** Narrow-angle glaucoma, prostatic hypertrophy, tachyarrhythmias and paralytic ileus.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

## 6. Patient's guide

- **General:** proper **instillation technique, adherence and regular follow-up** are needed for good IOP control; **wash hands with soap and water** before use and **keep the bottle tightly closed.**
- **Pilocarpine:** initial **brow ache** and **transient blurred vision**, especially in **low light.**
- **Latanoprost very common effects:** burning/itching; **permanent darkening of the iris**; **longer, thicker, darker eyelashes**; **darkening of the eyelid skin.** Others: blurred vision, watery eyes, redness, photophobia.
- **Do NOT:** let the **dropper tip touch** the eye, fingers or any surface; **wear contact lenses** (**benzalkonium chloride** is absorbed; **wait ≥ 15 minutes**); use drops that are **discolored or contain particles**; **drive/operate machinery** right after (blurry vision).
- **Formulations:** **pilocarpine HCl ophthalmic solution 2%** and **tablets 5 mg**; **latanoprost eye drops 0.005%** and a **topical dropper for hair growth 0.1% (for 6 months).**
- **Spelling to remember:** Pilocarpine, Latanoprost.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hygiene, no touching the dropper tip, lenses after 15 minutes.
- Latanoprost: permanent iris darkening and eyelash growth.
- Pilocarpine: brow ache and blurred vision in low light.
- Pilocarpine 2% drops; latanoprost 0.005% drops.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What would you tell the patient about using eye drops?</summary>

**Answer:** Wash hands, keep the bottle closed, do not touch the dropper tip to anything, do not use discolored drops, do not wear contact lenses (wait at least 15 minutes because of benzalkonium chloride), do not drive right after, and adhere to treatment with regular follow-up.

**Why:** Slide 2.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the very common side effects of latanoprost?</summary>

**Answer:** Burning or itching, permanent darkening of the iris, longer, thicker, darker eyelashes and darkening of the eyelid skin.

**Why:** Slide 2.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What side effects of pilocarpine drops should the patient expect, and in which formulations is it available?</summary>

**Answer:** Initial brow ache and transient blurred vision, particularly in low light. Available as 2% ophthalmic solution and 5 mg tablets.

**Why:** Slide 2.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
