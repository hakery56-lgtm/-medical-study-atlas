## 1. Morphology and classification

- ⭐ **Gram-positive spherical or ovoid cells in chains or pairs**; **non-spore-forming, non-motile**; some form **polysaccharide or hyaluronic acid capsules**; small non-pigmented colonies.
- **Facultative anaerobes** (peptostreptococci = **obligate anaerobes**); **catalase negative**; identified by **hemolysis on blood agar.**

| Hemolysis | Appearance | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Alpha (partial)** | **Green discoloration** | **S. pneumoniae, viridans group** |
| **Beta (complete)** | **Clear zone** | **Group A (S. pyogenes), group B (S. agalactiae)** |
| **Gamma (none)** | No hemolysis | **Group D (Enterococcus, E. faecalis)** |

- **Lancefield grouping:** by **species-specific C carbohydrate antigens** of the cell wall (groups A-H, K-V); **A, B, D** more frequent; **C, G, F** less; **S. pneumoniae and viridans are non-groupable.**
- **Group A** is subdivided by the **M protein (Griffith typing) into ~80 serotypes.**
- **Clinically important:** **S. pyogenes, S. agalactiae, viridans streptococci, S. pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Catalase negative separates streptococci from staphylococci.
- Alpha = green, beta = clear, gamma = none.
- Pneumococci and viridans are not Lancefield-groupable.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** Gram-positive spherical or ovoid cells in chains or pairs, non-spore-forming and non-motile, some capsulated, catalase negative, mostly facultative anaerobes.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

## 2. Streptococcus pyogenes (group A)

- ⭐ **Beta-hemolytic, group A, bacitracin-sensitive**; **hyaluronic acid capsule inhibits phagocytosis**; lives in **throat and nasopharynx (occasionally skin)**; main human pathogen with **invasion and post-streptococcal disorders.**

| Type | Diseases |
|---|---|
| **Pyogenic** | **Pharyngitis, cellulitis, impetigo (honey-crusted), erysipelas** |
| **Toxigenic** | **Scarlet fever, toxic shock-like syndrome, necrotizing fasciitis** |
| **Immunologic** | **Rheumatic fever, glomerulonephritis** |

- **Invasion:** **erysipelas, cellulitis, necrotizing fasciitis, puerperal fever, bacteremia/sepsis.** **Local:** **streptococcal sore throat (most common).** **Toxin-mediated:** **toxic shock syndrome, scarlet fever.** **Post-streptococcal:** **acute glomerulonephritis, rheumatic fever.**
- **Rheumatic fever:** follows pharyngitis in children; **carditis with valve damage, arthritis, chorea, fever.** **Acute glomerulonephritis:** **nephritis, hypertension, sometimes heart failure**; may become chronic → renal failure.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pyogenic, toxigenic and immunologic diseases.
- Rheumatic fever = carditis, arthritis, chorea, fever.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

**Answer:** Follows pharyngitis in children with carditis (possible extensive valve damage), arthritis, chorea and fever.

## 3. Diagnosis, treatment and epidemiology of S. pyogenes

- **Diagnosis:** **Gram stain** (Gram + cocci in chains); **culture** (beta-hemolytic on blood agar); **bacitracin sensitive** (S. pyogenes is the only sensitive streptococcus per the slide); **ASO test** (streptolysin O → antibody; indicates **previous pharyngeal infection**); **Dick test** (toxin-antitoxin neutralization for **scarlet fever immunity**: 0.1 mL toxin, redness > 10 mm in ~24 h = **no immunity**; **no longer used**).
- **Treatment:** **penicillin G**; allergic: **erythromycin or cephalexin**; **antibiotics do not affect established glomerulonephritis or rheumatic fever.**
- **Epidemiology:** **children 5-8 years**, **winter** (temperate), **crowding**, **closed communities** (boarding schools, army, camps).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ASO = previous pharyngeal infection; Dick test is obsolete.
- Penicillin G first-line; antibiotics don't reverse rheumatic fever or GN.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the laboratory tests for S. pyogenes.</summary>

**Answer:** Gram stain (Gram-positive cocci in chains), culture (beta-hemolysis), bacitracin sensitivity, ASO serology and the Dick test.

**Why:** Slides 18-19.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is S. pyogenes treated?</summary>

**Answer:** Penicillin G; erythromycin or cephalexin if allergic; antibiotics have no effect on established glomerulonephritis and rheumatic fever.

**Why:** Slide 21.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does the ASO test indicate?</summary>

**Answer:** A previous pharyngeal streptococcal infection (antibodies to streptolysin O).

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

## 4. Streptococcus pneumoniae

- ⭐ **Alpha-hemolytic, optochin-sensitive, lancet-shaped, encapsulated diplococci** (also short chains); **60-70% of bacterial pneumonias.**
- **Most common cause of:** **meningitis, otitis media (children), bacterial pneumonia, sinusitis.**
- **Culture:** **blood or chocolate agar**, **5-10% CO2**; **no catalase or peroxidases → dies in O2.**
- **Epidemiology:** **5-50% nasopharyngeal carriage, endogenous infection**; **delicate**; predisposed: **young children, elderly, immunocompromised, lung disease or viral infection, close quarters**; **aspiration → pneumonia** with **overwhelming inflammation**; **Eustachian tube → middle ear.**
- **Diagnosis:** **Gram stain**, **Quellung (capsular swelling) test**, **alpha-hemolysis, optochin sensitivity, bile solubility, inulin fermentation.**
- **Treatment and prevention:** **penicillin G or V** (rising resistance); **vaccines: capsular antigen (older adults and high risk, ~5 years)** and **conjugate (children 2-23 months).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Optochin sensitive, bile soluble, Quellung positive.
- Two vaccines: capsular polysaccharide and conjugate.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe S. pneumoniae.</summary>

**Answer:** Alpha-hemolytic, optochin-sensitive, encapsulated lancet-shaped diplococci causing 60-70% of bacterial pneumonias and most meningitis, otitis media and sinusitis.

**Why:** Slides 23 and 25.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is it identified?</summary>

**Answer:** Gram stain (presumptive), Quellung capsular swelling reaction, alpha-hemolysis with optochin sensitivity, bile solubility and inulin fermentation.

**Why:** Slide 30.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What vaccines exist?</summary>

**Answer:** A capsular antigen vaccine for older adults and high-risk people (effective about 5 years) and a conjugate vaccine for children aged 2 to 23 months.

**Why:** Slide 31.
</details>

## 5. Viridans and non-hemolytic streptococci

- **Viridans:** **optochin-resistant, alpha-hemolytic**; **normal oropharyngeal flora**; **dental caries (S. mutans, S. mitis)**; **subacute bacterial endocarditis on damaged valves (S. sanguinis).**
- **Gamma/non-hemolytic:** **S. bovis (non-enterococcus):** **cannot grow in 6.5% NaCl**, colonizes gut, bacteremia/endocarditis, **associated with colon cancer.**
- **Group D enterococci (E. faecalis):** **catalase negative, grow in 6.5% NaCl and bile**, normal colonic flora, **penicillin G resistant**, cause **UTI, biliary infections, subacute endocarditis after GI/GU procedures.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Viridans = caries and endocarditis; S. bovis = colon cancer; enterococci grow in 6.5% NaCl and bile.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What do viridans streptococci cause?</summary>

**Answer:** Dental caries (S. mutans, S. mitis) and subacute bacterial endocarditis on damaged valves (S. sanguinis).

**Why:** Slide 23.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare S. bovis and E. faecalis.</summary>

**Answer:** S. bovis cannot grow in 6.5% NaCl and is linked to colon cancer; E. faecalis grows in 6.5% NaCl and bile, is penicillin G resistant and causes UTI, biliary infections and endocarditis.

**Why:** Slide 24.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the hemolysis of enterococci?</summary>

**Answer:** Gamma (non-hemolytic).

**Why:** Slides 7-8 and 24.
