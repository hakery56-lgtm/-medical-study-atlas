## 1. Stem cells and hematopoiesis

- **The immune system has three parts:** **organs/tissues** (bone marrow, thymus, spleen, lymph nodes), **cells** (lymphocytes: T, B, NK; phagocytes: dendritic cells, macrophages, PMNs) and **molecules** (antibody, complement, cytokines, adhesion molecules).
- ⭐ **Stem cell** = can **divide, self-renew and differentiate** into specialized cells. **Potency:** totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent, oligopotent, unipotent.
- **Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs):** multipotent cells in bone marrow that make and repair **cartilage, bone and marrow fat.**
- ⭐ **Hematopoiesis:** all blood cells arise from the **hematopoietic stem cell (HSC)**, which splits into a **lymphoid progenitor** or a **myeloid progenitor**. Progenitors have **lost self-renewal** and are committed to a lineage. Regulated by genes such as **GATA-2.**

| Progenitor | Cells produced |
|---|---|
| **Lymphoid** | **B, T and NK cells** |
| **Myeloid** | **Erythrocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, monocytes, mast cells, dendritic cells, megakaryocytes (platelets)** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- HSC → lymphoid progenitor (B, T, NK) or myeloid progenitor (RBCs, granulocytes, monocytes, platelets...).
- Stem cells self-renew; progenitors do not.
- Pluripotent stem cell marker in the diagram: CD34+.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define hematopoiesis and name the two progenitor lines.</summary>

**Answer:** Formation of all blood cells from the hematopoietic stem cell, which differentiates into lymphoid and myeloid progenitor cells.

**Why:** Slides "Hematopoiesis" and "Lymphoid and myeloid progenitor cells".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which cells come from each progenitor?</summary>

**Answer:** Lymphoid: B, T and NK cells. Myeloid: erythrocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, monocytes, mast cells, dendritic cells and megakaryocytes.

**Why:** Slide "Lymphoid progenitor cells / Myeloid progenitor cells".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do stem cells differ from progenitor cells?</summary>

**Answer:** Stem cells self-renew to maintain their numbers; progenitor cells have lost self-renewal and are committed to one lineage.

**Why:** Slide "Hematopoiesis".
</details>

## 2. Lymphoid organs and bone marrow

| | **Primary (central)** | **Secondary (peripheral)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Organs** | **Thymus, bone marrow** | **Spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, appendix, Peyer's patches** |
| **Function** | **Immature lymphocytes develop**; stem cells differentiate, proliferate and mature into **immunocompetent cells** | **Antigen is localized** and exposed to mature lymphocytes; **adaptive response starts** |

- **Bone marrow:** generates **RBCs and WBCs** in childhood. **Red marrow** = active; **yellow marrow** = inactive, filled with fat. **75% of red marrow makes WBCs, 25% RBCs** (more RBCs circulate because their half-life is longer).
- **Colony stimulating factors** and other hormones/enzymes commit stem cells.
- **Bone marrow = site of B-cell maturation** and (secondary response) a **major source of plasma cells and antibody**; some lymphoid progenitors migrate to the **thymus** and become T cells.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Central organs: thymus + bone marrow. Peripheral: spleen, nodes, MALT.
- Red marrow 75% WBC / 25% RBC.
- B cells mature in bone marrow; T cells in the thymus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare primary and secondary lymphoid organs.</summary>

**Answer:** Primary organs (thymus, bone marrow) are where immature lymphocytes develop and mature; secondary organs (spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, appendix, Peyer's patches) are where antigen meets mature lymphocytes and adaptive responses begin.

**Why:** Slide "Primary and secondary lymphoid organs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are red and yellow bone marrow?</summary>

**Answer:** Red marrow is active and generates RBCs and WBCs; yellow marrow is inactive and filled with fat.

**Why:** Slide "Bone marrow".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are there more RBCs than WBCs in circulation though 75% of red marrow makes WBCs?</summary>

**Answer:** The half-life of RBCs is longer than that of WBCs.

**Why:** Slide "Bone marrow".
</details>

## 3. Thymus and DiGeorge syndrome

- **Location:** bilobed organ **between the sternum and the aorta**; connective tissue forms a **capsule.**
- ⭐ **Four cell types in the cortex:** **epithelial cells** (thymosin, thymopoietin, thymic humoral factors → T-cell maturation), **macrophage-like cells** (APCs rich in **class II HLA**; teach T cells), **nurse cells** (lymphocyte + cortical epithelial clusters), **T lymphocytes** (highly dividing; **95-99% lysed** by cortisone-like action; ~1% survive to the circulation/secondary organs or the medulla).
- The thymus is **antigen-independent** and works mainly in **fetal and early neonatal life**. **Congenital absence or thymectomy at birth** → almost **complete T-cell deficiency.**
- ⭐ **DiGeorge syndrome:** primary immunodeficiency: **T-cell deficiency, characteristic facies, congenital heart disease, hypocalcemia**; ~**90%** have a **22q11.2 deletion.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four thymic cortex cells: epithelial, macrophage-like, nurse cells, T lymphocytes.
- 95-99% of immature T cells die; ~1% mature.
- DiGeorge = 22q11.2 deletion: T-cell deficiency, facies, heart disease, hypocalcemia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name and describe the four cell types of the thymic cortex.</summary>

**Answer:** Epithelial cells (thymic hormones), macrophage-like cells (class II HLA-rich APCs that teach T cells), nurse cells (lymphocyte-epithelial clusters aiding differentiation) and T lymphocytes (highly dividing, mostly lysed).

**Why:** Slide "In the cortex we have 4 kinds of cells".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What happens to the great majority of immature T cells in the thymus?</summary>

**Answer:** 95-99% are lysed (cortisone-like action); about 1% go to the circulation and secondary organs or to the medulla to mature.

**Why:** Same slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is DiGeorge syndrome?</summary>

**Answer:** A primary T-cell immunodeficiency caused by early loss of the thymus (22q11.2 deletion in ~90%) with characteristic facies, congenital heart disease and hypocalcemia.

**Why:** Slide "As a primary lymphoid organ, the thymus...".
</details>

## 4. Lymph nodes, spleen and MALT

- **Lymph nodes:** oval, **few mm to 2 cm**, **500-600** in humans; swollen at **neck, axilla, groin.**
- ⭐ **Spleen:** responds to **blood-borne antigens.** **Red pulp** = sinusoids with macrophages, RBCs, few lymphocytes (**destroys old RBCs**). **White pulp** = around **splenic artery branches**; in the **marginal zone** interdigitating **dendritic cells** trap antigen and carry it to the **PALS**, where **T cells** surround the central arteriole.
- ⭐ **MALT:** lymphoid tissues of **digestive, respiratory and urogenital mucosa** (~**400 m²**, the major entry site); **tonsils, Peyer's patches, appendix.** Gut MALT must stay **unresponsive to food antigens.**

| MALT type | Features |
|---|---|
| **NALT** | Throat/nasal passages, **tonsils**; like lymph nodes but **not encapsulated, no lymphatics** |
| **GALT** | **Peyer's patches** (terminal ileum); **M cells + APCs** sample luminal antigen |
| **BALT** | Like Peyer's patches; lymphocyte follicles in **all lung lobes and main bronchi**; mostly **B cells** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Spleen: red pulp destroys old RBCs; white pulp (PALS, T cells) responds to blood antigens.
- MALT: NALT, GALT, BALT (+ genitourinary).
- M cells sample antigen in Peyer's patches.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the red and white pulp of the spleen.</summary>

**Answer:** Red pulp: sinusoids with macrophages, erythrocytes and few lymphocytes, removing old RBCs. White pulp: lymphoid tissue around splenic artery branches; antigen is trapped by interdigitating dendritic cells in the marginal zone and taken to the PALS where T cells lie.

**Why:** Slide "2-Spleen".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is MALT and how is it subdivided?</summary>

**Answer:** Organized lymphoid tissue defending mucosal surfaces: NALT, GALT, BALT and genitourinary-associated lymphoid tissue.

**Why:** Slide "3-Mucosal associated lymphoid tissue".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are M cells?</summary>

**Answer:** Specialized epithelial cells in GALT (Peyer's patches) that, with APCs, sample antigen from the gut lumen and deliver it to the lymphoid tissue.

**Why:** Slide "2-Gut-associated lymphoid tissue".
</details>

## 5. Lymphocytes: T cells, B cells and NK cells

- **Lymphocytes** drive **adaptive immunity**: diversity, specificity, memory, self/non-self recognition.
- **T cells:** **65-80%** of lymphocytes; mature in the **thymus**; **no morphologic difference** from B cells; **TCR** sees antigen on **MHC.**
- **B cells:** named for the **bursa of Fabricius** (birds); mature in **bone marrow** (humans); **BCR = membrane immunoglobulin.** After antigen: **plasma cells** (secrete antibody, end-stage) and **memory B cells.**

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **CD2, CD3, CD5** | **Pan-T markers** |
| **CD4 / CD8** | **Mature subclasses** (MHC restricted; **never both**) |
| **CD45** | Common leukocyte antigen |
| **CD28** | Binds **B7 on APCs**; needed for T-cell proliferation after priming |
| **CD16, CD56** | **NK cells** |

- **TCR:** Fab-like **heterodimer** (disulfide-linked), **αβ (TCR2) in > 95%**, γδ (TCR1); non-covalently associated with **CD3.**
- **T subpopulations:** **TH (CD4), TC (CD8), Treg**; **TH:TC ≈ 2:1.** **TH1** → IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12, TNF (cell-mediated); **TH2** → IL-4, IL-5, IL-10 (humoral, B cells → plasma cells).
- **CTLA-4:** checkpoint "brake" on T cells that prevents autoimmunity.
- ⭐ **NK cells:** **5-10%** of blood lymphocytes; kill tumor and virus-infected cells **without prior immunization.** They recognize targets by **reduced class I MHC** (killer inhibitory receptor) and by **CD16 binding IgG Fc → ADCC.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- B cell → plasma cell + memory cell; T cell: CD4 (helper) or CD8 (cytotoxic).
- TH1 = cell-mediated (IFN-γ, IL-2); TH2 = humoral (IL-4, IL-5, IL-10).
- NK cells: reduced MHC I or ADCC via CD16.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare TH1 and TH2 cells.</summary>

**Answer:** TH1 secrete IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 and TNF for the cell-mediated response; TH2 secrete IL-4, IL-5 and IL-10 that drive B cells into plasma cells for the humoral response.

**Why:** Slide "Th-helper cells".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the TCR and its associated molecule.</summary>

**Answer:** An antigen-specific heterodimer similar to an antibody Fab (αβ in more than 95%, γδ in others) linked by disulfide bonds and associated non-covalently with CD3; the TCR-CD3 complex contacts antigen and MHC.

**Why:** Slide "T-cell receptors (TCRs)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do NK cells recognize targets and what is ADCC?</summary>

**Answer:** They detect reduced class I MHC (via killer inhibitory receptor) or antibody-coated cells through CD16 (Fc receptor for IgG), killing them by antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

**Why:** Slides "Natural killer cells" and "ADCC".
</details>

## 6. Mononuclear phagocytes, granulocytes and dendritic cells

- **Mononuclear phagocytes** = **monocytes (blood) + macrophages (tissue).** Most potent activator: **IFN-γ** (from activated TH cells); activated macrophages express more **class II MHC** and secrete **IL-1, TNF-α, IL-6**, complement and lysosomal enzymes.

| Tissue | Macrophage-like cell |
|---|---|
| Lung | **Alveolar macrophages** |
| Connective tissue | **Histiocytes** |
| Liver | **Kupffer cells** |
| Kidney | **Mesangial cells** |
| Brain | **Microglial cells** |
| Bone | **Osteoclasts** |

- **Neutrophils:** first to arrive; **leukocytosis** = rise in circulating neutrophils; **extravasation** = adhere to endothelium → squeeze between endothelial cells → cross basement membrane; **larger respiratory burst than macrophages.**
- **Eosinophils:** defense against **parasites.** **Basophils:** non-phagocytic, **allergy.** **Mast cells:** differentiate in tissue; **allergy.**
- **Dendritic cells:** **antigen presentation to TH cells**; four types: **Langerhans, interstitial, myeloid, lymphoid**; constitutively high **class II MHC + B7** → **more potent APCs** than macrophages and B cells.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Macrophage names by organ (alveolar, histiocyte, Kupffer, mesangial, microglia, osteoclast).
- Neutrophil extravasation: adhere, cross endothelium, cross basement membrane.
- DCs are the most potent APCs (constitutive MHC II and B7).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name the macrophage-like cells of six tissues.</summary>

**Answer:** Alveolar macrophages (lung), histiocytes (connective tissue), Kupffer cells (liver), mesangial cells (kidney), microglia (brain), osteoclasts (bone).

**Why:** Slide "Macrophages".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe extravasation of neutrophils.</summary>

**Answer:** The neutrophil adheres to the endothelium, passes between endothelial cells, then crosses the basement membrane into the tissue, guided by chemotactic factors such as complement components.

**Why:** Slide "Granulocytic cells: neutrophils".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are dendritic cells better antigen-presenting cells than macrophages and B cells?</summary>

**Answer:** They constitutively express high levels of class II MHC and B7 co-stimulatory molecules; macrophages and B cells need activation first.

**Why:** Slide "Dendritic cell (DC)".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Thymus = T-cell maturation; bone marrow = B-cell maturation (in humans).**
- **CD4 and CD8 are never on the same mature T cell.**
- **TH1 → cell-mediated; TH2 → humoral.**
- **NK cells kill when MHC class I is reduced or via CD16 (ADCC).**
- **DiGeorge = 22q11.2 deletion with hypocalcemia and heart disease.**
- **95-99% of immature thymocytes die in the cortex.**
