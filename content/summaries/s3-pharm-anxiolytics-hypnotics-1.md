## 1. Anxiety overview and the drug list

- ⭐ **Anxiety** = unpleasant state of **tension, apprehension or uneasiness** (fear from a known or unknown source). Severe anxiety gives **tachycardia, sweating, trembling, palpitations** = **sympathetic activation.**
- **Mild anxiety** is common and **needs no treatment**; **severe, chronic, debilitating anxiety** may need **antianxiety drugs (anxiolytics)** and/or **psychotherapy.**
- Many antianxiety drugs also **sedate**, so they act as **anxiolytic and hypnotic (sleep-inducing)** agents. Some **antidepressants** treat certain anxiety disorders.
- **Benzodiazepines** largely **replaced barbiturates** (safer, more effective) but are **not always the best choice**: **SSRIs** are preferred in many cases; **nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics and antihistamines** may suit insomnia.
- **High therapeutic index:** the **lethal dose is over 1000-fold the typical therapeutic dose** (diazepam has the highest lethal/effective ratio versus morphine, chlorpromazine, phenobarbital).

| Group | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Benzodiazepines** | Alprazolam (Xanax), chlordiazepoxide (Librium), clonazepam (Klonopin), clorazepate (Tranxene), diazepam (Valium), estazolam, flurazepam, lorazepam (Ativan), midazolam, oxazepam, quazepam (Doral), temazepam (Restoril), triazolam (Halcion) |
| **Other anxiolytics** | Antidepressants, buspirone, meprobamate |
| **Barbiturates** | Amobarbital, pentobarbital, phenobarbital, secobarbital |
| **Other hypnotics** | Antihistamines, doxepin, eszopiclone, ramelteon, suvorexant, tasimelteon, zaleplon, zolpidem |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Anxiety = tension and apprehension with sympathetic symptoms; only severe or chronic anxiety is treated.
- Benzodiazepines replaced barbiturates, but SSRIs are often preferred.
- Benzodiazepines have a wide safety margin (lethal dose over 1000-fold the therapeutic dose).

</div>

## 2. Mechanism of action and pharmacological effects

- **Target:** **GABAA receptors**, a **pentamer: α (2), β (2), γ (1)** spanning the postsynaptic membrane (many subtypes, e.g. six α subtypes).
- **GABA binding** opens the **chloride channel** → **Cl⁻ influx** → **hyperpolarisation (IPSP)** → decreased neurotransmission.
- ⭐ **Benzodiazepines bind a high-affinity site distinct from GABA's, at the α/γ interface**, and **increase the frequency of channel opening** produced by GABA. Clinical effects correlate with **binding affinity** for the receptor-channel complex.

| Effect | Subunit / site |
|---|---|
| **Anxiolytic** (limbic system) | **α2** |
| **Sedative / hypnotic** | **α1** |
| **Anterograde amnesia** | **α1** |
| **Anticonvulsant** | partly **α1** |
| **Muscle relaxant** (high doses) | **α2**, presynaptic inhibition in the spinal cord |

- **Anterograde amnesia** = temporary memory impairment; ability to learn and form new memories is impaired.
- **Baclofen** is a muscle relaxant believed to act on **GABAB** receptors at the spinal cord.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- GABAA pentamer (α2 β2 γ1); benzodiazepines bind at the α/γ interface and increase the frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening.
- α2 = anxiolysis and muscle relaxation; α1 = sedation, amnesia, anticonvulsant.
- Baclofen acts on GABAB, not GABAA.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanism of action of benzodiazepines.</summary>

**Answer:** They bind the α/γ interface of the GABAA receptor (distinct from the GABA site) and increase the frequency of GABA-induced chloride channel opening, causing hyperpolarisation and reduced neuronal firing.

**Why:** Slides 7-8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which subunits mediate which effects?</summary>

**Answer:** α2: anxiolysis (limbic system) and muscle relaxation (spinal cord); α1: sedation or hypnosis, anterograde amnesia and part of the anticonvulsant effect.

**Why:** Slides 10-12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is anterograde amnesia?</summary>

**Answer:** Temporary impairment of memory with benzodiazepines: the ability to learn and form new memories is impaired (mediated by α1-GABAA receptors).

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

## 3. Therapeutic uses

- **Anxiety disorders:** **panic disorder, GAD, social anxiety, performance anxiety, extreme phobias** (e.g. fear of flying); also anxiety with **depression and schizophrenia.** Reserve for **severe anxiety**, **not everyday stress**; use **short periods** (addictive).
- **Longer-acting agents (clonazepam, lorazepam, diazepam)** are preferred for prolonged treatment. **Antianxiety effects show less tolerance than sedative/hypnotic effects.** **Tolerance** = decreased responsiveness after **1 to 2 weeks.**
- ⭐ **Panic disorder: alprazolam** (short- and long-term) but **withdrawal in about 30%.**
- **Sleep disorders:** ↓ sleep latency, ↑ **stage II non-REM**, ↓ **REM** and **slow-wave** sleep; balance bedtime sedation against **"hangover".**
  - **Triazolam** (short-acting): trouble **falling asleep**; higher risk of **withdrawal and rebound insomnia.**
  - **Temazepam** (intermediate): **frequent awakenings**; take **1 to 2 h before bed.**
  - **Flurazepam** (long): rarely used, **daytime sedation**, especially in the elderly.
  - Hypnotics for a **limited time: 1 to 3 weeks.**
- **Amnesia / premedication:** shorter-acting agents for **endoscopy, dental procedures, angioplasty** (**conscious sedation**); **midazolam** gives anterograde amnesia and sedation before anesthesia.
- **Seizures:** **clonazepam** adjunct; **lorazepam and diazepam** = drugs of choice for **status epilepticus.** **Chlordiazepoxide, clorazepate, diazepam, lorazepam, oxazepam** for **acute alcohol withdrawal** (reduce withdrawal seizures).
- **Muscular disorders:** **diazepam** for **skeletal muscle spasm and spasticity** (multiple sclerosis, cerebral palsy).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Anxiety: long-acting agents for prolonged use; alprazolam for panic disorder.
- Insomnia: triazolam (falling asleep), temazepam (staying asleep), flurazepam rarely.
- Midazolam for amnesia; lorazepam or diazepam for status epilepticus; diazepam for spasticity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Which benzodiazepine is chosen for each type of insomnia?</summary>

**Answer:** Triazolam (short-acting) for difficulty falling asleep, temazepam (intermediate) for frequent awakenings, flurazepam rarely because its long half-life causes daytime sedation.

**Why:** Slides 16-17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the uses of benzodiazepines other than anxiety and sleep.</summary>

**Answer:** Amnesia and conscious sedation (midazolam), seizures (clonazepam adjunct; lorazepam and diazepam for status epilepticus), alcohol withdrawal, and muscle spasm or spasticity (diazepam).

**Why:** Slides 18-19.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define tolerance and say which benzodiazepine effects show less of it.</summary>

**Answer:** Decreased responsiveness to repeated doses after more than 1 to 2 weeks; the antianxiety effects are less subject to tolerance than the sedative and hypnotic effects.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

## 4. Pharmacokinetics, dependence, adverse effects and flumazenil

- **Absorption:** **lipophilic**, **rapidly and completely absorbed orally**, widely distributed, enter the CNS.
- **Duration** (half-life decides usefulness); **longer-acting agents form active metabolites.**

| Group | Duration | Drugs |
|---|---|---|
| **Long-acting** | 1-3 days | Clorazepate, chlordiazepoxide, diazepam, flurazepam, quazepam |
| **Intermediate** | 10-20 h | Estazolam, lorazepam, temazepam |
| **Short-acting** | 3-8 h | Alprazolam, midazolam, oxazepam, triazolam |

- **Fate:** hepatic **microsomal** metabolism to **active** compounds (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam); excreted in urine as **glucuronides or oxidised metabolites.**
- **Pregnancy:** all **cross the placenta** and may **depress the newborn CNS** → not recommended; also present in **breast milk.**
- **Dependence:** psychological and physical dependence with **high doses for a prolonged period**; all are **controlled substances.** **Abrupt withdrawal:** confusion, anxiety, agitation, restlessness, insomnia, tension, rarely **seizures.** **Short half-life (triazolam)** = more abrupt, severe withdrawal than slowly eliminated **flurazepam.**
- **Adverse effects:** **drowsiness and confusion** (most common); **ataxia** at high doses (no driving); **cognitive impairment**; **caution in liver disease.** **Alcohol and other CNS depressants** enhance sedation; **overdose seldom lethal** unless with alcohol or opioids.
- ⭐ **Flumazenil:** **GABA receptor antagonist** that rapidly reverses benzodiazepines; **IV only**; rapid onset, **short half-life (about 1 h)** so repeat dosing may be needed. May precipitate **withdrawal** in dependent patients or **seizures** (if benzodiazepine controlled seizures, or **tricyclic antidepressant or antipsychotic** co-ingestion). Common adverse effects: **dizziness, nausea, vomiting, agitation.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Lipophilic, well absorbed; duration groups: long (1-3 days), intermediate (10-20 h), short (3-8 h).
- Short half-life drugs (triazolam) give the worst withdrawal; flurazepam the mildest.
- Flumazenil: IV antagonist, half-life about 1 h; can precipitate withdrawal or seizures.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify benzodiazepines by duration of action with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Long (1-3 days): clorazepate, chlordiazepoxide, diazepam, flurazepam, quazepam. Intermediate (10-20 h): estazolam, lorazepam, temazepam. Short (3-8 h): alprazolam, midazolam, oxazepam, triazolam.

**Why:** Slides 21-22.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe benzodiazepine dependence and withdrawal.</summary>

**Answer:** Psychological and physical dependence follow prolonged high doses; abrupt stopping causes confusion, anxiety, agitation, restlessness, insomnia, tension and rarely seizures; short half-life drugs such as triazolam cause more severe withdrawal than flurazepam.

**Why:** Slides 24-25.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is flumazenil and what are its cautions?</summary>

**Answer:** An IV-only GABA receptor antagonist reversing benzodiazepines with a short half-life (about 1 h); it may precipitate withdrawal in dependent patients and seizures with benzodiazepine seizure control or mixed tricyclic or antipsychotic ingestion.

**Why:** Slides 28-29.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **α2 = anxiolysis and muscle relaxation; α1 = sedation, amnesia, anticonvulsant.**
- **Benzodiazepines increase the frequency (not the duration) of chloride channel opening.**
- **Baclofen acts on GABAB, not GABAA.**
- **Triazolam: short-acting, worst withdrawal and rebound insomnia; flurazepam: long-acting, hangover.**
- **Lorazepam and diazepam are the drugs of choice in status epilepticus.**
- **Flumazenil: IV only, short half-life, may precipitate withdrawal or seizures.**
- **Overdose is seldom lethal unless alcohol or opioids are co-ingested.**
