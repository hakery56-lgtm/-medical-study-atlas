# Anatomy of the Arm I — Exam Night Review

This lecture covers the arm: its bones, deep fascia and compartments, the muscles of the front and back of the arm, the superficial veins, the brachial artery and the anastomosis around the elbow. After each topic you get **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions. Tap a question to reveal its answer, but try answering first.

---

## 1. Bones & deep fascia — how the arm is divided

### Bony landmarks (from the figure slides)
- The **medial epicondyle** of the humerus is **more prominent** than the lateral epicondyle (and it is the site of **golfer's elbow**).
- The **medial part of the trochlea** is more prominent than the lateral part.
- The **olecranon fossa** (posterior surface) is **deeper** than the radial and coronoid fossae (anterior surface).
- Ulna: the **trochlear notch faces forwards**, with the **radial notch on the lateral side**.

### Deep fascia and compartments
- The arm is enclosed in a **sheath of deep fascia**.
- From it, **two intermuscular septa** (medial and lateral) extend to the **medial and lateral supracondylar ridges** of the humerus.
- These septa divide the arm into **anterior** and **posterior fascial compartments**.

| Compartment | Muscles | Nerve | Artery |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior** | biceps brachii, coracobrachialis, brachialis | **musculocutaneous** | **brachial** |
| **Posterior** | triceps brachii | **radial** | **profunda brachii** + **ulnar collateral arteries** (branches of brachial) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Deep fascia sends medial and lateral intermuscular septa to the supracondylar ridges → anterior and posterior compartments.
- Anterior compartment: 3 muscles, musculocutaneous nerve, brachial artery.
- Posterior compartment: triceps, radial nerve, profunda brachii + ulnar collateral arteries.
- Medial epicondyle is more prominent (golfer's elbow); olecranon fossa is the deepest fossa.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is the arm divided into compartments?</summary>

**Answer:** The arm is enclosed in a sheath of deep fascia, from which two fascial intermuscular septa (medial and lateral) extend to the medial and lateral supracondylar ridges of the humerus. These septa divide the arm into anterior and posterior fascial compartments.

**Why:** Fascia → septa → attachment → two compartments is the full chain.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name the contents (muscles, nerve, artery) of the anterior compartment of the arm.</summary>

**Answer:** Three muscles — biceps brachii, coracobrachialis and brachialis; the nerve is the musculocutaneous nerve; the artery is the brachial artery.

**Why:** A classic "contents of a compartment" question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the nerve and arteries of the posterior compartment of the arm?</summary>

**Answer:** The radial nerve; the arteries are the profunda brachii and the ulnar collateral arteries, which are branches of the brachial artery.

**Why:** Pairs with Q2 — learn both compartments together.
</details>

## 2. Biceps brachii

### Origin — two heads
- **Long head** (lateral — "**L for L**"): from the **supraglenoid tubercle**, **intracapsular but extrasynovial**. It emerges at the top of the **intertubercular sulcus**, surrounded by a **tube-like extension of synovial membrane**.
- **Short head** (medial): from the **tip of the coracoid process**, in a **common tendon with coracobrachialis**.
- The muscle lies **in front of brachialis**, with the **musculocutaneous nerve sandwiched between them**.

### Insertion
- **Posterior part of the radial tuberosity** — a **bursa** lies between the tendon and the tuberosity.
- **Bicipital aponeurosis**: an extension from the tendon that passes **medially** to blend with the **deep fascia of the medial side of the forearm**.

### Actions — two main + one minor
1. **Powerful supinator** of the forearm (because it inserts on the **posterior** aspect of the radial tuberosity) — supination of the **flexed, pronated** forearm.
2. **Flexor of the forearm** (elbow) — flexion of the **supinated** forearm.
3. **Minor (long head)**: aids **flexion of the shoulder** and is an important factor in the **stability of the abducted shoulder**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Biceps is the **most powerful supinator**, but the **main flexor of the elbow is brachialis**, not biceps. And the long head is **intracapsular but extrasynovial** — not intrasynovial.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Long head: supraglenoid tubercle, intracapsular extrasynovial, in the intertubercular sulcus with a synovial sheath.
- Short head: coracoid process, common tendon with coracobrachialis.
- Insertion: posterior radial tuberosity (+ bursa) and bicipital aponeurosis to forearm deep fascia.
- Actions: powerful supinator, elbow flexor, long head flexes & stabilizes the abducted shoulder.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the origin of biceps brachii.</summary>

**Answer:** It has two heads. The long (lateral) head arises from the supraglenoid tubercle — intracapsular but extrasynovial — and emerges at the top of the intertubercular sulcus surrounded by a tube-like extension of synovial membrane. The short (medial) head arises from the tip of the coracoid process in a common tendon with coracobrachialis.

**Why:** Both heads with their exact landmarks and the intracapsular/extrasynovial detail.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is biceps brachii a powerful supinator?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it inserts onto the posterior aspect of the radial tuberosity; when it contracts on a pronated, flexed forearm it rotates the radius, producing powerful supination.

**Why:** The insertion site explains the action — examiners love "why" questions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the actions of biceps brachii.</summary>

**Answer:** Main: powerful supinator of the forearm (supination of the flexed pronated forearm) and flexor of the forearm (flexion of the supinated forearm). Minor: its long head aids flexion of the shoulder and is an important factor in stabilizing the abducted shoulder.

**Why:** Two main + one minor, as the lecture states.
</details>

## 3. Brachialis & coracobrachialis

### Brachialis
- **Origin**: front of the **lower half of the humerus** + the **medial and lateral intermuscular septa**.
- **Insertion**: **coronoid process** and **tuberosity of the ulna**.
- **Action**: passes in front of the elbow → **main (powerful) flexor of the elbow**.
- ⭐ **Double nerve supply**: **musculocutaneous** nerve + the **lateral ½ is supplied by the radial nerve**.

### Coracobrachialis
- A **small** muscle.
- **Origin**: **tip of the coracoid process**, in common with the short head of biceps.
- **Insertion**: **middle of the medial border (surface) of the humerus** — opposite the insertion of **deltoid** at the middle of the lateral border.
- **Action**: **weak flexor and adductor of the shoulder**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** All front-of-arm muscles are supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve **except the lateral half of brachialis (radial nerve)**. Biceps inserts on the **radius**; brachialis inserts on the **ulna**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Brachialis: lower ½ humerus + septa → coronoid process & ulnar tuberosity; main elbow flexor; double supply (musculocutaneous + radial).
- Coracobrachialis: coracoid tip (with short head of biceps) → middle of medial humerus (opposite deltoid insertion); weak flexor & adductor of the shoulder.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe brachialis: origin, insertion, action and nerve supply.</summary>

**Answer:** Origin from the front of the lower half of the humerus and the medial and lateral intermuscular septa; insertion into the coronoid process and tuberosity of the ulna; it passes in front of the elbow so it is the main powerful flexor of the elbow; it has a double nerve supply — musculocutaneous nerve plus the radial nerve to its lateral half.

**Why:** "Double nerve supply" is the mark-winning detail.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which muscle is the main flexor of the elbow and why?</summary>

**Answer:** Brachialis, because it passes directly in front of the elbow joint from the lower half of the humerus to the coronoid process and tuberosity of the ulna.

**Why:** A frequent one-liner — don't answer biceps.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe coracobrachialis.</summary>

**Answer:** A small muscle arising from the tip of the coracoid process in common with the short head of biceps, inserted into the middle of the medial border of the humerus (opposite the deltoid insertion laterally). It is a weak flexor and adductor of the shoulder and is supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve.

**Why:** Origin + insertion + action + nerve = full answer.
</details>

## 4. Triceps brachii & its bursae

### Origin — three heads
| Head | Origin |
|---|---|
| **Long** | **infraglenoid tubercle** of the scapula (**extracapsular**) |
| **Lateral** | posterior humerus **above** the radial (spiral) groove |
| **Medial** | posterior humerus **below** the radial groove (+ intermuscular septum); lies **deepest**, "comparable to brachialis" |

- **Insertion**: **superior surface of the olecranon** of the ulna.
- **Nerve**: **radial nerve** — the **long head is supplied in the axilla**; the **lateral and medial heads in the spiral groove**.
- **Actions**: **main extensor of the elbow**; the long head aids **extension of the shoulder** and **stabilizes the abducted shoulder**.

### Bursae related to the triceps tendon
1. **Subcutaneous (olecranon) bursa** — in the subcutaneous tissue.
2. **Subtendinous olecranon bursa** — between the tendon and the olecranon.

⭐ **Student's elbow**: inflammation of the **subcutaneous bursa** from **repeated friction** (leaning on the elbows).

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Biceps long head = **supraglenoid**, **intracapsular**. Triceps long head = **infraglenoid**, **extracapsular**. The radial nerve runs in the radial groove **between** the lateral (above) and medial (below) heads.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Triceps: long head infraglenoid (extracapsular); lateral head above, medial head below the radial groove.
- Insertion: olecranon; nerve: radial (long head in axilla, other heads in spiral groove).
- Main elbow extensor; long head extends and stabilizes the abducted shoulder.
- Two bursae: subcutaneous and subtendinous; student's elbow = inflamed subcutaneous bursa.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the origin of triceps brachii.</summary>

**Answer:** Long head from the infraglenoid tubercle of the scapula (extracapsular); lateral head from the posterior surface of the humerus above the radial groove; medial head from the posterior surface of the humerus below the radial groove and the intermuscular septum — it lies in a deeper plane, comparable to brachialis.

**Why:** Three heads, each with its relation to the radial groove.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is student's elbow?</summary>

**Answer:** Inflammation of the subcutaneous olecranon bursa (in the subcutaneous tissue over the olecranon) caused by repeated friction.

**Why:** A classic applied-anatomy definition.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the nerve supply and actions of triceps.</summary>

**Answer:** Radial nerve — the long head is supplied in the axilla, the lateral and medial heads in the spiral groove. Actions: main extensor of the elbow; the long head aids extension of the shoulder and stabilizes the abducted shoulder.

**Why:** The level at which each head is supplied matters clinically (radial nerve injury in the spiral groove spares the long head).
</details>

## 5. Superficial veins & the brachial artery

### Superficial veins
- The **dorsal venous arch** of the hand gives the **basilic vein** (medial side) and the **cephalic vein** (lateral side).
- **Cephalic vein**: runs in the **deltopectoral groove** and pierces the **clavipectoral fascia** to join the **axillary vein**.
- **Basilic vein**: pierces the deep fascia in the **medial mid-arm**, joins the **venae comitantes**, and at the **inferior border of teres major** becomes the **axillary vein**.

### Brachial artery
- **Beginning**: continuation of the **axillary artery** at the **lower border of teres major**.
- **End**: divides into **radial and ulnar arteries** in the **cubital fossa**, in front of the elbow, **opposite the neck of the radius**.
- **Course**: on the **medial border of brachialis**; **superficial** throughout (just deep to the deep fascia); accompanied by **venae comitantes** (brachial veins).
- **Relation to the median nerve**: **lateral** to the artery in the axilla → **crosses in front** of it in the middle of the arm → **medial** to it at the elbow.

### ⭐ Why is the brachial artery used to measure blood pressure?
1. It is **superficial** in front of the elbow, and its pulsation is easily felt **just medial to the biceps tendon**.
2. The hollow of the **cubital fossa** gives a flat area to place the stethoscope.

### Branches
1. **Profunda brachii** — the **first and largest** branch; runs in the **radial groove with the radial nerve**; divides into **anterior and posterior descending branches** (join the elbow anastomosis).
2. **Superior ulnar collateral** — arises about the **middle of the arm**, accompanies the **ulnar nerve behind the medial epicondyle**.
3. **Inferior ulnar collateral** — arises **just above the elbow**, passes **in front of the medial epicondyle**.
- Plus muscular and nutrient branches.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Brachial artery: from axillary at lower border of teres major → divides in the cubital fossa opposite the neck of radius.
- Median nerve: lateral → anterior (mid-arm) → medial to the artery.
- Used for BP: superficial, felt medial to the biceps tendon, flat cubital fossa for the stethoscope.
- Branches: profunda brachii (first/largest, with radial nerve), superior ulnar collateral (with ulnar nerve, behind medial epicondyle), inferior ulnar collateral (in front of medial epicondyle).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the beginning, course and termination of the brachial artery.</summary>

**Answer:** It begins as the continuation of the axillary artery at the lower border of teres major, runs superficially (just deep to the deep fascia) on the medial border of brachialis accompanied by venae comitantes, and ends in the cubital fossa in front of the elbow, opposite the neck of the radius, by dividing into the radial and ulnar arteries.

**Why:** Beginning–course–end is the standard artery question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the relation of the median nerve to the brachial artery.</summary>

**Answer:** In the axilla the median nerve is formed lateral to the axillary artery; in the middle of the arm it crosses anterior to the brachial artery; at the elbow it lies medial to the artery.

**Why:** Lateral → anterior → medial, in order.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the brachial artery commonly used to record blood pressure?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it is superficial in front of the elbow with pulsations easily felt just medial to the biceps tendon, and the triangular hollow of the cubital fossa provides a flat area to place the stethoscope over the artery.

**Why:** The lecturer's own question with its two reasons.
</details>

## 6. Anastomosis around the elbow

Formed by **8 branches**: **4 descending** arteries from above and **4 recurrent** arteries from below.
- **Descending**: anterior & posterior descending branches of the **profunda brachii**, **superior** and **inferior ulnar collateral** arteries.
- **Recurrent**: from the **radial**, **ulnar** and **posterior interosseous** arteries.

| Around | Position | Recurrent (from below) | Anastomoses with (from above) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Medial epicondyle** | anterior | **anterior ulnar recurrent** (ulnar a.) | **inferior ulnar collateral** |
| **Medial epicondyle** | posterior | **posterior ulnar recurrent** (ulnar a.) | **superior ulnar collateral** |
| **Lateral epicondyle** | anterior | **radial recurrent** (radial a.) | **anterior descending** branch of profunda brachii |
| **Lateral epicondyle** | posterior | **posterior interosseous recurrent** (posterior interosseous a.) | **posterior descending** branch of profunda brachii |

> 💡 **Memory aid:** Anterior pairs with anterior, posterior with posterior. The **inferior** ulnar collateral lies **in front** of the medial epicondyle (anterior ulnar recurrent); the **superior** one lies **behind** it with the ulnar nerve (posterior ulnar recurrent).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 8 branches: 4 descending (2 profunda branches + 2 ulnar collaterals) + 4 recurrent (radial, 2 ulnar, posterior interosseous).
- Medial: anterior ulnar recurrent ↔ inferior ulnar collateral; posterior ulnar recurrent ↔ superior ulnar collateral.
- Lateral: radial recurrent ↔ anterior descending profunda; posterior interosseous recurrent ↔ posterior descending profunda.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Enumerate the branches forming the anastomosis around the elbow.</summary>

**Answer:** Eight branches. Descending: anterior and posterior descending branches of the profunda brachii, the superior ulnar collateral and the inferior ulnar collateral arteries. Recurrent: the radial recurrent (radial artery), anterior and posterior ulnar recurrent (ulnar artery) and posterior interosseous recurrent (posterior interosseous artery).

**Why:** Four from above + four from below.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the anastomosis around the medial epicondyle.</summary>

**Answer:** Anteriorly, the anterior ulnar recurrent artery (from the ulnar artery) ascends in front of the medial epicondyle to anastomose with the inferior ulnar collateral artery. Posteriorly, the posterior ulnar recurrent artery (from the ulnar artery) ascends behind the medial epicondyle to anastomose with the superior ulnar collateral artery.

**Why:** Anterior with inferior, posterior with superior.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the anastomosis around the lateral epicondyle.</summary>

**Answer:** Anteriorly, the radial recurrent artery (from the radial artery) ascends in front of the lateral epicondyle to anastomose with the anterior descending branch of the profunda brachii. Posteriorly, the posterior interosseous recurrent artery ascends behind the lateral epicondyle to anastomose with the posterior descending branch of the profunda brachii.

**Why:** Both lateral pairs join branches of the profunda brachii.
</details>

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. Deep fascia + **medial & lateral intermuscular septa** → **anterior** (musculocutaneous n., brachial a.) and **posterior** (radial n., profunda brachii) compartments.
2. **Biceps**: long head **supraglenoid, intracapsular extrasynovial**; short head **coracoid** (with coracobrachialis); inserts **posterior radial tuberosity** + **bicipital aponeurosis**.
3. Biceps actions: **powerful supinator**, elbow flexor, long head **flexes & stabilizes the abducted shoulder**.
4. **Brachialis**: lower ½ humerus → **coronoid process & ulnar tuberosity**; **main elbow flexor**; **double supply** (musculocutaneous + radial).
5. **Coracobrachialis**: coracoid → **middle of medial humerus** (opposite deltoid); weak flexor & adductor of the shoulder.
6. **Triceps**: long **infraglenoid (extracapsular)**, lateral **above**, medial **below** the radial groove; **olecranon**; radial nerve; main elbow extensor.
7. **Student's elbow** = inflamed **subcutaneous olecranon bursa**.
8. **Cephalic** → deltopectoral groove → clavipectoral fascia → axillary v.; **basilic** → pierces fascia mid-arm → axillary v. at teres major.
9. **Brachial artery**: lower border of **teres major** → cubital fossa **opposite the neck of the radius**; median nerve **lateral → anterior → medial**.
10. BP: superficial, **medial to biceps tendon**, flat **cubital fossa**.
11. **Profunda brachii** = first & largest branch, **with the radial nerve** in the radial groove.
12. Elbow anastomosis: **8 branches**; ulnar recurrents ↔ ulnar collaterals (medial), radial & posterior interosseous recurrents ↔ profunda branches (lateral).
