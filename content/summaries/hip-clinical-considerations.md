## 1. Femoral neck angle and iliotibial band syndrome

- **Femoral neck angle** = angle between the **shaft and neck axes**: **adults 120°-135°; newborns 150°-160°.**
- **Coxa vara:** **< 120°** → **fracture risk, altered gait.** **Coxa valga:** **> 135°** → **hip instability, increased stress.**
- **Iliotibial band syndrome ("runner's knee"):** **overuse injury of runners and cyclists**; tight or inflamed band → **pain on the outer side of the knee** (repetitive knee bending and straightening).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Coxa vara < 120°; coxa valga > 135°.
- ITB syndrome = lateral knee pain in runners.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the femoral neck angle and its normal values.</summary>

**Answer:** The angle between the shaft and neck of the femur: 120°-135° in adults and 150°-160° in newborns.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare coxa vara and coxa valga.</summary>

**Answer:** Coxa vara (< 120°) raises fracture risk and alters gait; coxa valga (> 135°) causes instability and joint stress.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is iliotibial band syndrome?</summary>

**Answer:** An overuse injury of runners and cyclists with a tight or inflamed iliotibial band causing outer knee pain.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

## 2. Trendelenburg sign and sciatic nerve injury

- **Trendelenburg sign:** **superior gluteal nerve injury (L4, L5, S1)** denervates **gluteus medius and minimus**; when the patient lifts a leg the **pelvis drops on the unsupported side** (seen by the iliac crest levels). **Example:** weak **left** muscles → **right** pelvis drops when standing on the **left** leg.
- **Normally** the **contralateral** medius and minimus abduct the hip and keep the pelvis level.
- **Test:** stand on one leg; **normal:** the other pelvis **rises**; **abnormal:** the **good side drops → waddling gait.** **Causes:** **superior gluteal nerve paralysis; poliomyelitis** (lower lumbar and sacral segments).
- **Sciatic nerve injury:** **weak knee flexion, foot drop, calf wasting, loss of Achilles reflex, loss of sensation below the knee (except the medial side), trophic ulcers.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Trendelenburg: pelvis drops on the unsupported (normal) side.
- Sciatic injury: foot drop and lost Achilles reflex.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the Trendelenburg sign.</summary>

**Answer:** With superior gluteal nerve injury the medius and minimus cannot hold the pelvis so it drops on the unsupported side when the patient stands on the affected leg.

**Why:** Slides 6-7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What causes a positive Trendelenburg test?</summary>

**Answer:** Paralysis of the superior gluteal nerve (L4, L5, S1) and poliomyelitis affecting the lower lumbar and sacral cord.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the effects of sciatic nerve injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Weak knee flexion, foot drop, calf wasting, loss of the Achilles reflex, sensory loss below the knee (except the medial side) and trophic ulcers.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

## 3. Gluteal injections and piriformis syndrome

- **Intramuscular injection:** **thick gluteal muscles = large absorption area**; inject **well forward in the upper outer quadrant** to **avoid the sciatic nerve.**
- **Sciatic nerve surface marking (3 points):** **(1) 2.5 cm lateral to the midpoint of PSIS-ischial tuberosity; (2) halfway between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter; (3) junction of upper ⅔ and lower ⅓ of the back of the thigh.**
- **Exact site:** hand on the **greater trochanter** (thumb to groin, fingers to iliac crest); **index on the ASIS**, **middle finger back along the iliac crest**; the **V-shaped triangle's centre** is the site.
- **The injection enters gluteus medius**, because **gluteus maximus covers only its posterior part**; the upper lateral quadrant has only skin and fascia over the medius.
- **Piriformis syndrome:** **sciatic nerve compression by the piriformis** — **spasm or shortening from overuse or injury**; sometimes the nerve **pierces the piriformis**; **gluteal pain radiating into the leg**, **relieved by walking with the foot pointed outward (external rotation).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Inject in the upper outer quadrant, into gluteus medius.
- Piriformis syndrome: sciatic nerve entrapment.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Where is a gluteal injection given and why?</summary>

**Answer:** In the upper outer quadrant, well forward, to avoid the underlying sciatic nerve; the thick muscle gives good absorption.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is the sciatic nerve marked on the surface?</summary>

**Answer:** By joining three points: 2.5 cm lateral to the midpoint between PSIS and ischial tuberosity, halfway between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter, and the junction of the upper two-thirds and lower third of the posterior thigh.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe piriformis syndrome.</summary>

**Answer:** Sciatic nerve compression by a shortened or spastic piriformis, causing gluteal pain radiating to the leg, relieved by walking with the foot turned outward.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

## 4. Medial circumflex artery and neck of femur fractures

- **Medial circumflex femoral artery:** branch of the **profunda femoris**; supplies **most of the blood to the femoral head and hip joint**; damage → **ischemia, osteonecrosis.**
- **Fractures of the femoral neck (by site):**

| Type | Capsule | Vessels interrupted | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Subcapital, cervical** | **Intracapsular** | **Nutrient artery and retinacular vessels (main supply)** | **Avascular necrosis**; **prosthetic replacement** |
| **Basal, intertrochanteric** | **Extracapsular** | **Nutrient artery only** | **No avascular necrosis** |

- **Complications:** **avascular necrosis** (the **artery of the ligament of the head** is insufficient and often **atherosclerotic in the elderly**); **deformity:** **lateral rotation** (short gluteal rotators + gluteus maximus) and **shortening** (sartorius, rectus femoris, adductors, iliopsoas). **True leg length = ASIS to medial malleolus.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Intracapsular fractures → avascular necrosis; extracapsular → no necrosis.
- True leg length: ASIS to medial malleolus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the medial circumflex femoral artery important?</summary>

**Answer:** It supplies most of the blood to the femoral head and hip joint; damage causes ischemia and osteonecrosis.

**Why:** Slide 14.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Contrast intracapsular and extracapsular neck fractures.</summary>

**Answer:** Intracapsular (subcapital, cervical) interrupt the nutrient and retinacular vessels causing avascular necrosis; extracapsular (basal, intertrochanteric) interrupt only the nutrient artery.

**Why:** Slides 16-17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the deformity after a neck fracture.</summary>

**Answer:** Lateral rotation from the short gluteal rotators and gluteus maximus, and shortening from the pull of sartorius, rectus femoris, adductors and iliopsoas.

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

## 5. Hip dislocation

- **Types:** **congenital and acquired (traumatic)**; **uncommon** because the joint is very stable.
- **Congenital dislocation (CDH):** **loose capsule or external ligaments at birth**; **the ligament shortens in the first months** (difficult reduction); **acetabulum and femur fail to develop.** **Features:** **cannot abduct the thigh**, **shorter, externally rotated limb**, **asymmetric thigh skin folds.**
- **Radiology:** **Shenton's line** (neck of femur curve continuous with the obturator foramen) **disrupted**; **Perkin's lines** (horizontal through the triradiate cartilage, vertical through the superior acetabular lip): **dislocated epiphysis above the first and lateral to the second.**
- **Traumatic:** **excessive force (traffic accident)**; **usually posterior**; **hip flexed and medially rotated** is unstable; head covered **posteriorly by capsule, ligament weak posteriorly → posteroinferior dislocation.** **Adducted: no acetabular fracture; abducted: fracture of the posterior acetabular lip.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Traumatic dislocation is posterior with the hip flexed and medially rotated.
- CDH: cannot abduct, short externally rotated limb.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the features of congenital hip dislocation.</summary>

**Answer:** Loose capsule or ligaments at birth; inability to abduct the thigh, shorter externally rotated limb and asymmetric thigh skin folds.

**Why:** Slide 21.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the mechanism of traumatic posterior dislocation.</summary>

**Answer:** The flexed, medially rotated hip is unstable because the head is covered posteriorly only by capsule and the ligament is weak there, so it dislocates posteroinferiorly after great force.

**Why:** Slide 24.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are Shenton's and Perkin's lines?</summary>

**Answer:** Shenton's line is the continuous curve of the femoral neck and obturator foramen margin; Perkin's lines are horizontal and vertical lines at the acetabulum used to locate a dislocated epiphysis.

**Why:** Slide 22.
</details>

## 6. Avascular necrosis, septic hip and osteoarthritis

| Condition | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Osteonecrosis (AVN)** | **Ischemic bone necrosis**; **acute hip pain worse with weight bearing**; **no inflammatory signs**; **limited internal rotation and abduction**; **normal ESR and CRP**; associated with **femoral head fractures, sickle cell disease, steroids, SLE, alcoholism**; **normal X-ray, confirmed by MRI**; **observation or total hip arthroplasty** |
| **Septic hip** | **Intra-articular infection**, common in **children**, **surgical emergency**; **Staphylococcus aureus**; **acute pain, cannot bear weight, fever, severe pain on passive movement, swelling, erythema, warmth**; rests in **flexion, abduction and external rotation** |
| **Osteoarthritis** | **Degenerative cartilage loss**; **elderly or prior trauma**; **chronic hip or groin pain worse with activity**; **reduced internal rotation**; **joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis and cysts, osteophytes**; **NSAIDs, steroid injections, total hip arthroplasty** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- AVN: normal inflammatory markers, MRI confirms.
- Septic hip: emergency, Staphylococcus aureus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe avascular necrosis of the hip.</summary>

**Answer:** Ischemic bone necrosis with acute weight-bearing pain, no inflammatory signs and normal markers, associated with fractures, sickle cell disease, steroids, SLE and alcoholism, confirmed by MRI.

**Why:** Slides 28-29.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe septic hip.</summary>

**Answer:** An intra-articular infection (Staphylococcus aureus), a surgical emergency in children, with pain, fever, inability to bear weight and inflammatory signs; the hip rests in flexion, abduction and external rotation.

**Why:** Slide 30.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe hip osteoarthritis.</summary>

**Answer:** Degenerative loss of cartilage in the elderly or after trauma with activity-related groin pain, reduced internal rotation and X-ray narrowing, sclerosis, cysts and osteophytes; treated with NSAIDs, steroid injections or arthroplasty.

**Why:** Slides 31-32.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Trendelenburg: the pelvis drops on the side OPPOSITE the weak muscles (unsupported side).**
- **Intracapsular fractures cause AVN; extracapsular do not.**
- **Inject into gluteus MEDIUS (upper outer quadrant), not the lower medial buttock.**
- **Posterior dislocation is the usual traumatic type; the sciatic nerve is at risk.**
- **Septic hip is an emergency; AVN has normal inflammatory markers.**
- **Slide note:** the sciatic nerve injury slide leaves the sensory exception blank; the medial side (saphenous) is the standard answer.
