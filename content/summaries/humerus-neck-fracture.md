# Fracture of the Proximal (Neck) Humerus — Exam Night Review

## 1. Overview & anatomy

- A **proximal humerus fracture** involves the **surgical neck** or the **anatomical neck** of the humerus.
- The **Neer classification** is commonly used — it describes the **number of displaced parts**.
- Important complications: **nerve injury, stiffness, malunion and avascular necrosis**.

### Landmarks of the proximal humerus (lecture figure)
**Humeral head**, **anatomical neck**, **greater tuberosity**, **lesser tuberosity**, **intertubercular (bicipital) groove**, **surgical neck**, **deltoid tuberosity**.

- **Anatomical neck** = the constriction just around the head (capsule attachment).
- **Surgical neck** = the narrow part **below the tuberosities** — a common fracture site, closely related to the **axillary nerve**.

## 2. Neer classification

### The four segments
1. **Humeral head**
2. **Greater tuberosity**
3. **Lesser tuberosity**
4. **Shaft**

⭐ A segment is **displaced** when there is **separation > 1 cm** or **angulation > 45°**.

| Neer type | What is displaced |
|---|---|
| **1-part** | **no segment** displaced > 1 cm or angulated > 45° (no displacement) |
| **2-part** | **one segment** displaced — commonly the **surgical neck** or **greater tuberosity** |
| **3-part** | **two segments** displaced — typically the **surgical neck + one tuberosity** |
| **4-part** | **all four segments** displaced — the head may **lose its soft-tissue attachments** → **treated by surgery** |

- **Fracture-dislocations** and **head-split injuries** are important **severe patterns**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four segments: head, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, shaft.
- Displaced = > 1 cm separation or > 45° angulation.
- 1-part none, 2-part one, 3-part two (neck + tuberosity), 4-part all → surgery.
- Fracture-dislocation and head-split = severe.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the Neer classification of proximal humerus fractures.</summary>

**Answer:** It is based on four segments — humeral head, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity and shaft — and counts how many are displaced (> 1 cm separation or > 45° angulation). 1-part: no segment displaced; 2-part: one segment displaced (commonly surgical neck or greater tuberosity); 3-part: two segments displaced (typically surgical neck plus one tuberosity); 4-part: all four segments displaced, the head may lose its soft-tissue attachments, treated by surgery. Fracture-dislocations and head-split injuries are severe patterns.

**Why:** Segments + definition of displacement + the four types = a complete answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the criteria for a segment to be considered displaced?</summary>

**Answer:** Separation of more than 1 cm or angulation of more than 45°.

**Why:** The two numbers — 1 cm and 45° — are the most tested fact in this lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is a 4-part fracture treated by surgery?</summary>

**Answer:** Because all four major segments are displaced and the humeral head may lose its normal soft-tissue attachments (and therefore its blood supply), giving a high risk of avascular necrosis; it cannot be managed conservatively.

**Why:** Loss of soft tissue = loss of blood supply = AVN.
</details>

## 3. Complications

1. ⭐ **Avascular necrosis (osteonecrosis) of the humeral head** — particularly in **complex 4-part fractures** and **fracture-dislocations**.
2. **Axillary nerve injury** — assess **sensation over the lateral shoulder** and **motor function** (deltoid) when possible.
3. ⭐ **Shoulder stiffness** — **one of the commonest complications**.
4. **Malunion or nonunion**.
5. **Rotator cuff dysfunction** — especially with **tuberosity displacement**.
6. **Post-traumatic arthritis**.
7. **Vascular injury** — **uncommon**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- AVN of the head → mainly 4-part fractures and fracture-dislocations.
- Axillary nerve: check lateral shoulder sensation and deltoid.
- Stiffness is one of the commonest; vascular injury is uncommon.
- Tuberosity displacement → rotator cuff dysfunction.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Which fractures carry the greatest risk of avascular necrosis of the humeral head?</summary>

**Answer:** Complex 4-part fractures and fracture-dislocations, because the humeral head is separated from its soft-tissue attachments and blood supply.

**Why:** The take-home point: "greater concern for humeral-head ischaemia/osteonecrosis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do you assess the axillary nerve after a proximal humerus fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** Test sensation over the lateral shoulder (regimental badge area) and assess motor function of the deltoid when possible.

**Why:** The axillary nerve winds around the surgical neck — always check it.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why may rotator cuff dysfunction follow this fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** The rotator cuff muscles insert on the greater and lesser tuberosities; displacement of a tuberosity disturbs their attachment and function.

**Why:** Tuberosities = rotator cuff insertions.
</details>

## 4. Clinical assessment

- **Pain, swelling, bruising** and **inability to move the shoulder normally**.
- Look for **deformity**.
- **Radiographs: AP and axillary views**.
- **CT** is useful for **complex 3-part / 4-part fractures**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Symptoms: pain, swelling, bruising, inability to move the shoulder; look for deformity.
- X-ray: AP + axillary view; CT for complex 3- and 4-part fractures.
- Always check the axillary nerve and the distal vascular status.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical assessment of a suspected proximal humerus fracture.</summary>

**Answer:** The patient presents with pain, swelling, bruising and inability to move the shoulder normally; look for deformity. Check the axillary nerve (lateral shoulder sensation, deltoid) and distal vascular status. Imaging: AP and axillary radiographs; CT for complex 3-part/4-part fractures.

**Why:** History/exam + neurovascular check + imaging.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which X-ray views are requested?</summary>

**Answer:** Anteroposterior (AP) and axillary views.

**Why:** Two views are needed to judge displacement.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When is CT useful?</summary>

**Answer:** For complex 3-part and 4-part fractures, to define the fragments and plan treatment.

**Why:** CT shows the fragments in three dimensions.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- Displacement = **> 1 cm OR > 45°** (not 1 cm AND 45°, not 2 cm).
- Neer counts **displaced segments**, not fracture lines.
- **2-part** = one displaced segment (surgical neck or greater tuberosity); **3-part** = surgical neck + **one** tuberosity.
- **Stiffness** is one of the **commonest** complications; **vascular injury** is **uncommon**.
- AVN risk is highest in **4-part** fractures and **fracture-dislocations**.

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. Proximal humerus fracture = **surgical or anatomical neck**.
2. Neer: **4 segments** — head, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, shaft.
3. Displaced = **> 1 cm** separation or **> 45°** angulation.
4. **1-part** none; **2-part** one; **3-part** two (neck + tuberosity); **4-part** all → **surgery**.
5. Severe patterns: **fracture-dislocation, head-split**.
6. Complications: **AVN, axillary nerve, stiffness (commonest), mal/nonunion, cuff dysfunction, arthritis, vascular (uncommon)**.
7. Imaging: **AP + axillary**; **CT** for 3-/4-part.
8. Always check **axillary nerve + distal vascular status**; early recognition → urgent referral.
