## 1. Myelin and demyelination

- ⭐ **Myelin = protein-lipid complex** made by **spiral wrapping of the plasma membrane** around the axon. In the **PNS** the **Schwann cell** wraps an axon **up to 100 times**; gaps between segments = **nodes of Ranvier**.
- *Beyond the lecture (box in the file):* in the **CNS** myelin comes from the **oligodendrocyte** (one cell myelinates segments of several axons); **voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated at the nodes.**
- **Insulation:** ion leakage **decreased ~5,000-fold**; membrane capacitance **lowered** → **faster signal.**
- **Multiple sclerosis:** **autoimmune attack on CNS myelin**, **patchy** (some segments lose myelin, others keep it).
- **Consequences of demyelination:** **K⁺ leakage** from the exposed **axolemma** and **conduction block** (signals slow down or fail) → **delayed or blocked conduction.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Myelin = insulation: less leakage (~5,000-fold), lower capacitance, faster signal.
- PNS: Schwann cell (up to 100 wraps); CNS: oligodendrocyte.
- MS: patchy autoimmune demyelination → K⁺ leak, delayed/blocked conduction.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is myelin and how does it insulate the axon?</summary>

**Answer:** Myelin is a protein-lipid complex formed by spiral wrapping of the plasma membrane around the axon (Schwann cell in the PNS, up to 100 wraps). It decreases ion leakage about 5,000-fold and lowers membrane capacitance, so signals travel faster.

**Why:** Slide 3 (sections 1.1 and 1.2).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What happens in multiple sclerosis?</summary>

**Answer:** The immune system attacks the myelin of the CNS in a patchy way; some segments of an axon lose myelin while others keep it.

**Why:** Slide 3 (section 1.3).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the consequences of losing myelin?</summary>

**Answer:** The exposed axolemma leaks K⁺ ions rapidly, and conduction is blocked: signals slow down or fail to propagate (delayed or blocked conduction).

**Why:** Slide 3 (section 1.4) and self-test question 1.
</details>

## 2. Impulse conduction

- ⭐ **Saltatory conduction** (Latin *saltare*, to leap): in myelinated fibers the AP **jumps node to node** → **saves energy** and **up to ~50× faster** than unmyelinated fibers.
- **Steps:** **(1) node fires → (2) current jumps (ions flow along the axon) → (3) next node reaches threshold and regenerates the AP.**
- *Beyond the lecture (box):* ions cross the membrane **only at nodes**, so less energy is needed to restore gradients; textbooks quote **~5 to 50-fold** speed gain.

### Action potential characteristics
| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **All-or-none** | Threshold gives the **maximum** potential; **subthreshold fails** entirely |
| **Stereotypical** | **Constant amplitude and kinetics** for a given cell type |
| **Non-decremental** | Travels **without losing amplitude** over distance |

### Orthodromic vs antidromic conduction
| | **Orthodromic** | **Antidromic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | **Physiological** | **Experimental** |
| **Direction** | **Forward:** soma → axon terminals | **Backward:** terminal → soma |
| **At synapses** | **Crosses** synapses forward | **Dies out** at the first synapse |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Saltatory conduction = node-to-node jumping: faster (up to ~50×) and energy-saving.
- AP: all-or-none, stereotypical, non-decremental.
- Orthodromic = physiological forward; antidromic = experimental backward, dies at first synapse.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is saltatory conduction and why is it advantageous?</summary>

**Answer:** The action potential jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next in myelinated fibers; this preserves energy and increases conduction velocity up to about 50 times compared with unmyelinated fibers.

**Why:** Slide 4 (section 2.1).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the three characteristics of the action potential.</summary>

**Answer:** All-or-none (threshold gives the maximum potential, subthreshold fails), stereotypical (constant amplitude and kinetics for a cell type) and non-decremental (no loss of amplitude over distance).

**Why:** Slide 4 (section 2.2).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare orthodromic and antidromic conduction.</summary>

**Answer:** Orthodromic is physiological, forward (soma to terminals) and crosses synapses; antidromic is experimental, backward (terminal to soma) and dies out at the first synapse.

**Why:** Slide 4 (section 2.3).
</details>

## 3. Axonal transport

- ⭐ **Why transport?** Axons depend almost entirely on **protein made in the soma**; **microtubules** are the continuous tracks for **molecular motors**. If the axon is severed, the distal segment breaks down quickly = **Wallerian degeneration.**
- **Axoplasmic flow** = transport of proteins and polypeptides to the axonal ending.

### Types of axonal transport
| Type | Direction | Velocity | Motor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fast anterograde** | Soma → terminal | **~400 mm/day** | **Kinesin** |
| **Slow anterograde** | Soma → terminal | **0.5–10 mm/day** | **Cytoskeletal flow** |
| **Retrograde** | Terminal → soma | **~200 mm/day** | **Dynein** |

- **Brain storming:** rabies, herpes simplex and tetanus toxin **hijack retrograde transport (dynein)** to travel from the nerve terminal to the CNS; disrupting transport cuts the distal axon off from the soma → it degenerates.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Kinesin = anterograde (to terminal, ~400 mm/day); dynein = retrograde (to soma, ~200 mm/day).
- Slow anterograde = cytoskeletal flow, 0.5–10 mm/day.
- Rabies, HSV and tetanus toxin use retrograde transport.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why do axons need axonal transport?</summary>

**Answer:** Axons depend almost entirely on protein synthesized in the soma, which must be carried along the axon on microtubule tracks by molecular motors; if the axon is cut, the distal segment degenerates (Wallerian degeneration).

**Why:** Slide 5 (section 3.1).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare anterograde and retrograde transport.</summary>

**Answer:** Anterograde: kinesin, soma to terminal, about 400 mm/day (fast). Retrograde: dynein, terminal to soma, about 200 mm/day. Slow anterograde transport (0.5–10 mm/day) is cytoskeletal flow.

**Why:** Slide 5 (sections 3.2 and 3.3).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which transport do rabies, herpes simplex and tetanus toxin exploit?</summary>

**Answer:** Retrograde axonal transport driven by dynein, which carries cargo from the terminal to the soma and so gives them a route to the CNS.

**Why:** Slide 6 (brain storming).
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Kinesin = anterograde (to terminal); dynein = retrograde (to soma).**
- **Fast anterograde ≈ 400 mm/day; retrograde ≈ 200 mm/day; slow anterograde 0.5–10 mm/day.**
- **Schwann cell = PNS; oligodendrocyte = CNS.**
- **Demyelination → K⁺ leakage and conduction block, NOT faster conduction.**
- **Saltatory conduction = jumping between nodes, not regeneration at every point.**
- **Action potential is non-decremental: amplitude does not change along the axon.**
- **Antidromic = experimental; it dies out at the first synapse.**
