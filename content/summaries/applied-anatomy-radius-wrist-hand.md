## 1. Radial head fracture, PIN palsy and the interosseous nerves

- ⭐ **Mechanism:** fall with **axial load + valgus stress** → radial head compressed against the **capitellum** (which bears about **60%** of the axial load). The radial head stabilizes forearm rotation at the **PRUJ**.
- **PIN palsy signs:** **finger and thumb drop**; **wrist extension intact** (ECRL/ECRB spared) but with **radial deviation** (ECU paralyzed, so ECRL predominates).
- **Red flag:** **Essex-Lopresti lesion** = radial head fracture + **interosseous membrane tear** + **DRUJ injury**.
- **PIN course:** through the **radial tunnel / arcade of Frohse** (fibrous arch of the superficial supinator) → extensor muscles except ECRL/ECRB; injured by **fragments** or by **hematoma/swelling** compression.
- **Proof of PIN injury:** sensation of the **dorsal 1st web space is intact** (superficial radial nerve spared).

| | **Anterior interosseous (AIN)** | **Posterior interosseous (PIN)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | Motor branch of the **median** nerve | Motor branch of the **radial** nerve |
| **Supplies** | **3 muscles:** FPL, pronator quadratus, lateral ½ of FDP | **9 muscles** (extensors of the posterior forearm) |
| **Course** | Between the **2 heads of pronator teres** | **Pierces supinator** |
| **Injured by** | **Supracondylar fracture**, pronator teres syndrome | **Radial neck fracture**, supinator syndrome |
| **Sign** | **OK sign negative** (no flexion of thumb IP and index/middle DIP) | **Finger and thumb drop** |

- **Interosseous membrane:** a **fibrous joint** between radius and ulna; connects the moving radius to the fixed ulna, gives **wide muscle attachment** area, **transmits forces**.
- Note: the slide table writes "medial ½ FDP" for the AIN; elsewhere in the course the median/AIN supply is to the **lateral** half (index and middle).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Radial head fracture → PIN palsy (finger drop, intact wrist extension with radial deviation).
- Essex-Lopresti = radial head + interosseous membrane + DRUJ.
- AIN (median) = OK sign negative; PIN (radial) = finger and thumb drop.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. A woman falls on wet ground and has finger and thumb extension weakness with normal wrist extension and radial deviation; X-ray shows a radial neck fracture. Explain the findings.</summary>

**Answer:** The fracture injured the PIN: finger/thumb extension is weak (finger drop), wrist extension is normal because ECRL is spared, and the radial deviation is due to ECU weakness so ECRL predominates. The dorsal 1st web space sensation is intact because the superficial radial nerve is spared.

</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the AIN and PIN.</summary>

**Answer:** AIN: branch of the median nerve, supplies FPL, pronator quadratus and lateral half of FDP, runs between the heads of pronator teres, injured by supracondylar fracture and pronator teres syndrome, OK sign negative. PIN: branch of the radial nerve, supplies 9 extensors, pierces supinator, injured by radial neck fracture and supinator syndrome, finger and thumb drop.

## 2. Radial shaft fracture

- **Mechanism:** **FOOSH** → axial loading + rotational torque. **Signs:** mid-forearm pain, tenderness, deformity, **impaired pronation/supination**.
- **Nerves:** irritation of the **superficial radial nerve** (subcutaneous in the distal third) or the **PIN** (curves around the neck, at risk if the fracture extends upward).
- **Associated injuries:** **Monteggia** = ulnar shaft fracture (usually proximal third) + **radial head dislocation**; **Galeazzi** = distal radial shaft fracture + **DRUJ dislocation/instability**.
- ⭐ **Red flag:** loss of forearm rotation → displacement and disruption of the interosseous membrane.
- **Displacement:** **biceps** (radial tuberosity) supinates and flexes the **proximal** fragment; **pronator teres** (mid-shaft) pulls the **distal** fragment into pronation.
- **Reduction depends on the pronator teres insertion:** fracture **proximal** to the midpoint → proximal supinated, distal pronated → splint in **supination**; fracture **distal** to the midpoint → forces balance → **neutral (mid-prone)**.
- Note: the lecture describes the pronator teres insertion as "midway along the medial shaft".

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Radial shaft fracture = loss of rotation; watch for Monteggia (ulna + radial head) and Galeazzi (radius + DRUJ).
- Biceps supinates the proximal fragment; pronator teres pronates the distal fragment.
- Proximal to pronator teres → splint in supination; distal → neutral.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why does a radial shaft fracture limit pronation and supination?</summary>

**Answer:** The radius rotates around the ulna, so a mid-shaft fracture disrupts the rotational axis of the forearm (and may disrupt the interosseous membrane).

**Why:** Slides "Fracture of the radial shaft" (anatomical focus and MCQ Q1).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate Monteggia from Galeazzi fractures.</summary>

**Answer:** Monteggia: fracture of the ulnar shaft (usually the proximal third) with dislocation of the radial head at the elbow. Galeazzi: fracture of the distal radial shaft with dislocation or instability of the DRUJ.

**Why:** Slide "Fracture of the radial shaft" table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does the level of the fracture determine the splint position?</summary>

**Answer:** Proximal to the pronator teres insertion, the proximal segment is supinated by biceps and the distal pronated by pronator teres, so the forearm is splinted in supination. Distal to the insertion, biceps and pronator teres balance, so the forearm is immobilized in the neutral (mid-prone) position.

**Why:** Slide "Factors influencing segment displacement in radial fractures".
</details>

## 3. Distal radius (Colles') fracture

| Feature | Key point |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | **FOOSH with the wrist in extension**; elderly with **osteoporosis** |
| **Site** | **2–3 cm proximal** to the distal articular surface of the radius |
| **Deformity** | **"Dinner fork"**: dorsal angulation, dorsal displacement and radial deviation of the distal fragment |
| **Why it displaces** | **Brachioradialis** on the radial styloid pulls the distal fragment **proximally and radially**; thumb extensors/abductors also pull it proximally |
| **Associated injuries** | Ulnar styloid fracture, **median nerve compression**, **DRUJ/TFCC injury** |
| **X-ray** | Loss of **volar tilt** (normal ≈ 11°; 10–12°), dorsal angulation, **radial shortening**, radial deviation; radial inclination normally 20–25° |
| **Reduction** | **Traction → ulnar deviation → palmar flexion** |
| **Complications** | Malunion, loss of pronation-supination, chronic pain, **median nerve compression**, post-traumatic arthritis |

- **Radial styloid** is normally **longer** than the ulnar styloid; in Colles' the distal fragment shifts **posteriorly and upward**, shortening the radius, so the relationship is lost.
- **EPL tendon rupture** can follow because the tendon bends sharply around the **radial tubercle**. (The slide calls this a "mallet thumb".)

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Colles' = FOOSH with the wrist extended; dinner-fork deformity; brachioradialis pulls the fragment proximally and radially.
- Complications: median nerve compression, EPL rupture, DRUJ/TFCC injury, malunion.
- Reduction: traction, ulnar deviation, palmar flexion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanism, site and deformity of Colles' fracture.</summary>

**Answer:** A fall on an outstretched hand with the wrist extended (typically in elderly osteoporotic patients) fractures the radius 2–3 cm proximal to its articular surface; the distal fragment is displaced dorsally, angulated dorsally and deviated radially, giving the "dinner fork" deformity.

**Why:** Slide "Distal radius fracture (Colles') table".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the anatomical basis of the displacement and list the complications.</summary>

**Answer:** Brachioradialis, inserted on the radial styloid, pulls the distal fragment proximally and radially; extensors and abductors of the thumb also pull it proximally and the fragment tilts posteriorly. Complications include median nerve injury by the proximal fragment, EPL rupture, DRUJ/TFCC injury, malunion, loss of pronation-supination, chronic pain and arthritis.

**Why:** Slides "Anatomical basis for displacement" and "Anatomical complications of this fracture".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What happens to the normal relation of the radial and ulnar styloid processes?</summary>

**Answer:** Normally the radial styloid is longer than the ulnar styloid; after a Colles' fracture the distal fragment shifts posteriorly and upward carrying the styloid, so the radius shortens and this relationship is lost.

**Why:** Slide on styloid process relationship.
</details>

## 4. Scaphoid fracture

- **Mechanism:** **FOOSH** with wrist **extension + radial deviation**; young adults, contact athletes, manual laborers.
- ⭐ **Anatomical snuffbox tenderness** is the key sign (described as pathognomonic and 100% sensitive).
- **Snuffbox:** **anterior** = abductor pollicis longus + extensor pollicis brevis; **posterior** = extensor pollicis longus; **floor (proximal → distal)** = radial styloid, **scaphoid**, trapezium, base of the 1st metacarpal.
- **Blood supply:** **retrograde** (dorsal branch of the radial artery enters at the **distal pole**), so the **proximal pole is a watershed**.

| Site | AVN risk |
|---|---|
| **Proximal pole** | **30–50%** (highest) |
| **Waist** | **10–15%** |
| **Distal pole** | **Minimal** |

- **Scapholunate ligament** disruption → **DISI** deformity.
- **Missed fractures:** ≈ 15% are **occult** on first X-rays (it may not show for **2 weeks**) → **MRI/CT**; **non-union** up to **50%** without fixation.
- **Why missed:** few clinical and radiological signs, mistaken for a **sprained wrist**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Snuffbox tenderness after FOOSH = scaphoid fracture until proven otherwise.
- Retrograde blood supply: proximal pole has the highest AVN risk (30–50%).
- Occult in about 15%: repeat imaging, MRI/CT; non-union up to 50%.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. A 24-year-old athlete falls on an outstretched hand; X-rays are negative but pain persists after 2 weeks. What is the diagnosis and how do you prove it?</summary>

**Answer:** Scaphoid fracture. Snuffbox tenderness is the key sign; since about 15% are occult on initial films, MRI or CT is needed.

**Why:** Slides "Scaphoid fracture — case scenario and key features".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is scaphoid fracture commonly complicated by avascular necrosis?</summary>

**Answer:** The blood supply enters mainly through the distal part (retrograde flow); in a fracture across the waist, vessels to the proximal part are cut, causing ischemic (avascular) necrosis. The proximal pole carries the highest risk (30–50%).

**Why:** Slides "Scaphoid blood supply" and "Why fracture of scaphoid is complicated by AVN".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the boundaries and floor of the anatomical snuffbox.</summary>

**Answer:** Anterior: abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis. Posterior: extensor pollicis longus. Floor from proximal to distal: styloid process of the radius, scaphoid, trapezium and base of the 1st metacarpal.

**Why:** Slides "Anatomical snuffbox".
</details>

## 5. Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS)

- **Mechanism:** chronic **compression of the median nerve** under the flexor retinaculum; **females, diabetics, repetitive wrist motion**; also synovial hypertrophy (RA, pregnancy).
- **Signs:** **Tinel's** (tapping over the retinaculum), **Phalen's** (wrist flexion 60 s), **thenar atrophy**, weak thumb opposition; **nocturnal paresthesia** of thumb/index/middle (to the radial half of the ring finger); **little finger spared**.
- ⭐ **Red flag:** **thenar atrophy** → chronic compression → urgent surgical referral.
- **Tunnel:** **roof** flexor retinaculum (transverse carpal ligament); **floor** carpal bones; **contents** median nerve + tendons of **FDS, FDP, FPL** with synovial sheaths.
- **Palmar cutaneous branch** of the median nerve arises **proximal** to the retinaculum and runs **outside** the tunnel → **palmar sensation is spared**.
- **Flexor retinaculum:** attached **medially** to the pisiform and hook of the hamate, **laterally** to the scaphoid and trapezium; crossed anteriorly (lateral → medial) by the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve, palmaris longus tendon, palmar cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve, ulnar artery and ulnar nerve.
- **Mimics:** cervical radiculopathy (C6/C7), pronator teres syndrome. **Treatment:** **decompression by cutting the flexor retinaculum**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- CTS = median nerve compression: Tinel, Phalen, thenar wasting, weak opposition, little finger spared.
- Palmar cutaneous branch bypasses the tunnel → palmar sensation preserved.
- Surgical cure: cut the flexor retinaculum.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. A 62-year-old woman has pins and needles in the index and middle fingers, a flattened thenar eminence and cannot bring the thumb and little finger tips together. Explain each finding.</summary>

**Answer:** Median nerve compression in the carpal tunnel causes the paresthesia; the thenar eminence is flattened by wasting of the three thenar muscles (FPB, APB, OP; adductor pollicis is ulnar); the thumb–little finger opposition is lost through weakness of opponens pollicis.

**Why:** Slides "Carpal tunnel syndrome — case scenario and Q&A".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is palmar sensation spared in CTS?</summary>

**Answer:** The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve arises proximal to the flexor retinaculum and passes superficial to it, so it is not caught by compression of the main nerve; sensation is lost at the digit tips (1 and 2).

**Why:** Slides "Carpal tunnel syndrome — MCQs and Q&A".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the boundaries and contents of the carpal tunnel and the treatment of CTS?</summary>

**Answer:** Roof: flexor retinaculum; floor: carpal bones (scaphoid, trapezium, hamate, pisiform). Contents: median nerve and the tendons of FDS, FDP and FPL in synovial sheaths. Treatment: decompression by cutting the flexor retinaculum.

**Why:** Slides "Carpal tunnel syndrome — anatomical focus" and "surgery".
</details>

## 6. Guyon canal syndrome

- **Mechanism:** repetitive compression (**cycling, typing**) or a space-occupying lesion (**ganglion cyst, hook of hamate fracture**). Case: cyclist with weak grip, **clawing of the 4th/5th fingers**, numbness of the **palmar** ring and little fingers, **no dorsal sensory loss**.
- **Classic triad:** **motor weakness** (intrinsics, hypothenar), **sensory loss** of the palmar 4th/5th digits, **sparing of dorsal ulnar sensation** (the dorsal branch arises **proximal** to the canal).
- **Differential:** **cubital tunnel syndrome**.

| Boundary | Structure |
|---|---|
| **Roof** | Palmar carpal ligament |
| **Floor** | Transverse carpal ligament + pisohamate ligament |
| **Medial** | Pisiform + FCU tendon |
| **Lateral** | **Hook of the hamate** |

- **Branches in the canal:** **deep motor** (interossei, lumbricals 3–4, hypothenars) and **superficial sensory** (palmar 4th/5th digits). **Palmaris brevis** is supplied by the superficial branch, not the deep motor branch.
- **Zones:** **Zone 1** (proximal) mixed motor/sensory (ganglion cyst); **Zone 2** (deep motor) hook of hamate fracture → **pure motor**; **Zone 3** (superficial sensory) isolated sensory loss (rare).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Guyon canal: ulnar nerve compression at the wrist (cyclists, ganglion, hook of hamate).
- Triad: weak intrinsics, palmar 4th/5th sensory loss, dorsal sensation spared.
- Zone 1 mixed, zone 2 motor, zone 3 sensory.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the boundaries of the Guyon canal.</summary>

**Answer:** Roof: palmar carpal ligament; floor: transverse carpal ligament and pisohamate ligament; medial: pisiform and FCU tendon; lateral: hook of the hamate.

**Why:** Slide "Guyon canal syndrome — anatomical focus".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is dorsal hand sensation spared in Guyon canal syndrome?</summary>

**Answer:** The dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve arises proximal to the wrist, before the canal, so it is not compressed.

**Why:** Slide "Guyon canal syndrome — classic triad" and MCQs.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the zones of compression in the Guyon canal.</summary>

**Answer:** Zone 1 (proximal): mixed motor and sensory loss, e.g. ganglion cyst. Zone 2 (deep motor): hook of hamate fracture, pure motor deficit. Zone 3 (superficial sensory): isolated sensory loss, rare.

**Why:** Slide "Guyon canal syndrome — zones of compression".
</details>

## 7. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) of the hand

- **Mechanism:** chronic **synovitis** → ligament laxity, tendon imbalance, joint destruction. **Risk:** **women (3:1), smokers, HLA-DR4**.
- **Targets:** **PIP, MCP, wrist** joints and flexor/extensor **tendon sheaths** (trigger fingers or ruptures).
- **Red flags:** **tendon ruptures** (EPL, FPL) and **cervical spine instability** (C1–C2) before intubation.

| Deformity | Joint position | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Swan-neck** | **PIP hyperextension + DIP flexion** | Volar plate laxity, ruptured FDS, tight intrinsics/lateral bands |
| **Boutonnière** | **PIP flexion + DIP hyperextension** | **Central slip rupture**, volar migration of lateral bands |
| **Ulnar deviation** | MCP deviation toward the ulna | Radial collateral ligament weakening, intrinsic pull, volar MCP subluxation |
| **Z-thumb** | **MCP flexion + IP hyperextension** | Tendon imbalance, instability |
| **Tendon rupture** | Extensor loss, especially ulnar side | **EDM first** (ulnar head, Vaughn-Jackson); **EPL** over Lister's tubercle → finger drop; **FPL (Mannerfelt)** by scaphoid osteophyte → loss of thumb flexion |
| **Nerve compression** | Median (CTS) and ulnar (Guyon) | Synovial hypertrophy |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Swan-neck = PIP extension; boutonnière = PIP flexion (central slip rupture).
- EDM ruptures first; FPL ruptures in the Mannerfelt lesion.
- Check C1–C2 stability before intubation in long-standing RA.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the swan-neck and boutonnière deformities.</summary>

**Answer:** Swan-neck: PIP hyperextension with DIP flexion (volar plate laxity, ruptured FDS, tight intrinsics and lateral bands). Boutonnière: PIP flexion with DIP hyperextension (central slip rupture and volar migration of the lateral bands).

**Why:** Slides "RA of the hand" and "Anatomical basis for common RA hand deformities".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which tendons rupture in RA?</summary>

**Answer:** EDM ruptures first (prominent ulnar head, Vaughn-Jackson); EPL ruptures by attrition over Lister's tubercle (finger drop); FPL ruptures from erosion by scaphoid osteophytes in the carpal tunnel (Mannerfelt lesion), causing loss of thumb flexion.

**Why:** Slides "RA of the hand — anatomical focus and MCQs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the most important pre-intubation assessment in long-standing RA?</summary>

**Answer:** Cervical spine imaging to assess C1–C2 instability.

**Why:** Slide "RA of the hand — MCQs".
</details>

## 8. Radiological anatomy of the wrist

- **TFCC components:** (a) **triangular fibrocartilage disc** — primary stabilizer, transmits **20%** of axial load; (b) **radioulnar ligaments** (dorsal/volar) stabilize the **DRUJ**; (c) **ulnocarpal ligaments** (ulnolunate, ulnotriquetral) support the proximal carpal row.
- **Ossification:** all carpal bones are **cartilaginous at birth**; each ossifies from **one centre**; the **capitate** (largest) ossifies **first (1 year)**; others in sequence by size — used to estimate **bone age**.
- **Scaphoid:** floor of the snuffbox, most frequently fractured, may not show for 2 weeks, AVN or non-union.
- **Lunate:** most commonly dislocated; forced hyperextension → **anterior dislocation** compressing the median nerve. It is tied to the radius by the **scapholunate and lunotriquetral ligaments** (its blood supply); it survives if **one** ligament tears, **both** → AVN. On a lateral film **radius, lunate and capitate** align (capitate in the lunate cup).
- **Pisiform:** sesamoid in the FCU tendon, last to ossify. **Trapezium:** saddle joint. **Hamate:** hook, fractured in racket sports and golf, Guyon canal.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- TFCC disc is the primary DRUJ stabilizer and transmits 20% of axial load.
- Capitate ossifies first (1 year); pisiform last.
- Lunate dislocation depends on two ligaments carrying its blood supply.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the components of the TFCC.</summary>

**Answer:** The triangular fibrocartilage disc (primary stabilizer, transmits 20% of axial load), the dorsal and volar radioulnar ligaments (stabilize the DRUJ) and the ulnocarpal ligaments (ulnolunate and ulnotriquetral) supporting the proximal carpal row.

**Why:** Slide "TFCC components".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is carpal ossification important and what is its sequence?</summary>

**Answer:** It determines the bone age of the child. All carpal bones are cartilaginous at birth, each ossifies from one centre, the capitate (the largest) ossifies first at 1 year and the others follow in order of size.

**Why:** Slide on ossification of carpal bones.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the lunate the most commonly dislocated carpal bone?</summary>

**Answer:** It is attached to the radius by the scapholunate and lunotriquetral ligaments, which carry its blood supply and are frequently torn in a fall with wrist hyperextension. It survives if one ligament is torn; rupture of both causes avascular necrosis.

**Why:** Slide "Lunate is the most common dislocated carpal bone".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **PIN palsy keeps wrist extension (ECRL spared)** but with radial deviation.
- **AIN = OK sign negative (median); PIN = finger drop (radial).**
- **Monteggia = ulna + radial head; Galeazzi = radius + DRUJ.**
- **Colles': brachioradialis pulls the distal fragment proximally and radially; dinner-fork deformity.**
- **Scaphoid AVN risk is highest in the proximal pole (30–50%).**
- **CTS spares the little finger and the palmar skin** (palmar cutaneous branch bypasses the tunnel).
- **Guyon canal spares the dorsal ulnar sensation**; the hook of the hamate is the lateral boundary.
- **Swan-neck = PIP hyperextension; boutonnière = PIP flexion.**
- **Palmaris brevis is NOT supplied by the deep motor branch of the ulnar nerve.**
