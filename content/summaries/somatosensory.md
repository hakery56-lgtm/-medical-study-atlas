# Somatosensory Physiology — Exam Night Review

This physiology lecture starts with the nervous system and the neuron, then answers five questions about somatic sensation: **how do we detect a stimulus, how strong is it, how long does it last, where is it, and where do we feel it?** After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

## 1. The nervous system and the neuron

**The nervous system** is a physically connected network of **cells, tissues and organs** that lets us communicate with and react to the environment and perform life activities.

### Facts from the lecture
- In the foetus, neurons develop at about **250,000 per minute**.
- An adult brain has about **100 billion** nerve cells, yet we typically use about **4%** at any time.
- **Neurons are our largest cells**; messages travel at up to **180 m/s**.

### Four primary functions
1. **Sensing** the world (vision, hearing, smell, taste, touch).
2. **Transmitting** information.
3. **Processing** information.
4. **Producing a response**.

### The neuron — the functional unit
| Part | Role |
|---|---|
| **Dendrites** | **receive** chemical signals from neighbouring cells |
| **Cell body** | contains the **nucleus and organelles** |
| **Axon** | long extension carrying electrical messages **away from the cell body** |
| **Axon terminals** | **pass the signal** to the next cell |
| **Myelin sheath** | **protective covering** of the axon (Schwann cells, nodes of Ranvier) |

**Glial cells** support and protect neurons.

### Types of neurons and the reflex arc
- **Sensory neuron** (unipolar) — **detects stimuli**.
- **Interneuron** (multipolar) — **relays signals** between neurons.
- **Motor neuron** (multipolar) — carries the message from the brain/spinal cord **to muscles**.
- Together they form the ⭐ **reflex arc** — **automatic, unplanned responses**. Stepping on a nail: sensory neurons in the foot → spinal cord interneuron → motor neuron moves the foot (and the signal also goes up to the brain).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Nervous system = connected network of cells, tissues and organs to sense and respond.
- Functions: sensing, transmitting, processing, responding.
- Neuron parts: dendrites (receive), cell body (nucleus), axon (carries away), terminals (pass on), myelin (protects).
- Sensory → interneuron → motor = reflex arc (automatic response).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the parts of a neuron and their functions.</summary>

**Answer:** Dendrites receive chemical signals from neighbouring cells; the cell body contains the nucleus and organelles; the axon is a long extension that carries electrical messages away from the cell body to the axon terminals, which pass the signal to the next cell; the myelin sheath is a protective covering of the axon.

**Why:** Five parts, each with its job.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the four primary functions of the nervous system.</summary>

**Answer:** Sensing the world (vision, hearing, smell, taste, touch), transmitting information, processing information, and producing a response.

**Why:** A direct list question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the reflex arc using the example of stepping on a nail.</summary>

**Answer:** A reflex is an automatic, unplanned response. The stimulus (the nail) is received by sensory neurons in the foot; the signal travels to the spinal cord, where an interneuron is triggered; the interneuron relays the signal (also up to the brain) and stimulates the motor neuron, which moves the foot away.

**Why:** Sensory → interneuron → motor, with the example.
</details>

## 2. Somatic sensation, receptors and stimulus properties

**Sensation** = a **conscious or subconscious awareness** of an external or internal stimulus.

### The senses
- **Somatic** — from the body: **tactile** (touch, pressure, vibration), **thermal** (warm, cold), **pain**, **proprioception**.
- **Visceral** — from internal organs.
- **Special senses** — smell, taste, vision, hearing.

### First-order neurone receptors
1. **Free nerve ending** (e.g. cold).
2. **Encapsulated nerve ending** (e.g. pressure).
3. **Specialized receptor cell** (e.g. taste receptor for sugar).

### Modality and quality
- **Modality** = the **type** of stimulus (light, touch, temperature, chemical…). ⭐ **Receptors are modality-specific**.
- **Quality** = a **subdivision** of a modality (taste → sweet, sour, salt…).

### ⭐ The CNS distinguishes 4 stimulus properties
1. **Modality** (nature) — by the type of receptor.
2. **Location**.
3. **Intensity**.
4. **Duration**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sensation = conscious or subconscious awareness of a stimulus.
- Somatic (tactile, thermal, pain, proprioception), visceral, special senses.
- Receptors: free, encapsulated, or specialized cells; modality-specific.
- CNS codes modality, location, intensity and duration.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define sensation and list the somatic senses.</summary>

**Answer:** Sensation is a conscious or subconscious awareness of an external or internal stimulus. The somatic senses are tactile (touch, pressure, vibration), thermal (warm, cold), pain and proprioception.

**Why:** Definition + the four somatic senses.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate modality and quality of a stimulus.</summary>

**Answer:** Modality is the type of stimulus, such as light, touch, temperature or chemical change; sensory receptors are modality-specific. Quality is a subdivision of a modality — for example taste is divided into sweet, sour and salt.

**Why:** Two definitions with examples.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which four properties of a stimulus does the CNS distinguish?</summary>

**Answer:** Modality (nature of the stimulus, from the type of receptor), location, intensity and duration.

**Why:** The four questions the rest of the lecture answers.
</details>

## 3. Sensory receptors of the skin

### Where they lie
- **Epidermis**: nociceptors; **Merkel discs** (type I mechanoreceptor).
- **Dermis**: **Meissner corpuscles** (in dermal papillae); **Ruffini corpuscles** (type II); hair root plexus.
- **Subcutaneous layer**: **Pacinian (lamellated) corpuscles**.

### Mechanoreceptors
Activated by **physical deformation** (touch, pressure, stretch, vibration) of skin, muscles, tendons, ligaments and joint capsules; they include exteroceptors and proprioceptors.

**Non-encapsulated** (⭐ **slowly adapting**):
- **Free nerve endings** — very widespread (epidermis, dermis, cornea, dental pulp, mucous membranes, muscles, tendons, joints, bone).
- **Merkel's discs** — **discriminative touch**: texture, shape and edges.

**Encapsulated:**
| Receptor | Detects |
|---|---|
| **Meissner's corpuscles** | ⭐ **two-point (fine) discrimination** — lets the visually impaired read **Braille** |
| **Pacinian corpuscles** | **largest**; **pressure and vibration** (incl. tickle); **rapidly adapting** |
| **Ruffini's corpuscles** | **stretch** of collagen in skin/joint capsules; may give proprioception |
| **Muscle spindles & Golgi tendon organs** | input from skeletal muscle to the spinal cord → **reflexes and motor control** (with the cerebellum) |

Touch receptors open a **mechanically gated ion channel** when stimulated.

### Other receptors
- **Thermoreceptors**: free endings in the hypodermis; **cold** (< body temperature) and **warm** (> body temperature); adapt only between about **20 and 40 °C**; used in **thermoregulation**. Temperature-sensitive nociceptors respond to excessive heat or cold.
- **Nociceptors**: **free nerve endings**, activated by strong noxious stimuli → **pain**; 3 categories — **mechanical, thermal, chemical**; **polymodal** nociceptors respond to all three.
- **Proprioceptors**: in muscles and joints → **body position** (muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Non-encapsulated (slowly adapting): free nerve endings, Merkel discs (texture, shape, edges).
- Meissner = fine two-point touch (Braille); Pacinian = largest, pressure/vibration, rapidly adapting; Ruffini = stretch.
- Thermoreceptors: cold/warm free endings, adapt between 20–40 °C.
- Nociceptors: free endings; mechanical, thermal, chemical, polymodal.
- Proprioceptors: muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the encapsulated mechanoreceptors and their functions.</summary>

**Answer:** Meissner's corpuscles detect two-point (fine) tactile discrimination and allow the visually impaired to read Braille; Pacinian corpuscles, the largest, respond to pressure and vibration including tickle; Ruffini's corpuscles respond to stretch of collagen in the skin or joint capsules and may give proprioceptive information; muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs carry input from skeletal muscle to the spinal cord for reflexes and motor control.

**Why:** Four receptors, one function each.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are nociceptors and how are they classified?</summary>

**Answer:** Pain-sensitive free dendritic endings activated by strong, noxious stimuli, transmitting pain. They are mechanical, thermal or chemical, and polymodal nociceptors respond to all three types of painful stimuli.

**Why:** Definition + 3 categories + polymodal.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the thermoreceptors.</summary>

**Answer:** Free dendritic endings in the hypodermis that function in thermoregulation: cold receptors respond below body temperature and warm receptors above it, and they adapt only between about 20 and 40 °C. Temperature-sensitive nociceptors are activated by excessive heat or cold.

**Why:** Type, function, range.
</details>

## 4. How do we detect a stimulus? — sensory transduction

1. The **stimulus changes the permeability** of the receptor membrane to ions.
2. Ion movement produces a **receptor potential**.
3. The **generator potential** triggers
4. **Action potentials** (nerve impulses) that propagate along the axon into the **CNS**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Stimulus → permeability change → receptor potential → generator potential → action potentials → CNS.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the steps of sensory transduction.</summary>

**Answer:** The stimulus changes the receptor membrane's permeability to ions; ion movement produces a receptor potential; this generator potential triggers action potentials (nerve impulses) that propagate into the CNS.

**Why:** Four ordered steps.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is a generator potential?</summary>

**Answer:** The receptor potential produced by ion movement across the receptor membrane in response to a stimulus, which triggers action potentials in the sensory neuron.

**Why:** Links the receptor to the nerve impulse.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What happens when a touch receptor is stimulated?</summary>

**Answer:** Deformation opens a mechanically gated ion channel, changing ion permeability and producing a receptor (generator) potential that triggers action potentials.

**Why:** Transduction applied to touch.
</details>

## 5. How strong and how long? — intensity, duration and adaptation

### Intensity
- ⭐ Coded by the **rate (frequency) of action potentials** — **frequency coding**: a stronger stimulus → more APs per unit time.
- A stronger stimulus also **recruits neighbouring receptors** (to a lesser degree).

### Duration and adaptation
- **Duration** is coded by the **duration of APs** in the sensory neuron.
- **Sustained stimulation → adaptation**, which divides receptors into two types:

| | **Tonic** receptors | **Phasic** receptors |
|---|---|---|
| Adaptation | **slow or none** | **rapid** |
| Firing | continuous for the **whole stimulus** | **stop firing** if a continuous stimulus stays constant (fire at onset/offset) |
| Purpose | **monitor** things that must be checked continuously | let the body **ignore** constant unimportant information |
| Examples | **joint receptors, baroreceptors** | **touch receptors, smell** |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Intensity = **frequency** of APs (not their size — APs are all-or-none). **Baroreceptors are tonic**; **touch and smell are phasic**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Intensity: frequency coding + recruitment of neighbouring receptors.
- Duration: duration of AP firing; sustained stimulation → adaptation.
- Tonic (slow): joint receptors, baroreceptors. Phasic (rapid): touch, smell.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does the nervous system code stimulus intensity?</summary>

**Answer:** By frequency coding: a stronger stimulus produces a higher rate of action potentials in the afferent neurone over time; a stronger stimulus also activates neighbouring receptors to a lesser degree.

**Why:** Frequency + recruitment.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare tonic and phasic receptors.</summary>

**Answer:** Tonic receptors adapt slowly or not at all and fire continuously for the whole stimulus, monitoring parameters that must be evaluated continually (e.g. joint receptors, baroreceptors). Phasic receptors adapt rapidly, respond briefly and stop firing if a constant stimulus continues, allowing the body to ignore constant unimportant information (e.g. touch, smell).

**Why:** The comparison table in words.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is sensory adaptation?</summary>

**Answer:** A decline in a receptor's response during sustained, constant stimulation; the speed of adaptation classifies receptors as tonic (slow) or phasic (rapid).

**Why:** Definition + classification.
</details>

## 6. Where is the stimulus? — sensory acuity

**Acuity** = the **precision** with which a stimulus can be located. ⭐ Determined by:
1. **Lateral inhibition** in the CNS.
2. **Two-point discrimination**.
3. **Synaptic convergence and divergence**.

### Lateral inhibition
Excited neurons **reduce the activity of their neighbours**, so only the **most stimulated, least inhibited** neurons respond — this **sharpens** the signal peak.

### Two-point discrimination
- The **minimal distance** at which two simultaneous touches are felt as two: **fingertips ≈ 2 mm**, **forearm ≈ 40 mm**.
- Determined by: **receptor density** (3–4× greater in fingertips), **size of receptive fields** (fingertips 1–2 mm; palm 5–10 mm), and **psychological factors**.
- Practical: an opened paper clip with tips about 1 cm apart, tested on fingertips, palm and forearm.

### Receptive fields
- The area of sensory space that can **elicit a neuron's response** when stimulated; each first-order neuron covers one.
- **Smaller fields → greater sensitivity/acuity**; fields vary in size and density and **overlap**.

### Convergence and divergence
- **Convergence**: many sensory neurons converge on one neuron → **summation**, but **decreased acuity**.
- **Divergence**: one sensory neuron branches to many neurons → **amplifies** the signal and **increases acuity**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Acuity: lateral inhibition, two-point discrimination, convergence/divergence.
- Two-point threshold: fingertip 2 mm, forearm 40 mm — receptor density, receptive field size, psychology.
- Small receptive fields = high acuity.
- Convergence ↓ acuity; divergence ↑ acuity (amplifies).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is lateral inhibition and why is it important?</summary>

**Answer:** The capacity of excited neurons to reduce the activity of their neighbours. Neurons that are firing inhibit the stimulation of surrounding neurons, so only the most stimulated and least inhibited ones respond, which sharpens localization and increases acuity.

**Why:** Definition + effect.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What determines two-point discrimination?</summary>

**Answer:** The density of sensory receptors (3–4 times greater in the fingertips than elsewhere in the hand), the size of the receptive fields (1–2 mm in the fingertips versus 5–10 mm in the palm), and psychological factors. The threshold is about 2 mm on the fingertips but 40 mm on the forearm.

**Why:** Three factors with the numbers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare convergence and divergence.</summary>

**Answer:** In convergence, multiple sensory neurons project onto a single neuron, allowing summation but decreasing acuity. In divergence, one sensory neuron branches to synapse with many neurons, amplifying the signal and increasing acuity.

**Why:** Opposite effects on acuity.
</details>

## 7. Where do we feel it? — ascending pathways and the cortex

### Functional classification
- **General somatic afferent (GSA)**: touch, pressure, vibration, pain, temperature, stretch and position from **somatic** structures.
- **General visceral afferent (GVA)**: pressure, pain and other sensation from **visceral** structures.

### Anatomical classification — three pathways
| Pathway | Includes | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterolateral system (ALS)** | spinothalamic, spinoreticular, spinomesencephalic, spinotectal, spinohypothalamic | ⭐ **pain and temperature**, **crude** (poorly localized) touch and pressure, some proprioception |
| **Dorsal column–medial lemniscus (DCML)** | fasciculus **gracilis**, fasciculus **cuneatus**, medial lemniscus | ⭐ **fine (discriminative) touch, vibration, position sense** |
| **Spinocerebellar pathways** | anterior, posterior, rostral spinocerebellar, cuneocerebellar | mainly **proprioception** to the **cerebellum** |

### ALS — three-neuron sequence
1. **First-order** neuron: cell body in the **dorsal root ganglion** → to the **dorsal horn**.
2. **Second-order** neuron: cell body in the **dorsal horn**; axon **decussates** (crosses) and ascends to the **contralateral thalamus** (spinothalamic = direct pathway), or to the **reticular formation** (spinoreticular = indirect), or to brainstem nuclei.
3. **Third-order** neuron: cell body in the **thalamus** → ascends **ipsilaterally** to the **somatosensory cortex**.
- Result: the **right side of the body is represented on the left side of the brain**.

### Thalamus
**Crude localization** and discrimination; highly organized projections to the cortex; **thalamic lesions** (e.g. stroke) can cause **thalamic over-reaction**.

### Somatosensory cortex (post-central gyrus)
- **Sharp localization** and **full recognition of the qualities** of each modality; cortical columns.
- **Somatotopic** representation — the **sensory homunculus** (contralateral): the size of each area reflects that region's **sensory acuity** (hands, lips and face are large).
- **Perception** = awareness of stimuli and the ability to discriminate between them; the **decision to respond** is taken at the cortex.
- **Cortical lesions** (e.g. repeated seizures) may cause **loss of two-point discrimination** or **astereognosis**.
- Investigating sensory disturbance: **which sensations** are affected, and **is it localized** (e.g. to a **dermatome**)?

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- GSA (somatic) vs GVA (visceral).
- ALS: pain, temperature, crude touch; DCML: fine touch, vibration, position; spinocerebellar: proprioception to cerebellum.
- ALS neurons: DRG → dorsal horn (decussates) → thalamus → cortex; contralateral representation.
- Thalamus = crude localization; post-central gyrus = sharp localization, homunculus; lesions → loss of two-point discrimination, astereognosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the anterolateral system and the dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway.</summary>

**Answer:** The anterolateral system (spinothalamic, spinoreticular, spinomesencephalic, spinotectal and spinohypothalamic tracts) carries pain and temperature plus crude, poorly localized touch and pressure. The DCML pathway (fasciculus gracilis, fasciculus cuneatus and medial lemniscus) carries discriminative fine touch, vibration and position sense.

**Why:** Contents + modalities of each.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the neuron sequence of the anterolateral system.</summary>

**Answer:** The first-order neuron has its cell body in the dorsal root ganglion and carries information to the dorsal horn. The second-order neuron's cell body is in the dorsal horn; its axon decussates and ascends to the contralateral thalamus (spinothalamic), to the reticular formation (spinoreticular) or to brainstem nuclei. The third-order neuron's cell body is in the thalamus, and its axon ascends ipsilaterally to the somatosensory cortex.

**Why:** Three neurons, where each starts, and where the crossing happens.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the sensory homunculus?</summary>

**Answer:** The somatotopic map of the body in the post-central gyrus, in which each body area has a specific cortical representation on the opposite side; the relative size of each area reflects the sensory acuity of that body region (e.g. hands, lips and face are large).

**Why:** Somatotopic + contralateral + size = acuity.
</details>

5. **Merkel** = texture/edges (slow); **Meissner** = fine two-point, **Braille**; **Pacinian** = largest, pressure/vibration, **rapid**; **Ruffini** = stretch.

7. Transduction: **permeability change → receptor/generator potential → APs → CNS**.
8. Intensity = **frequency coding** + recruitment; duration = AP duration.
9. **Tonic** (slow: joint receptors, **baroreceptors**) vs **phasic** (rapid: **touch, smell**).

12. **Convergence ↓ acuity**, **divergence ↑ acuity**.
13. **ALS** = pain, temperature, crude touch; **DCML** = fine touch, vibration, position; **spinocerebellar** = proprioception.
14. ALS: **DRG → dorsal horn (crosses) → thalamus → post-central gyrus**; **contralateral**.
