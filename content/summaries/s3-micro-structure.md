## 1. Objectives and introduction to bacteria

- The lecture objectives (Bloom's taxonomy): **identify** the major structures (**cell wall, cytoplasmic membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, nucleoid, plasmids, flagella, pili, capsules, endospores, biofilms**), **compare Gram-positive and Gram-negative** cell walls, explain how structure affects **virulence and antibiotic susceptibility**, interpret **Gram stain**, and examine the role of **pili, flagella, capsules and biofilms** in infection and immune evasion.
- Curriculum context: the **3rd stage** courses are **Pathology, Pharmacology, Microbiology, Parasitology, Internal medicine, Surgery, Family & Community Medicine**.
- **Bacteria** = **microscopic, single-celled organisms** with a major role in health, disease and the environment; the basis of microbiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis and treatment.

### Classification
- By **shape, Gram-staining, metabolic activity and oxygen requirement**: **Gram-positive, Gram-negative, aerobic, anaerobic, facultative anaerobes**.

### Shapes
- **Cocci (spherical), bacilli (rod), spirilla (spiral), vibrios (comma-shaped).**

### Arrangements
- **Chains (streptococci), clusters (staphylococci), pairs (diplococci), tetrads.** They arise from the **pattern of cell division**.

### Size
- Most bacteria are **0.5–5 µm**; **larger than viruses, smaller than eukaryotic cells**; **Mycoplasma** is exceptionally small.

## 2. Cell envelope

### The cell wall
- Provides **structural integrity**, protects against **osmotic pressure**, and **determines the shape**; a **major target for antibiotics (β-lactams)**.

### Peptidoglycan
- **Mesh-like polymer of sugars and amino acids** (alternating **NAG and NAM** + **tetrapeptide chain** + **peptide interbridge**) → rigidity; **thick in Gram-positive, thin in Gram-negative**.
- **Penicillin inhibits peptidoglycan synthesis → cell lysis.**

### Gram-positive vs Gram-negative
| | **Gram-positive** (e.g. *S. aureus*) | **Gram-negative** |
|---|---|---|
| Peptidoglycan | **thick** | **thin** |
| Special components | **teichoic acids** (rigidity + adherence) | **outer membrane with lipopolysaccharide (LPS = endotoxin)** |
| Outer membrane | **none** | **present** |
| Gram stain | **retain crystal violet → purple** | **lose crystal violet → pink** |
| Antibiotics | **highly susceptible to β-lactams** (exposed wall) | **more resistant** (protective outer membrane) |
| Immune response | — | **LPS triggers a strong immune response** |

### Cytoplasmic membrane
- **Phospholipid bilayer** controlling passage of substances; houses proteins for **nutrient transport, energy production, signal transduction**; in some bacteria also the enzymes for **cell-wall synthesis and respiration**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cell wall: shape, integrity, osmotic protection; β-lactams act on peptidoglycan synthesis.
- Gram +: thick peptidoglycan + teichoic acids, purple, β-lactam sensitive. Gram −: thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane/LPS (endotoxin), pink, more resistant.
- Cytoplasmic membrane: phospholipid bilayer; transport, energy, signalling.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare Gram-positive and Gram-negative cell walls.</summary>

**Answer:** Gram-positive: thick peptidoglycan with teichoic acids, no outer membrane, retains crystal violet (purple) and is highly susceptible to β-lactams. Gram-negative: thin peptidoglycan plus an outer membrane containing LPS (endotoxin), stains pink and is more resistant to antibiotics.

**Why:** Cell wall structure determines staining and antibiotic response.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the function of peptidoglycan and which antibiotics target it?</summary>

**Answer:** It gives rigidity and shape to the cell wall and protects against osmotic lysis; penicillin and other β-lactams inhibit its synthesis, causing cell lysis.

**Why:** A mesh of NAG/NAM sugars and peptide chains.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the role of LPS?</summary>

**Answer:** LPS in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria acts as an endotoxin that triggers strong immune responses and contributes to antibiotic resistance.

**Why:** Part of the Gram-negative outer membrane.
</details>

## 3. Cytoplasmic components and genetic material

| Component | Features |
|---|---|
| **Cytoplasm** | gel-like; contains **ribosomes, enzymes, nutrients, metabolites**; site of **DNA replication, protein synthesis, energy production**; **no membrane-bound organelles** |
| ⭐ **Ribosomes** | **70S = 50S + 30S**; translate mRNA into proteins; targeted by **tetracyclines and aminoglycosides** without affecting human ribosomes |
| ⭐ **Nucleoid** | **no true nucleus**; a **single circular double-stranded DNA** molecule; **supercoiling** compacts the genome and regulates gene expression |
| ⭐ **Plasmids** | **small circular DNA separate from the chromosome**; carry **antibiotic-resistance or toxin genes**; central to **horizontal gene transfer** and genetic engineering |
| **Inclusion bodies** | **storage** of nutrients/energy: **glycogen, sulfur, phosphate, lipids**; some are **diagnostic markers** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- No organelles; 70S ribosomes (50S + 30S) — target of tetracyclines and aminoglycosides.
- Nucleoid = single circular dsDNA, no nucleus. Plasmids = extra circular DNA (resistance, toxins, horizontal transfer).
- Inclusion bodies = storage (glycogen, sulfur, phosphate, lipids).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the bacterial ribosome and its clinical significance.</summary>

**Answer:** Bacterial ribosomes are 70S, made of 50S and 30S subunits, and translate mRNA into proteins; antibiotics such as tetracyclines and aminoglycosides target them selectively without affecting human ribosomes.

**Why:** Selective toxicity.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate the nucleoid from plasmids.</summary>

**Answer:** The nucleoid is the region holding the single circular double-stranded chromosomal DNA (bacteria lack a true nucleus); plasmids are small circular DNA molecules separate from the chromosome that carry advantageous genes (antibiotic resistance, toxins) and mediate horizontal gene transfer.

**Why:** Chromosomal vs extra-chromosomal DNA.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are inclusion bodies?</summary>

**Answer:** Storage sites for nutrients and energy reserves (glycogen, sulfur, phosphate, lipids); some serve as diagnostic markers for particular pathogens.

**Why:** Depend on species and conditions.
</details>

## 4. Surface structures and survival forms

| Structure | Function / significance |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Flagella** | whip-like **motility**; **monotrichous** (single), **lophotrichous** (tuft), **amphitrichous** (both ends), **peritrichous** (all around); help **colonization, invasion, biofilm formation** |
| ⭐ **Pili and fimbriae** | short hair-like appendages; **fimbriae = adhesion to host tissues**; **pili = DNA transfer in conjugation**; virulence + biofilm |
| ⭐ **Capsule / slime layer** | **polysaccharide or protein**; **prevents phagocytosis → immune evasion**; biofilm and antibiotic resistance; critical virulence factor |
| ⭐ **Endospores** | **highly resistant dormant forms** of **Bacillus and Clostridium**; survive **heat, radiation, desiccation, nutrient deprivation**; associated with **tetanus (Clostridium) and anthrax (Bacillus)** |
| ⭐ **Biofilms** | **structured communities in a protective matrix** on **medical devices, tissues, surfaces**; ↑ resistance to antibiotics and immune responses → **chronic, recurrent infections** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Flagella (motility; mono-, lopho-, amphi-, peritrichous), fimbriae (adhesion), pili (conjugation).
- Capsule prevents phagocytosis; endospores (Bacillus, Clostridium) resist heat/radiation/desiccation (tetanus, anthrax).
- Biofilms on devices → resistant chronic infection.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify flagellar arrangements.</summary>

**Answer:** Monotrichous (single flagellum), lophotrichous (tuft), amphitrichous (flagella at both ends) and peritrichous (all around the cell).

**Why:** Flagella give motility and aid colonization and biofilm formation.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare pili and fimbriae and mention the role of the capsule.</summary>

**Answer:** Fimbriae are mainly for adhesion to host tissues; pili transfer DNA during conjugation (both promote virulence and biofilm). The capsule (polysaccharide or protein) prevents phagocytosis, helps immune evasion and contributes to biofilm and antibiotic resistance.

**Why:** All are virulence factors.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are endospores and biofilms and why are they clinically important?</summary>

**Answer:** Endospores are highly resistant dormant structures formed by Bacillus and Clostridium (heat, radiation, desiccation) associated with tetanus and anthrax. Biofilms are structured bacterial communities in a protective matrix on devices and tissues, increasing resistance to antibiotics and immunity and causing chronic, recurrent infections.

**Why:** Both defeat usual treatment and disinfection.
</details>

## 5. Antibiotic targets, disease, diagnosis and patient safety

- **Antibiotic targets:** **cell wall synthesis** (β-lactams), **protein synthesis** (macrolides), **nucleic acid replication** (fluoroquinolones) and **metabolic pathways**.
- **Bacterial diseases:** skin infections, sepsis, meningitis; examples ***Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Mycobacterium tuberculosis***.
- **Diagnostic techniques:** **microscopy, culture, biochemical tests, molecular diagnostics (PCR)**.
- **Patient safety:** strict **aseptic technique**; correct **Gram stain** performance/interpretation; **biosafety precautions**; recognize that **capsules, biofilms and endospores increase treatment resistance**; **accurate reporting**; **infection control for devices** (biofilm-related hospital infections).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Targets: cell wall (β-lactams), protein synthesis (macrolides), nucleic acids (fluoroquinolones), metabolism.
- Diagnosis: microscopy, culture, biochemical tests, PCR.
- Safety: aseptic technique, correct Gram staining, biosafety, accurate reporting, device infection control.

</div>

## 6. Small-group case scenario (S. aureus skin infection)

- **Case:** 50-year-old with severe skin infection, *S. aureus* suspected; a new lab technician misreads the Gram stain as **Gram-negative** → **Gram-negative antibiotic** given → **no improvement at 48 h**; a **urinary catheter** may carry a **biofilm**.
- **Answers:**
  - *S. aureus* (**Gram-positive**): **thick peptidoglycan + teichoic acids, no outer membrane, purple**; Gram-negative: **thin peptidoglycan + LPS outer membrane, pink**. Thick peptidoglycan → **sensitive to β-lactams** (penicillin, cephalosporins); LPS → **strong immune reaction and drug resistance**.
  - **Capsule** prevents phagocytosis; **pili/fimbriae** adhesion to tissues and devices; **flagella** motility/spread; **biofilm** protects from antibiotics/immune cells → **chronic, recurrent infection**.
  - **Misidentification** → wrong antibiotic, failure, progression, ↑ resistance, longer stay, higher cost.
  - **Prevention:** training, **second verification**, **SOPs**, supervision, **control organisms**, double-check microscopy, correlate with the **clinical picture**.
  - **Better treatment:** assume Gram-positive *S. aureus* → **β-lactam (if sensitive) or vancomycin (if MRSA)**; **remove the catheter**, biofilm-penetrating antibiotics, strict infection control.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Misreading a Gram stain → wrong antibiotic → failure.
- S. aureus = Gram-positive → β-lactam or vancomycin (MRSA); catheter biofilm → remove device.
- Prevent errors: training, second verification, SOPs, controls, clinical correlation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does the cell wall difference affect staining and antibiotic response in the case?</summary>

**Answer:** Gram-positive S. aureus has a thick peptidoglycan layer and teichoic acids, retains crystal violet (purple) and is sensitive to β-lactams; Gram-negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer and an LPS-containing outer membrane, stain pink and are more resistant.

**Why:** A Gram-negative antibiotic failed because the organism was Gram-positive.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why do biofilms on catheters complicate treatment and how is it managed?</summary>

**Answer:** Antibiotics penetrate poorly, bacteria grow slowly inside the biofilm and immune cells are blocked; management includes removing the catheter, biofilm-penetrating antibiotics and strict infection control.

**Why:** Device removal is the key step.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How could the laboratory error have been prevented?</summary>

**Answer:** Careful review of Gram stain steps, use of control organisms, double-checking microscopy, consulting senior staff, correlating with the clinical picture, SOPs and supervision of new technicians.

**Why:** Patient-safety measures.
</details>
