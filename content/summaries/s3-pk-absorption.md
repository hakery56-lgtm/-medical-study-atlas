## 1. Absorption and its mechanisms

- **Absorption** = transfer of a drug **from the site of administration into the bloodstream**.
- **IV** → absorption is **complete (100 % bioavailability)**. Other routes → **partial** absorption → lower bioavailability.

### Mechanisms of absorption from the GIT
| Mechanism | Features |
|---|---|
| **1. Passive diffusion** | **water-soluble** drugs pass through **aqueous channels/pores**; **lipid-soluble** drugs cross easily through the **lipid bilayer**; down the concentration gradient |
| **2. Facilitated diffusion** | via **specialized transmembrane carrier proteins** for **large molecules**, **high → low concentration**, **no energy** |
| **3. Active transport** | **energy-dependent (ATP hydrolysis)**, **against the concentration gradient** (low → high) |
| **4. Endocytosis / exocytosis** | for **exceptionally large** molecules; **vitamin B12** enters by **endocytosis**; **noradrenaline** is released by **exocytosis** from vesicles |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Absorption = site of administration → bloodstream; IV = 100 %.
- Passive diffusion (aqueous pores / lipid bilayer), facilitated diffusion (carrier, no energy), active transport (ATP, against gradient), endocytosis (B12) / exocytosis (noradrenaline).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the mechanisms of drug absorption from the GIT.</summary>

**Answer:** Passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport, and endocytosis/exocytosis.

**Why:** Four mechanisms in the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate between facilitated diffusion and active transport.</summary>

**Answer:** Facilitated diffusion uses carrier proteins to move molecules from high to low concentration and needs no energy; active transport is driven by ATP hydrolysis and moves the drug against its concentration gradient (from low to high).

**Why:** Energy requirement and direction of movement.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give examples of endocytosis and exocytosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Vitamin B12 is transported across the gut wall by endocytosis; noradrenaline stored in nerve-terminal vesicles is released by exocytosis.

**Why:** Used for very large molecules.
</details>

## 2. Factors influencing absorption (1): effect of pH and pKa

- Most drugs are **weak acids or weak bases**.
  - **Weak acid:** **HA ⇄ H⁺ + A⁻** — **HA (protonated) is uncharged → crosses membranes**; **A⁻ (charged) cannot**.
  - **Weak base:** **BH⁺ ⇄ B + H⁺** — **B (uncharged) crosses**; **BH⁺ (charged) cannot**.
- **A drug passes the membrane more readily if it is uncharged (non-ionized).**
- The ratio of charged/uncharged forms depends on the **pH at the site of absorption** and the **pKa** (strength of the weak acid/base).
  - **Lower pKa = more acidic; higher pKa = more basic.**
- **pH = pKa → [HA] = [A⁻] and [BH⁺] = [B]** (50 : 50).
- **pH < pKa** → protonated forms (HA, BH⁺) predominate; **pH > pKa** → deprotonated forms (A⁻, B) predominate.

| Medium | Acidic drugs | Basic drugs |
|---|---|---|
| **Acidic (low pH, e.g. stomach)** | **non-ionized (HA), lipid-soluble** → absorbed | **ionized (BH⁺), water-soluble** |
| **Basic (high pH, e.g. small intestine)** | **ionized (A⁻), water-soluble** | **non-ionized (B), lipid-soluble** → absorbed |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Uncharged (non-ionized) form crosses membranes: HA for acids, B for bases.
- Acidic drugs are non-ionized in the stomach; basic drugs are non-ionized in the intestine.
- pH = pKa → 50 % ionized; pH < pKa → protonated forms; pH > pKa → deprotonated forms.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the effect of pH on the absorption of weak acids and weak bases.</summary>

**Answer:** Only the uncharged form crosses the membrane: HA for weak acids and B for weak bases. In acidic media (stomach) acidic drugs are non-ionized (lipid-soluble) and basic drugs ionized; in basic media (small intestine) acidic drugs are ionized and basic drugs non-ionized.

**Why:** Ionization depends on the pH relative to the pKa.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What happens when pH equals the pKa of a drug?</summary>

**Answer:** The concentrations of ionized and non-ionized forms are equal: [HA] = [A⁻] for an acid and [BH⁺] = [B] for a base.

**Why:** Definition of pKa as the pH of half-ionization.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does pKa indicate?</summary>

**Answer:** The strength of the interaction of the compound with a proton; the lower the pKa the more acidic the drug, the higher the pKa the more basic.

**Why:** Lecture definition.
</details>

## 3. Factors influencing absorption (2): blood flow, surface area, contact time, P-glycoprotein

2. **Blood flow:** intestinal flow ≫ gastric flow → **absorption from the intestine is favoured**.
3. **Surface area:** the **intestine** has a much **larger surface area** than the stomach.
4. **Contact time:** very rapid transit (e.g. **severe diarrhea**) → poor absorption. **Food** in the stomach **dilutes** the drug and **slows gastric emptying** → a drug taken with a meal is **absorbed more slowly**.
5. **P-glycoprotein (P-gp)** — a **multidrug transmembrane transporter** expressed throughout the body:
   - **Liver:** drugs → **bile** (elimination).
   - **Kidney:** drugs → **urine** (excretion).
   - **Placenta:** drugs **back into maternal blood**.
   - **Intestine:** drugs **into the lumen** (↓ absorption).
   - **Brain capillaries:** drugs **back into blood** (limits CNS access).
   - ⭐ **High expression → reduced drug absorption.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Intestine = higher blood flow and surface area → main absorption site.
- Diarrhea shortens contact time; food slows gastric emptying and absorption.
- P-glycoprotein pumps drugs out (bile, urine, back to mother, intestinal lumen, back to blood from brain) → lowers absorption.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is absorption from the intestine favoured over the stomach?</summary>

**Answer:** The intestine has a much greater blood flow and a much larger surface area than the stomach.

**Why:** Both increase the absorption rate.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do severe diarrhea and food affect drug absorption?</summary>

**Answer:** Severe diarrhea shortens the contact time so the drug is poorly absorbed; food dilutes the drug and slows gastric emptying, so a drug taken with a meal is generally absorbed more slowly.

**Why:** Contact time at the absorbing surface.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is P-glycoprotein and what are its functions?</summary>

**Answer:** A multidrug transmembrane transporter that moves drugs across membranes: into bile in the liver, into urine in the kidney, back to maternal blood in the placenta, into the lumen in the intestine, and back into the blood in brain capillaries; in areas of high expression it reduces drug absorption.

**Why:** It acts as an efflux pump.
</details>

## 4. Bioavailability and bioequivalence

- **Bioavailability** = the **fraction of the administered drug that reaches the systemic circulation**; important for calculating **enteral doses**.
- ⭐ **Bioavailability = (AUC oral ÷ AUC injected) × 100**.
  - IV drugs enter the circulation directly; oral drugs pass the **liver (portal circulation)** first and may be **extensively metabolized**.

### Factors that influence bioavailability
1. **First-pass hepatic metabolism** — drug is metabolized in the liver/gut wall during the first passage → **high first-pass = low oral bioavailability**.
2. **Solubility** — **hydrophilic** drugs are poorly absorbed (cannot cross the lipid-rich membrane).
3. **Chemical instability** — e.g. **penicillin G** is unstable at gastric pH; **insulin** is destroyed in the GIT.
4. **Nature of the formulation** — **particle size, salt form, crystal polymorphism, enteric coatings, excipients**.

### Bioequivalence
- Two related drug preparations are **bioequivalent** if they show **comparable bioavailability** and **similar times to peak blood concentration**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Bioavailability = fraction reaching systemic circulation = (AUC oral / AUC IV) × 100.
- Factors: first-pass metabolism, solubility, chemical instability (penicillin G, insulin), formulation.
- Bioequivalent = comparable bioavailability + similar time to peak.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define bioavailability and write the equation used to calculate it.</summary>

**Answer:** The fraction of the administered drug that reaches the systemic circulation; Bioavailability = (AUC oral / AUC injected) × 100.

**Why:** Comparison of the oral AUC with the IV AUC (100 %).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Mention the factors that influence bioavailability.</summary>

**Answer:** First-pass hepatic metabolism, solubility of the drug (hydrophilic drugs are poorly absorbed), chemical instability (penicillin G in gastric acid, insulin destroyed in the GIT), and the nature of the formulation (particle size, salt form, crystal polymorphism, enteric coating, excipients).

**Why:** Four factors listed in the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is bioequivalence?</summary>

**Answer:** Two related drug preparations are bioequivalent if they have comparable bioavailability and similar times to reach peak blood concentration.

**Why:** Definition on the slide.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **IV = 100 % bioavailability**; oral < 100 % because of incomplete absorption and **first-pass metabolism**.
- **Acidic drug + acidic pH (stomach) = non-ionized = absorbed; basic drug + basic pH (intestine) = non-ionized = absorbed.**
- **pH = pKa → 50 % ionized.**
- **Facilitated diffusion = no energy; active transport = ATP, against gradient.**
- **P-glycoprotein REDUCES absorption** (efflux pump).
- **Food slows absorption** (dilution + slower gastric emptying).
- **Bioequivalence needs similar bioavailability AND similar time to peak.**
