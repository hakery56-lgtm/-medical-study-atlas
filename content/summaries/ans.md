## 1. Organization of the nervous system

```
Nervous system
├── CNS: brain + spinal cord
└── PNS
    ├── Sensory (afferent) nerves → receptors → CNS
    └── Motor (efferent) nerves → CNS → effectors
          ├── Somatic NS (voluntary) → skeletal muscle
          └── Autonomic NS (involuntary) → smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands
                ├── Sympathetic → "fight or flight"
                └── Parasympathetic → "rest and digest"
```

- The **somatic NS** = motor neurons stimulating **skeletal muscles** (**voluntary**).
- The **ANS** = motor neurons controlling **smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands** (**involuntary**). It also **monitors visceral organs and blood vessels with sensory neurons** that give input to the CNS.
- The ANS has two divisions: **sympathetic (SNS)** and **parasympathetic (PNS)**; both can **stimulate and inhibit** effector organs.
- ⭐ **Tone:** both systems are **continually active** at a low rate — the basal rates are **sympathetic tone** and **parasympathetic tone**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- CNS = brain + spinal cord; PNS = sensory + motor; motor → somatic (voluntary, skeletal muscle) + autonomic (involuntary, smooth/cardiac muscle, glands).
- ANS = sympathetic (fight or flight) + parasympathetic (rest and digest).
- The ANS also has visceral sensory neurons.
- Sympathetic and parasympathetic tone = basal continuous activity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Outline the organization of the nervous system.</summary>

**Answer:** The nervous system has a central part (brain and spinal cord) and a peripheral part. The peripheral part has sensory nerves carrying information from receptors to the CNS and motor nerves carrying commands from the CNS to effectors. The motor part is subdivided into the somatic nervous system (voluntary; skeletal muscle) and the autonomic nervous system (involuntary; smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands), which is divided into sympathetic and parasympathetic systems.

**Why:** A tree — learn the branches in order.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate between the somatic and autonomic nervous systems (basic definition).</summary>

**Answer:** The somatic nervous system consists of motor neurons that stimulate skeletal muscles under voluntary control. The autonomic nervous system consists of motor neurons that control smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands involuntarily, and it also monitors visceral organs and blood vessels with sensory neurons.

**Why:** Effectors + control are the keys.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are sympathetic and parasympathetic tone?</summary>

**Answer:** Both systems are continually active, transmitting impulses at a low rate through most of their fibres; these basal rates of activity are called sympathetic tone and parasympathetic tone, which allow an organ's activity to be raised or lowered.

**Why:** Tone = continuous background activity.
</details>

## 2. Neurons, ganglia & central control of the ANS

### Pre- and postganglionic neurons
- Both divisions have **preganglionic neurons that originate in the CNS** and **postganglionic neurons that originate outside the CNS in ganglia**.
- ⭐ **Ganglia** = ovoid structures containing **cell bodies of neurons and glial cells** supported by connective tissue; they work like **relay stations** (one nerve enters, another exits).
- **Ganglia vs nuclei:** ganglia = clusters of cell bodies in the **PNS**; nuclei = clusters of cell bodies in the **CNS**.

### Functions
The ANS controls **most visceral functions** of the body — some almost entirely and some only partially.

### Central control — the ANS is activated by centers in:
| Center | Functions |
|---|---|
| **Spinal cord** | autonomic reflexes |
| **Medulla** | **vasomotor center**, **respiratory center**, **swallowing, coughing, vomiting** centers |
| **Pons** | **pneumotaxic center**, **micturition center** |
| ⭐ **Hypothalamus** | **main overall integration center of the ANS ("the boss")**; **temperature regulation**, **thirst and food-intake** centers |
| **Cerebral cortex** | **frontal lobe and limbic system** |

- Higher regions that mainly influence the ANS: **brainstem, hypothalamus and limbic system**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Preganglionic neurons are in the CNS; postganglionic neurons are in ganglia outside the CNS.
- Ganglion = PNS cell bodies (relay station); nucleus = CNS cell bodies.
- Hypothalamus = main integration center ("the boss"); medulla = vasomotor, respiratory, swallowing, coughing, vomiting; pons = pneumotaxic + micturition.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are ganglia and how do they differ from nuclei?</summary>

**Answer:** Ganglia are ovoid structures containing neuron cell bodies and glial cells supported by connective tissue; they act as relay stations where one nerve enters and another exits. Ganglia are clusters of nerve cell bodies in the peripheral nervous system, whereas nuclei are clusters of nerve cell bodies in the CNS.

**Why:** Location (PNS vs CNS) is the difference.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name the central regions controlling the ANS and one function of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Spinal cord — autonomic reflexes; medulla — vasomotor, respiratory, swallowing, coughing and vomiting centers; pons — pneumotaxic and micturition centers; hypothalamus — the main integration center ("the boss"), with temperature regulation and thirst/food-intake centers; cerebral cortex (frontal lobe and limbic system).

**Why:** Five levels, bottom to top.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the hypothalamus called "the boss" of the ANS?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it is the main overall integration center of the autonomic nervous system, coordinating temperature regulation, thirst, food intake and other visceral functions and sending commands through the brainstem and spinal cord.

**Why:** A frequently quoted lecture phrase.
</details>

## 3. The sympathetic nervous system (thoracolumbar)

### Structure
1. **Paravertebral sympathetic chains of ganglia** — beside the vertebral column, connected with the spinal nerves.
2. **Prevertebral ganglia** — **celiac, superior mesenteric, aortico-renal, inferior mesenteric, hypogastric**.
3. **Nerves** from the ganglia to the internal organs.

### Anatomy
- ⭐ **Origin:** spinal cord segments **T1 to L2** (the lateral gray horn); fibers pass first into the **sympathetic chain**, then to the organs.
- **Preganglionic fibers: short** (travel in the ventral root and spinal nerve, via the **white ramus** — myelinated).
- **Postganglionic fibers: long** (return via the **gray ramus** — unmyelinated).
- Targets (figure): superior cervical ganglion → tarsal muscle, lacrimal gland, **radial muscle (dilates pupil)**, salivary glands, heart, bronchial tree, stomach, intestines, **adrenal medulla**, genitalia, **blood vessels, sweat glands and pilomotor muscles**.

### Sympathetic fibers in the skeletal nerves
- Some postganglionic fibers return to the spinal nerves through **gray rami at all levels**.
- They are very small **type C fibers** reaching the whole body.
- They control **blood vessels, sweat glands and piloerector muscles**.
- ⭐ **About 8%** of the fibers in an average skeletal nerve are sympathetic.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- SNS = paravertebral chains + prevertebral ganglia + nerves to organs.
- Origin: T1–L2 (lateral gray horn) → thoracolumbar division.
- Preganglionic fibers short (white ramus), postganglionic fibers long (gray ramus).
- Gray-ramus type C fibers control blood vessels, sweat glands, piloerector muscles; ≈ 8% of skeletal nerve fibers.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure and origin of the sympathetic nervous system.</summary>

**Answer:** It consists of the paravertebral sympathetic chains of ganglia beside the vertebral column, the prevertebral ganglia (celiac, superior mesenteric, aortico-renal, inferior mesenteric, hypogastric) and nerves extending from the ganglia to the internal organs. Its fibers originate from the lateral gray horn of spinal cord segments T1 to L2 and pass first into the sympathetic chain and then to the organs.

**Why:** Three components + origin.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the pre- and postganglionic fibers of the sympathetic system.</summary>

**Answer:** Preganglionic fibers arise in the lateral horn (T1–L2), are short and travel in the ventral root, spinal nerve and white ramus. Postganglionic fibers arise in the ganglia, are long and return to the spinal nerves through the gray rami.

**Why:** Short pre, long post — the opposite of the parasympathetic.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are sympathetic fibers in skeletal nerves important?</summary>

**Answer:** Small type C postganglionic fibers pass back into the spinal nerves through gray rami at all levels and supply blood vessels, sweat glands and piloerector muscles of the hairs in all parts of the body; they make up about 8% of the fibers in an average skeletal nerve.

**Why:** A mark-winning number: 8%.
</details>

## 4. The parasympathetic nervous system (craniosacral)

- **Preganglionic neurons:** in the **brainstem nuclei of cranial nerves III, VII, IX and X** and in the **sacral segments S2–S4**.
- Preganglionic fibers leave the brain in **cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X**; ⭐ **cranial nerve X provides about 75%** of the parasympathetic outflow.
- **Ganglionic neurons are in peripheral ganglia near or within the target organs** (**terminal** and **intramural** ganglia) → **preganglionic fibers are long, postganglionic fibers short**.

| Nerve | Supplies | Ganglion |
|---|---|---|
| **Oculomotor (III)** | eye (ciliary muscle, pupillary sphincter) | **ciliary ganglion** |
| **Facial (VII)** | salivary glands, **anterior ⅔ of tongue**; lacrimal and nasal glands | **submandibular ganglion** (and **pterygopalatine** for lacrimal and nasal glands) |
| **Glossopharyngeal (IX)** | **parotid gland**, **posterior ⅓ of tongue** | **otic ganglion** |
| ⭐ **Vagus (X)** | **~70% of the GIT** (esophagus → half of the large intestine), heart, lungs, stomach | terminal/intramural ganglia |
| **Sacral (pelvic) nerves S2–S4** | **last half of the large intestine to the anus**, **bladder**, **genital tract** | pelvic ganglia |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Parasympathetic = craniosacral: cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X + sacral S2–S4.
- Vagus carries ≈ 75% of parasympathetic outflow; supplies GIT from the esophagus to the middle of the large intestine.
- Ganglia are near/in the target organs: long preganglionic, short postganglionic fibers.
- III → ciliary ganglion; VII → submandibular (and pterygopalatine); IX → otic; S2–S4 → distal colon, bladder, genitalia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the organization of the parasympathetic division.</summary>

**Answer:** Preganglionic neurons are located in the brainstem (nuclei of cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X) and the sacral spinal cord (S2–S4), so it is the craniosacral division. The preganglionic fibers leave in cranial nerves III, VII, IX and X (the vagus giving about 75% of the outflow) and in the sacral (pelvic) nerves. The ganglionic neurons lie in terminal or intramural ganglia near or within the target organs.

**Why:** Origin + nerves + position of ganglia.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the cranial nerves of the parasympathetic system and the structures each supplies.</summary>

**Answer:** Oculomotor (III): the eye via the ciliary ganglion. Facial (VII): salivary glands and the anterior two-thirds of the tongue via the submandibular ganglion (lacrimal and nasal glands via the pterygopalatine ganglion). Glossopharyngeal (IX): the parotid gland and posterior one-third of the tongue via the otic ganglion. Vagus (X): about 70% of the GIT from the esophagus to half of the large intestine, plus the heart and lungs.

**Why:** 4 nerves × target × ganglion.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What do the sacral parasympathetic fibers supply?</summary>

**Answer:** The pelvic nerves (S2–S4) supply the GIT from the last half of the large intestine to the anus, the bladder (detrusor, trigone) and the genital tract.

**Why:** Pelvic organs + distal colon.
</details>

## 5. Somatic vs autonomic nervous system

| Feature | **Autonomic** | **Somatic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Site of origin** | **Sympathetic:** lateral horn of thoracic and lumbar cord; **parasympathetic:** brain and sacral cord | spinal cord for most; muscles of the head: brain |
| **Neurons from CNS to effector** | ⭐ **two-neuron chain** (pre- and postganglionic) | ⭐ **single motor neuron** |
| **Organs innervated** | cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, most exocrine and some endocrine glands | **skeletal muscle** |
| **Neurotransmitter at the effector** | **ACh** (parasympathetic terminals) or **NE** (sympathetic terminals) | **only ACh** |
| **Control** | **involuntary** | **voluntary** |
| **Innervation** | most effector organs **dually innervated** | effector organs singly innervated |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Autonomic = two-neuron chain, involuntary, cardiac/smooth muscle and glands, ACh or NE, dual innervation.
- Somatic = single neuron, voluntary, skeletal muscle, only ACh.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the autonomic and somatic nervous systems.</summary>

**Answer:** The autonomic system arises from the lateral horn of the thoracic/lumbar cord (sympathetic) or the brain and sacral cord (parasympathetic), uses a two-neuron chain (preganglionic and postganglionic), innervates cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands, releases ACh or norepinephrine, is involuntary and most organs are dually innervated. The somatic system arises mainly from the spinal cord, uses a single motor neuron, innervates skeletal muscle, releases only ACh and is voluntary.

**Why:** A six-row table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which neurotransmitters are released by the two autonomic divisions?</summary>

**Answer:** Parasympathetic terminals release acetylcholine (ACh) and sympathetic terminals release norepinephrine (NE) at the effector organs (sympathetic fibers to sweat glands are cholinergic).

**Why:** The somatic system uses only ACh.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is meant by dual innervation?</summary>

**Answer:** Most effector organs receive both sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers, which generally have opposite effects.

**Why:** The basis of the organ-effect table below.
</details>

## 6. Effects of sympathetic vs parasympathetic stimulation

| Organ | **Sympathetic** | **Parasympathetic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Eye – pupil** | **dilated** | **constricted** |
| **Eye – ciliary muscle** | slight relaxation (**far vision**) | **constricted (near vision)** |
| **Glands (nasal, lacrimal, parotid, submandibular, gastric, pancreatic)** | **vasoconstriction and slight secretion** | **copious secretion** (rich in enzymes) |
| **Sweat glands** | **copious sweating (cholinergic)**; sweating on palms | — |
| **Blood vessels** | **most often constricted** | most often little or no effect |
| **Heart** | **↑ rate, ↑ force** | **↓ rate, ↓ force (especially atria)** |
| **Bronchi** | **dilated** | **constricted** |
| **Lung blood vessels** | mildly constricted | dilated |
| **Gut lumen** | **↓ peristalsis and tone** | **↑ peristalsis and tone** |
| **Gut sphincters** | **↑ tone** (most times) | **relaxed** (most times) |
| **Bladder detrusor** | relaxed (slight) | **contracted** |
| **Bladder trigone** | **contracted** | relaxed |
| **Basal metabolism** | **↑ up to 100%** | none |
| **Skeletal muscle** | **↑ glycogenolysis, ↑ strength** | none |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sympathetic: dilates pupil, ↑ heart rate and force, dilates bronchi, ↓ gut motility, tightens sphincters, relaxes detrusor, contracts trigone, ↑ metabolism up to 100%, glycogenolysis.
- Parasympathetic: constricts pupil (near vision), ↓ heart rate (atria), constricts bronchi, ↑ gut motility, relaxes sphincters, contracts detrusor, relaxes trigone, copious secretions; no effect on metabolism or most blood vessels.
- Sweating = sympathetic cholinergic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the effects of sympathetic and parasympathetic stimulation on the eye, heart and lungs.</summary>

**Answer:** Eye: sympathetic dilates the pupil and slightly relaxes the ciliary muscle (far vision); parasympathetic constricts the pupil and contracts the ciliary muscle (near vision). Heart: sympathetic increases rate and force of contraction; parasympathetic slows the rate and decreases force, especially of the atria. Lungs: sympathetic dilates the bronchi (and mildly constricts pulmonary vessels); parasympathetic constricts the bronchi (and dilates pulmonary vessels).

**Why:** Three organs, each with opposite effects.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the effects on the gut and urinary bladder.</summary>

**Answer:** Gut: sympathetic decreases peristalsis and tone and increases sphincter tone; parasympathetic increases peristalsis and tone and relaxes the sphincters. Bladder: sympathetic slightly relaxes the detrusor and contracts the trigone (retention); parasympathetic contracts the detrusor and relaxes the trigone (micturition).

**Why:** "Sympathetic = hold, parasympathetic = empty".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the metabolic and sweat-gland effects of sympathetic stimulation?</summary>

**Answer:** Sympathetic stimulation can increase basal metabolism by up to 100% and increases glycogenolysis and strength in skeletal muscle; it causes copious sweating through cholinergic sympathetic fibers (and sweating of the palms). Parasympathetic stimulation has no effect on basal metabolism.

**Why:** Sweat glands are the exception — sympathetic but cholinergic.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Sympathetic = thoracolumbar (T1–L2)**; **parasympathetic = craniosacral (III, VII, IX, X + S2–S4)**.
- Sympathetic: **short preganglionic, long postganglionic**; parasympathetic: **long preganglionic, short postganglionic**.
- **Vagus** carries **~75%** of parasympathetic outflow and supplies **~70% of the GIT**.
- **Sweat glands**: sympathetic but **cholinergic** (ACh).
- **Ganglia = PNS; nuclei = CNS.**
- Parasympathetic stimulation has **no effect on basal metabolic rate**; sympathetic raises it up to 100%.
- Sympathetic **constricts** most blood vessels; parasympathetic has little or no effect on them.
- Ciliary muscle: **parasympathetic = near vision**; sympathetic = far vision.

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. NS → CNS + PNS; motor PNS → **somatic (voluntary, skeletal muscle)** + **autonomic (involuntary)**; ANS = **sympathetic ("fight or flight")** + **parasympathetic ("rest and digest")**.
2. **Tone** = continuous basal activity of both divisions.
3. **Preganglionic neurons in the CNS; postganglionic in ganglia.**
4. **Hypothalamus** = "the boss"; medulla = vasomotor/respiratory/swallowing/coughing/vomiting; pons = pneumotaxic/micturition.
5. **SNS:** chains + prevertebral ganglia (celiac, superior and inferior mesenteric, aortico-renal, hypogastric); **T1–L2**; type C fibers = 8% of skeletal nerve fibers.
6. **PNS:** III (ciliary), VII (submandibular/pterygopalatine), IX (otic), X (75%, 70% of GIT), S2–S4 (pelvic).
7. **ANS vs somatic:** two neurons vs one; ACh/NE vs ACh only; involuntary vs voluntary; dual innervation.
8. **Organ effects:** pupil dilated/constricted; heart ↑/↓; bronchi dilated/constricted; gut ↓/↑; sphincters ↑/relaxed; detrusor relaxed/contracted; trigone contracted/relaxed; metabolism ↑ 100%/none.
