## 1. Deep fascia of the thigh

- **Fascia lata:** strong inelastic stocking around the thigh; **thickened laterally = iliotibial tract**; **supero-medial part = saphenous opening.**
- **Iliotibial tract:** from the **iliac crest tubercle** to the **lateral patellar border, lateral tibial condyle, knee capsule and fibular head**; receives **tensor fasciae latae (anterior border)** and **superficial ¾ of gluteus maximus (posterior border).** **Function:** **stabilizes the knee in extension and partial flexion**; **supports the knee against gravity** when leaning forward.
- **Saphenous opening:** **4 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle**; **falciform margin** (upper, lateral, lower); **cribriform fascia** roof pierced by **superficial inguinal vessels, great saphenous vein and lymphatics**; **femoral vein deep to its center, artery deep to its lateral margin, femoral canal medially.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Iliotibial tract: TFL anteriorly, gluteus maximus posteriorly.
- Saphenous opening 4 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the iliotibial tract and its functions.</summary>

**Answer:** A lateral thickening of the fascia lata from the iliac crest to the tibia and fibula that receives TFL and gluteus maximus and stabilizes the knee in extension and partial flexion.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the saphenous opening.</summary>

**Answer:** An oval opening 4 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle with a sharp falciform margin, closed by cribriform fascia pierced by superficial vessels and the great saphenous vein.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What lies deep to the saphenous opening?</summary>

**Answer:** The femoral vein centrally, the femoral artery laterally and the femoral canal medially.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

## 2. Femoral triangle and femoral sheath

- **Boundaries:** **lateral: medial border of sartorius; medial: medial border of adductor longus; base: inguinal ligament; apex: meeting of the borders; floor: iliopsoas, pectineus, adductor longus; roof: skin, superficial fascia (long saphenous vein, superficial vessels, inguinal nodes, ilioinguinal branches) and deep fascia with the saphenous opening.**
- **Contents (lateral → medial):** **femoral nerve → femoral sheath (artery, vein, canal) → deep inguinal nodes.** **Vein medial to the artery at the base, behind it at the apex.**
- **Femoral sheath:** **below the middle of the inguinal ligament, 1.5 inch**; **anterior wall = fascia transversalis; posterior wall = fascia iliaca**; **3 compartments:** **lateral (artery + femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve), middle (vein), medial (femoral canal).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Femoral nerve lies outside the sheath, lateral.
- Sheath compartments: artery, vein, canal (lateral to medial).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give the boundaries of the femoral triangle.</summary>

**Answer:** Sartorius laterally, adductor longus medially, inguinal ligament as base, with iliopsoas, pectineus and adductor longus forming the floor.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the contents from lateral to medial.</summary>

**Answer:** Femoral nerve, femoral artery, femoral vein, femoral canal (within the sheath) and deep inguinal lymph nodes.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the femoral sheath.</summary>

**Answer:** A 1.5 inch sheath from fascia transversalis (front) and fascia iliaca (back) with lateral (artery), middle (vein) and medial (canal) compartments.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

## 3. Femoral artery, profunda femoris and anastomoses

- **Femoral artery:** begins at the **mid-inguinal point** (between symphysis pubis and ASIS) as the **external iliac continuation**; ends at the **adductor hiatus** (junction of upper ⅔ and lower ⅓) as the **popliteal artery**; **upper half superficial (triangle), lower half deep (adductor canal)**; **pulse 2-3 cm below the mid-inguinal point**; **psoas major separates it from the femoral head.**
- **Branches:** **triangle: 3 superficial (superficial epigastric, superficial external pudendal, superficial circumflex iliac) + 3 deep (profunda femoris, deep external pudendal, muscular)**; **canal: muscular, descending genicular.**
- **Profunda femoris:** **largest branch**, **chief supply of the thigh muscles**; gives **lateral and medial circumflex femoral arteries** and **four perforating branches.**

| Anastomosis | Site | Formation | Importance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trochanteric** | **Floor of the trochanteric fossa** | **Superior and inferior gluteal + ascending branches of the lateral and medial circumflex femoral** | **Retinacular arteries to the femoral head**; links internal iliac and femoral |
| **Cruciate** | **Upper back of the thigh at the lesser trochanter** | **Transverse branches of both circumflex arteries, descending inferior gluteal, ascending first perforator** | **Collateral flow if the femoral artery is blocked proximal to the profunda** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Femoral artery: mid-inguinal point to the adductor hiatus.
- Anastomoses link the internal iliac and femoral systems.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the course of the femoral artery.</summary>

**Answer:** From the mid-inguinal point as the external iliac continuation through the femoral triangle and adductor canal to the adductor hiatus, where it becomes the popliteal artery.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the branches of the femoral artery.</summary>

**Answer:** In the triangle superficial epigastric, superficial external pudendal, superficial circumflex iliac, profunda femoris, deep external pudendal and muscular branches; in the canal muscular branches and the descending genicular artery.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the trochanteric and cruciate anastomoses.</summary>

**Answer:** Trochanteric: floor of the trochanteric fossa, supplies the femoral head; cruciate: cross-shaped at the lesser trochanter, collateral for the femoral artery.

**Why:** Slides 14-15.
</details>

## 4. Femoral nerve

- **Nerve of the anterior thigh**, **lumbar plexus (L2, 3, 4)**; enters **deep to the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral artery, outside the sheath, on iliacus**; **divides about an inch below the ligament.**
- **Branches:** **muscular** (sartorius, four heads of **quadriceps**, part of pectineus, iliacus); **articular** (**rectus femoris → hip; vastus medialis → knee**); **cutaneous** (**medial and intermediate cutaneous nerves of the thigh; saphenous nerve** to leg and foot).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Femoral nerve L2-L4; saphenous is its cutaneous branch.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the femoral nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** L2-L4 nerve of the anterior thigh entering lateral to the femoral artery outside the sheath and dividing into muscular, articular and cutaneous branches.

**Why:** Slides 17-19.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List its muscular branches.</summary>

**Answer:** Sartorius, the four heads of quadriceps femoris, part of pectineus and iliacus.

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List its cutaneous and articular branches.</summary>

**Answer:** Medial and intermediate cutaneous nerves of the thigh and the saphenous nerve; articular to the hip (nerve to rectus femoris) and knee (nerve to vastus medialis).

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

## 5. Hip joint: structure, capsule and ligaments

- **Synovial polyaxial ball-and-socket**; **weight bearing.** **Head of femur** (⅔ sphere, hyaline cartilage) in the **lunate surface of the acetabulum**, completed by the **transverse acetabular ligament** and deepened by the **labrum.**
- **Capsule:** strong; to the **acetabular margin** and to the femur at the **intertrochanteric line** anteriorly and **middle of the back of the neck** posteriorly → **anteriorly the neck is intracapsular; posteriorly the lateral half is extracapsular.** **Outer longitudinal** and **inner circular (zona orbicularis)** fibers; reflected longitudinal fibers form the **retinacula** (carry retinacular arteries; hold fragments in neck fractures).
- **Synovial membrane:** lines the capsule, sheathes the **ligament of the head**, covers the intracapsular neck and labrum.
- **Ligaments:** **intracapsular:** **ligament of the head** (acetabular fossa → fovea; **minor artery from the obturator**) and **transverse acetabular ligament.** **Extracapsular:**

| Ligament | Location and attachments | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Iliofemoral** (inverted Y) | **Anterior; below AIIS → intertrochanteric line (two places)** | **Strongest hip ligament (2nd in the body)**; **prevents hyperextension**; keeps body erect |
| **Pubofemoral** (triangular) | **Anteroinferior; iliopubic eminence and obturator membrane** | **Prevents excessive abduction** |
| **Ischiofemoral** | **Posterior; ischium → greater trochanter** | **Weakest**; **limits medial rotation and adduction** (posterior dislocation) |

- **Spiral arrangement:** taut in **extension**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Iliofemoral strongest, ischiofemoral weakest.
- Anteriorly the neck is intracapsular.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the extracapsular ligaments.</summary>

**Answer:** Iliofemoral (anterior, inverted Y, strongest, prevents hyperextension), pubofemoral (anteroinferior, prevents excessive abduction) and ischiofemoral (posterior, weakest, limits medial rotation and adduction).

**Why:** Slides 26-29.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the intracapsular ligaments and their functions?</summary>

**Answer:** The ligament of the head (stability and a minor obturator artery branch) and the transverse acetabular ligament (completes the socket).

**Why:** Slide 25.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is the capsule attached and what is the clinical importance of the retinacula?</summary>

**Answer:** To the acetabular margin and femoral intertrochanteric line and back of the neck; the retinacula carry the blood supply to the head and neck.

**Why:** Slide 23.
</details>

## 6. Stability, relations, vascular and nerve supply

- **Stability:** **bony** (head fits the deep acetabulum), **ligamentous** (strong capsule, spiral ligaments, especially iliofemoral) and **muscular.** **Reciprocal:** **anterior ligaments strongest, flexors fewer**; **posterior ligaments weakest, medial rotators many and strong.**
- **Relations:** **anterior:** floor of the femoral triangle; **superior:** reflected head of rectus and gluteal muscles; **inferior:** obturator externus and pectineus; **posterior:** piriformis, obturator internus with gemelli, quadratus femoris; **sciatic nerve** is the **at-risk posterior relation** (posterior dislocation).
- **Movements:** **transverse axis:** flexion and extension; **anteroposterior axis:** abduction and adduction; **vertical axis:** medial and lateral rotation.
- **Blood supply of the femoral head:** **(1) nutrient artery; (2) artery of the ligament of the head (obturator); (3) retinacular arteries (main supply, from the trochanteric anastomosis).** **High intracapsular neck fracture → avascular necrosis**; **medial circumflex femoral artery** provides most retinacular arteries.
- **Nerve supply:** **femoral, obturator, superior gluteal, nerve to quadratus femoris** → pain referred to the **knee.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Retinacular arteries are the main supply of the head.
- Sciatic nerve at risk in posterior dislocation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What gives the hip its stability?</summary>

**Answer:** Bony fit of head in acetabulum, strong capsule and spiral ligaments (especially iliofemoral) and strong surrounding muscles.

**Why:** Slide 30.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the blood supply of the femoral head and its clinical importance.</summary>

**Answer:** Nutrient artery, artery of the ligament of the head and retinacular arteries (main); retinacular supply is cut in high intracapsular neck fractures causing avascular necrosis.

**Why:** Slides 35-36.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the posterior relations and the nerve at risk.</summary>

**Answer:** Piriformis, obturator internus with the gemelli and quadratus femoris; the sciatic nerve is at risk in posterior dislocation.

**Why:** Slide 31.
</details>

## 7. Hip versus shoulder

| Feature | Hip | Shoulder |
|---|---|---|
| **Function** | **Stability and weight bearing** | **Motility** |
| **Bony factor** | Head fits the acetabulum well | **4:1 disproportion** of head to glenoid |
| **Muscles** | Strong muscles all around | **Rotator cuff** |
| **Ligaments** | **Iliofemoral, ischiofemoral, pubofemoral** (spiral) | **Glenohumeral, coracohumeral, transverse humeral, coracoacromial** |
| **Movement** | **Limited:** flexion 110-120°, extension 10-15°, abduction 30-50° | **Free:** flexion 180°, extension 45-60°, abduction 150° |
| **Dislocation** | **Extremely rare, usually posterior**; sciatic nerve damage | **Common**, inferior and anterior (94%); **axillary nerve** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hip: stable; shoulder: mobile.
- Shoulder dislocation common; hip dislocation rare and posterior.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the stability of the hip and shoulder.</summary>

**Answer:** The hip is stable (deep socket, strong ligaments and muscles) whereas the shoulder is mobile and unstable (4:1 head-to-glenoid disproportion, rotator cuff).

**Why:** Slides 38-39.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare dislocations.</summary>

**Answer:** Hip dislocation is rare, posterior, needs great force and may injure the sciatic nerve; shoulder dislocation is common, usually inferior and anterior, and may injure the axillary nerve.

**Why:** Slide 40.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare ranges of movement.</summary>

**Answer:** Hip: flexion 110-120°, extension 10-15°, abduction 30-50°; shoulder: flexion 180°, extension 45-60°, abduction 150°.

**Why:** Slide 39.
</details>
