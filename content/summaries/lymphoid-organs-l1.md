## 1. Lymphoid nodules and tonsils

- ⭐ **Lymphoid nodules** = isolated immune cells in the **mucosa of the digestive (tonsils, Peyer's patches, appendix), respiratory, reproductive and urinary systems = MALT**; also in **lymph nodes and spleen, but not in the thymus.**
- **Tonsils** = **partially encapsulated** lymphoid tissue under the epithelium of the **oral cavity and pharynx**: **palatine, pharyngeal, lingual.**
- **Palatine tonsil:** posterior **soft palate**; **stratified squamous epithelium**; **10-20 crypts** (epithelial invaginations); a **dense connective tissue capsule** that **limits spread of infection**; **minor mucous salivary glands** in the capsule; **no afferent lymphatics and no blood sinuses.**
- **Tonsillitis** = inflammation of the tonsils.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- MALT is found in gut, respiratory, genital and urinary mucosa, not in the thymus.
- Palatine tonsil: crypts, stratified squamous epithelium, no afferent lymphatics.

</div>

## 2. Lymph node structure

- ⭐ **Bean-shaped, encapsulated**; capsule sends **trabeculae** inside.
- **All lymph is filtered by at least one node** before returning to the blood.
- **Convex surface:** entry of **afferent lymphatics.** **Hilum (concavity):** **arteries and nerves enter; veins and efferent lymphatics leave.**
- **Three regions:** **cortex, paracortex, medulla.**

| Region | Features |
|---|---|
| **Cortex** | Reticular cells, macrophages, APCs, lymphocytes; **lymphoid nodules** (primary without, secondary **with germinal centers**), mainly **B cells**; **subcapsular sinuses** and **cortical sinuses** |
| **Paracortex** | No precise boundaries; **T-cell zone**, no B-cell nodules; **venules = entry point of lymphocytes from blood** |
| **Medulla** | **Medullary cords** (B cells, plasma cells, macrophages) and **medullary sinuses** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cortex = B cells and nodules; paracortex = T cells; medulla = cords and sinuses.
- Afferent at the convex surface; efferent at the hilum.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of a lymph node.</summary>

**Answer:** A bean-shaped encapsulated organ with trabeculae, a convex surface for afferent lymphatics and a hilum for arteries, nerves, veins and efferent lymphatics, divided into cortex, paracortex and medulla.

**Why:** Slides "Lymph Nodes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What does the cortex contain?</summary>

**Answer:** Reticular cells, macrophages, APCs and lymphocytes; B-cell lymphoid nodules (primary or secondary); subcapsular sinuses and cortical sinuses.

**Why:** Slide "The cortex".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is the paracortex recognized?</summary>

**Answer:** It lacks B-cell nodules and accumulates T cells (shown by immunohistochemistry); its venules are an important entry point for lymphocytes.

**Why:** Slide "The paracortex".
</details>

## 3. Lymph flow and recirculation of lymphocytes

- ⭐ **Flow:** **afferent lymphatics → subcapsular sinus → cortical sinuses → medullary sinuses → efferent lymphatics at the hilum.**
- Lymphocytes leaving by efferent lymphatics reach the **bloodstream**, may enter tissues and return with lymph to another node.
- **90%** return by crossing **high endothelial venules (HEVs)** in the **paracortex** (**tall cuboidal endothelium**).
- **Medullary cords:** extensions from the paracortex with **B cells, plasma cells, macrophages**; separated by **medullary sinuses** continuous with the cortical sinuses.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- HEVs in the paracortex = main lymphocyte entry (90%).
- Medullary sinuses deliver lymph to the efferent vessel.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Trace the lymph through a node.</summary>

**Answer:** Afferent vessels, subcapsular sinus, cortical sinuses, medullary sinuses, then efferent lymphatics at the hilum.

**Why:** Slide "Recirculation of Lymphocytes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are HEVs?</summary>

**Answer:** Postcapillary venules of the paracortex lined by tall cuboidal cells through which about 90% of lymphocytes re-enter a node.

**Why:** Slide "Recirculation of Lymphocytes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the medulla.</summary>

**Answer:** Medullary cords (B cells, plasma cells, macrophages) separated by medullary sinuses that are continuous with the cortical sinuses and deliver lymph to the efferent lymphatic at the hilum.

**Why:** Slide "Medulla".
</details>

## 4. Medical application

- ⭐ **Metastasis:** each satellite node drains a limited region, so **tumor cells reach nodes** and spread through **efferent lymph and blood vessels.**
- **Infection:** nodes **enlarge**, palpable as signs of inflammation, with **multiple germinal centers and active proliferation.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Nodes trap and spread tumor cells.
- Swollen nodes = germinal center proliferation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why do tumors metastasize to lymph nodes?</summary>

**Answer:** Each satellite node receives lymph from a limited region, so malignant cells reach it and spread onward via efferent lymph and blood vessels.

**Why:** Slide "Medical application".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why do nodes swell in infection?</summary>

**Answer:** They develop multiple germinal centers with active cell proliferation.

**Why:** Slide "Medical application".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the tissue in which nodules are absent.</summary>

**Answer:** The thymus.
