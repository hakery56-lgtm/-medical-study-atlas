## 1. Bone cells and remodeling

| Cell | Function | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **Osteocytes** | **Sense mechanical strain; direct osteoclast and osteoblast activity** | **From osteoblasts; linked by fingerlike extensions** |
| **Osteoclasts** | **Dissolve and resorb bone** | **Bone marrow (related to white blood cells); large, multinucleated** |
| **Osteoblasts** | **Fill the cavity with osteoid and catalyze mineralization; become lining cells** | **Bone marrow (related to structural cells)** |
| **Lining cells** | **Cover bone not remodeling; regulate calcium movement** | **From finished osteoblasts** |

- **Osteoid** = **unmineralized organic matrix, mostly collagen**, in which **hydroxyapatite** (calcium phosphate crystals) is embedded.
- **Estrogen deficiency:** **osteoclasts live longer (↓ apoptosis) → more resorption**; compensatory formation → **high-turnover state, bone loss, perforated trabecular plates.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Osteocyte senses; osteoclast resorbs; osteoblast forms.
- Estrogen deficiency prolongs osteoclast life.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the four bone cell types.</summary>

**Answer:** Osteocytes sense strain; osteoclasts resorb bone; osteoblasts form osteoid and mineralize it; lining cells cover resting bone and regulate calcium movement.

**Why:** Slides 2–3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is osteoid?</summary>

**Answer:** The unmineralized organic matrix, mostly collagen, in which hydroxyapatite crystals are embedded.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does estrogen deficiency cause bone loss?</summary>

**Answer:** Osteoclasts live longer and resorb more; a high-turnover state develops with perforation of trabecular plates.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

## 2. Osteoporosis and risk factors

- **Osteoporosis:** **low bone mass + structural deterioration → fragility and fractures (hip, spine, wrist).**
- **Non-modifiable:** **gender, age, body size, ethnicity, family history.**
- **Modifiable:** **sex hormone levels, anorexia, low calcium/vitamin D diet, glucocorticoids or some anticonvulsants, inactivity, smoking.**
- **Osteopenia:** **less severe loss** than osteoporosis; risk higher than normal but lower than osteoporosis.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Fractures: hip, spine, wrist.
- Osteopenia is the milder stage.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define osteoporosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Low bone mass and structural deterioration of bone, causing fragility and fractures, especially of the hip, spine and wrist.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List modifiable risk factors.</summary>

**Answer:** Sex hormone levels, anorexia, low calcium and vitamin D diet, glucocorticoids/anticonvulsants, inactivity and smoking.

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Distinguish osteopenia from osteoporosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Osteopenia has less severe bone loss: fracture risk is above normal but below osteoporosis.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

## 3. Calcium: requirements, sources and absorption

- **Plasma calcium (this lecture):** **45% ionized (active), 45% protein-bound (albumin), 10% complexed (citrate, sulfate, phosphate).**
- **Requirements:** **1–3 y 500 mg; 4–8 y 800 mg; 9–18 y 1300 mg; 19–50 y 1000 mg; 51+ y 1500 mg; pregnancy/lactation 1000 mg.**
- **Sources:** milk and dairy, green leafy vegetables, seafood, almonds, broccoli, enriched soy/rice milk, figs, soybeans, tofu.
- **Absorption:** **duodenum (first and second parts)**, **active** (carrier protein, Ca-ATPase; dominant at low intake) and **paracellular passive** (dominant at high intake).
- **↑ absorption:** **calcitriol, PTH, acidic pH, lysine, arginine.** **↓ absorption:** **phytic acid, oxalates, phosphate, magnesium, caffeine.**
- **Functions:** bone/teeth mineralization, neuromuscular excitability, coagulation, secretion, membrane integrity, enzyme reactions, hormone/neurotransmitter release, second messenger.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Absorbed in the duodenum.
- Phytates and oxalates inhibit absorption.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is calcium absorbed?</summary>

**Answer:** In the duodenum by active transcellular transport (carrier and Ca-ATPase, dominant at low intake) and passive paracellular transport (dominant at high intake).

**Why:** Slides 28–29.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name factors that increase and decrease absorption.</summary>

**Answer:** Increase: calcitriol, PTH, acidic pH, lysine, arginine. Decrease: phytic acid, oxalates, phosphate, magnesium, caffeine.

**Why:** Slide 28.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the daily calcium requirement by age.</summary>

**Answer:** 500 mg (1–3 y), 800 mg (4–8 y), 1300 mg (9–18 y), 1000 mg (19–50 y), 1500 mg (51+); 1000 mg in pregnancy and lactation.

**Why:** Slide 26.
</details>

## 4. PTH and calcitonin

- **Low calcium → parathyroid → PTH → osteoclasts release calcium** (also ↑ renal Ca reabsorption, ↑ calcitriol).
- **PTH:** from **chief cells**, **half-life 3–4 min**, normal **1.3–6.8 pmol/L**; **↑ plasma Ca²⁺, ↓ plasma phosphate** (renal phosphate excretion ↑); **bone resorption is fast, gut absorption slow.**
- **Regulation:** **plasma Ca²⁺ dominant, inverse relation**; **Ca²⁺-sensing G-protein-coupled receptor** (PLC and adenylate cyclase; **falling Ca²⁺ → ↑ cAMP → PTH secreted**); **high calcitriol inhibits PTH gene**; **no direct phosphate control.**
- **Calcitonin:** polypeptide from **thyroid C cells**; stimulated by **↑ plasma Ca²⁺**; **physiological antagonist of PTH**; **inhibits osteoclasts → rapid fall in Ca²⁺.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PTH ↑ Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate.
- Calcitonin inhibits osteoclasts.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List PTH actions.</summary>

**Answer:** Osteoclastic bone resorption (fast), increased renal calcium reabsorption, renal phosphate excretion, calcitriol synthesis and slower intestinal absorption.

**Why:** Slides 42–43.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is PTH secretion regulated?</summary>

**Answer:** Inversely by plasma Ca²⁺ via a G-protein-coupled calcium receptor; falling Ca²⁺ raises cAMP and PTH is secreted; calcitriol inhibits PTH gene expression; phosphate has no direct control.

**Why:** Slides 45–46.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe calcitonin.</summary>

**Answer:** From thyroid C cells, stimulated by high calcium; inhibits osteoclasts and bone resorption, causing a rapid fall in Ca²⁺; antagonizes PTH.

**Why:** Slide 47.
</details>

## 5. Vitamin D

- **Synthesis:** **7-dehydrocholesterol → (UV 290–320 nm) → previtamin D → cholecalciferol**; **liver 25-hydroxylation → calcidiol**; **kidney 1α-hydroxylase → calcitriol.**
- **1α-hydroxylase ↑ by:** **↓ Ca²⁺, ↑ PTH, ↓ phosphate** (also estrogen, prolactin, GH in pregnancy, lactation, growth).
- **Actions:** **↑ intestinal Ca²⁺ and phosphate absorption, ↑ renal Ca²⁺ reabsorption, ↑ osteoclast activity (via osteoblast signal).** Induces **calcium-binding protein (CaBP)** via a steroid-hormone-like receptor.
- **Bone:** **osteoblasts (not osteoclasts) have vitamin D receptors**; deficiency → **excess osteoid, rickets** (bowed legs, bone pain, delayed growth).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Liver 25-OH, kidney 1α-OH.
- Deficiency → rickets.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Outline vitamin D activation.</summary>

**Answer:** UV converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to cholecalciferol in skin; the liver makes calcidiol; the kidney makes calcitriol via 1α-hydroxylase.

**Why:** Slide 34.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What increases 1α-hydroxylase?</summary>

**Answer:** Low calcium, high PTH and low phosphate; also estrogen, prolactin and growth hormone.

**Why:** Slides 35 and 38.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe vitamin D action on bone.</summary>

**Answer:** Osteoblasts have the receptors and signal osteoclasts to resorb bone; deficiency causes excess osteoid and rickets.

**Why:** Slide 40.
</details>
