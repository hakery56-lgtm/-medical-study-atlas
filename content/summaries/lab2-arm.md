</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which muscles are labelled in the axilla dissection?</summary>

**Why:** Straight from the slide labels.
</details>

## 2. Shoulder region questions

### Deltopectoral approach to the shoulder joint
1. **Skin incision** along the **anterior margin of the deltoid** → exposes the **deltopectoral groove**, where the ⭐ **cephalic vein** is isolated.
2. **Pectoralis major and deltoid** are **retracted** from each other → expose the **coracoid process**.
3. The **conjoint tendon** (⭐ **pectoralis minor + short head of biceps**) attached to its tip is **divided ~2 cm below the coracoid** → exposes the **subscapularis tendon**.
4. **Subscapularis** is cut → reach the **capsule of the shoulder joint**.

### Scapular anastomosis
- **Why doesn't ligation of the 3rd part of the subclavian or 1st part of the axillary artery cut off the limb?** → A **profuse scapular anastomosis** between branches of the **1st part of the subclavian** and the **3rd part of the axillary** artery provides **collateral circulation**.
- The axillary artery may be ligated **between the thyrocervical trunk and the subscapular artery**; blood flow in the **subscapular artery is then reversed**.
- Arteries involved: **suprascapular, dorsal scapular, circumflex scapular (from subscapular)**, subscapular, thoracodorsal.

### Deltoid intramuscular injection
- Preferred because it is **well developed in most adults** and **easily accessible**.
- ⭐ Inject into the **lower half** of the muscle to **avoid the axillary nerve** (which runs around the surgical neck, ~5 cm below the acromion).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Deltopectoral approach: incision on anterior deltoid border → cephalic vein → retract pectoralis major/deltoid → divide conjoint tendon 2 cm below coracoid → cut subscapularis → capsule.
- Scapular anastomosis links the 1st part of subclavian with the 3rd part of axillary → collateral circulation; subscapular flow reverses.
- Deltoid injection: lower half, to spare the axillary nerve.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the deltopectoral approach to the shoulder joint.</summary>

**Answer:** A skin incision is placed along the anterior margin of the deltoid to expose the deltopectoral groove, where the cephalic vein is isolated. Pectoralis major and deltoid are retracted apart to expose the coracoid process. The conjoint tendon (pectoralis minor and short head of biceps) attached to its tip is divided about 2 cm below the coracoid to expose the subscapularis tendon, which is cut to reach the capsule of the shoulder joint.

**Why:** Four ordered steps.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does ligation of the 3rd part of the subclavian or 1st part of the axillary artery not interfere with the blood supply of the upper limb?</summary>

**Answer:** Because of the profuse scapular anastomosis between branches of the first part of the subclavian artery (suprascapular, dorsal scapular) and the third part of the axillary artery (subscapular, circumflex scapular), which provides a collateral circulation. If the axillary artery is ligated between the thyrocervical trunk and the subscapular artery, blood reaches the limb by reversed flow in the subscapular artery.

**Why:** Collaterals + the ligation site.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the deltoid a preferred site for intramuscular injection, and where exactly do you inject?</summary>

**Answer:** It is a well-developed muscle in most adults and easily accessible. The injection must be given in the lower half of the muscle to avoid injuring the axillary nerve.

**Why:** Reason + safe site.
</details>

## 3. Veins

### Superficial vs deep veins
| Feature | Superficial veins | Deep veins |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | **subcutaneous tissue** | **deep to the deep fascia** |
| **Relation to arteries** | **do not** accompany arteries | usually **two veins accompany an artery** (**venae comitantes**) and share its name |
| **Function** | ⭐ **thermal regulation** | **drain blood** |

### Why the basilic vein is chosen for central venous catheterization
- Catheter route: **basilic → axillary → subclavian → brachiocephalic → superior vena cava**.
- The basilic vein **increases in diameter** as it ascends from the cubital fossa to the axillary vein — the cephalic does not (it **divides into branches** as it enters the deltopectoral groove).
- The basilic is **directly in line with the axillary vein**, whereas the cephalic joins it **at a right angle where there is a valve**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Superficial = subcutaneous, no artery, thermoregulation; deep = under deep fascia, venae comitantes, drain blood.
- Basilic for central lines: widens as it ascends, straight into axillary vein; cephalic branches and joins at a right angle with a valve.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare superficial and deep veins.</summary>

**Answer:** Superficial veins lie in the subcutaneous tissue, do not accompany arteries and function in thermal regulation. Deep veins lie deep to the deep fascia, usually as two venae comitantes accompanying an artery and sharing its name, and function to drain blood.

**Why:** Location, relation, function.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the basilic vein preferred over the cephalic vein for central venous catheterization?</summary>

**Answer:** The catheter passes basilic → axillary → subclavian → brachiocephalic → SVC. The basilic vein increases in diameter as it ascends and continues directly in line with the axillary vein, while the cephalic vein divides into multiple branches as it enters the deltopectoral groove and joins the axillary vein at a right angle where there is a valve.

**Why:** Size + straight course vs branching + angle + valve.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the route of a catheter passed through the basilic vein?</summary>

**Answer:** Basilic vein → axillary vein → subclavian vein → brachiocephalic vein → superior vena cava.

**Why:** A one-line sequence question.
</details>

## 4. Fractures of the humerus

### Displacement depends on the site
| Site | Proximal segment | Distal segment |
|---|---|---|
| **Above the bicipital groove** | **abducted** (greater tuberosity muscles, mainly **supraspinatus**) | **adducted** (bicipital groove muscles) |
| **Above the deltoid tuberosity** | **adducted** (bicipital groove muscles) | **abducted & pulled up** (**deltoid**) |
| **Below the deltoid tuberosity** | **abducted** (**deltoid**) | **adducted & pulled up** (**biceps & triceps**) |

### Arteries and nerves at risk (4 sites)
| Fracture site | Nerve | Artery | Deformity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surgical neck** | **axillary** | **posterior circumflex humeral** | **flat shoulder** |
| **Midshaft (spiral groove)** | **radial** | **profunda brachii** | **wrist drop** |
| **Supracondylar** | **median** | **brachial** | Volkmann's contracture |
| **Medial epicondyle** | **ulnar** | **ulnar collateral** | **claw hand** |

### Volkmann's ischaemic contracture
Supracondylar fracture → proximal fragment displaced **forward & downward** → **compresses the brachial artery and median nerve** → collaterals fail → **ischaemia and necrosis of forearm flexors** → **fibrosis, thinning and shortening** → ⭐ **flexion deformity of the wrist and fingers**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Know the 3-row displacement table (bicipital groove, deltoid tuberosity).
- Surgical neck → axillary; spiral groove → radial; supracondylar → brachial a. + median; medial epicondyle → ulnar.
- Volkmann's = brachial artery compression → flexor necrosis → fibrous flexion deformity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. In humeral shaft fractures the displacement depends on the site. Explain.</summary>

**Answer:** Above the bicipital groove the proximal segment is abducted by the muscles attached to the greater tuberosity (mainly supraspinatus) while the distal segment is adducted by the muscles attached to the bicipital groove. Above the deltoid tuberosity the proximal segment is adducted by the bicipital groove muscles and the distal segment abducted and pulled up by the deltoid. Below the deltoid tuberosity the proximal segment is abducted by the deltoid and the distal segment adducted and pulled up by biceps and triceps.

**Why:** The most repeated table of the arm.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which arteries and nerves are liable to damage at the four sites of humeral fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** Surgical neck — axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery; midshaft — radial nerve and profunda brachii artery; supracondylar — brachial artery and median nerve; medial epicondyle — ulnar nerve and ulnar collateral artery.

**Why:** 4 sites × nerve + artery.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain Volkmann's ischaemic contracture.</summary>

**Answer:** In supracondylar fracture the proximal fragment is displaced forward and downward, compressing the brachial artery and median nerve. When collateral circulation cannot compensate, the forearm flexor muscles become ischaemic and necrotic; they are replaced by fibrous tissue that thins and shortens them, causing a flexion deformity of the wrist and fingers.

**Why:** Sequence of events.
</details>

## 5. Nerve injuries around the arm and elbow

### Radial nerve injured in the radial groove — elbow extension preserved
Because the **long and medial heads of triceps** (chief extensor of the elbow) are **supplied by the radial nerve in the axilla**, **before** it enters the radial groove. (Loss: **wrist drop**.)

### Median nerve injury at the elbow
- **Motor:** **loss of pronation**; **weakened wrist flexion**; **ape hand** (thenar atrophy); ⭐ **benediction hand** — on making a fist, the **index and middle fingers stay extended** (paralysed flexors).
- **Sensory:** loss over the **lateral half of the palm and lateral 3½ digits**.

### Ulnar nerve injury at the elbow
- **Motor:** ⭐ **partial claw hand** of the **ring and little fingers** — **hyperextension at MP** and **flexion at IP** joints (paralysed intrinsic muscles); the **1st and 2nd lumbricals are spared** (median nerve); **flattened hypothenar eminence**; **loss of finger abduction and adduction** (interossei).
- **Sensory:** loss over the **palmar and dorsal medial ⅓ of the hand**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Radial nerve in the groove → wrist drop, but elbow extension preserved (triceps branches arise in the axilla).
- Median at elbow → loss of pronation, ape hand, benediction hand, lateral 3½ digits numb.
- Ulnar at elbow → partial claw (ring + little), hypothenar flattening, lost finger ab/adduction, medial ⅓ numb.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. A patient with radial nerve injury in the radial groove can still extend his elbow. Explain.</summary>

**Answer:** Because the long and medial heads of triceps, the chief extensor of the elbow, receive their branches from the radial nerve in the axilla, before it enters the radial groove; the injury is distal to these branches.

**Why:** Level of branching explains the sparing.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the effects of median nerve injury at the elbow?</summary>

**Answer:** Motor: loss of pronation, weakened flexion at the wrist, ape hand deformity (thenar atrophy), and benediction hand (on making a fist the index and middle fingers remain extended due to paralysis of their flexors). Sensory: loss over the lateral half of the palm and lateral 3½ digits.

**Why:** Motor + sensory.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the effects of ulnar nerve injury at the elbow?</summary>

**Answer:** Motor: partial claw hand of the ring and little fingers (hyperextension at MP and flexion at IP joints from paralysis of the intrinsic muscles; the 1st and 2nd lumbricals are spared because they are supplied by the median nerve), flattening of the hypothenar eminence and loss of abduction and adduction of the fingers (interossei). Sensory: loss over the palmar and dorsal medial third of the hand.

**Why:** Claw + hypothenar + interossei + sensory.
</details>

## 6. Cubital fossa — clinical questions

### Clinical importance
1. Common site for **venipuncture**.
2. Site for **recording blood pressure**.
3. Its contents are liable to injury in **supracondylar fracture** of the humerus.

### Contents (medial → lateral)
**Median nerve** (between the 2 heads of pronator teres) → **brachial artery** end with **radial** (superficial to pronator teres) and **ulnar** (deep to pronator teres) arteries → **biceps tendon** → **radial nerve** (deep to brachioradialis) and **posterior interosseous nerve** (pierces supinator).

### Brachial artery for blood pressure
1. **Superficial** in front of the elbow; pulse felt **just medial to the biceps tendon**.
2. The fossa gives a **flat area** for the stethoscope.

### Why keep the elbow extended during venipuncture of the median cubital vein?
The vein lies **superficial to the bicipital aponeurosis**, which separates it from the **median nerve and brachial artery**. With the elbow **extended**, the aponeurosis becomes **tense** → a **firm barrier** protecting the artery and nerve.

### Rules
- **Rule 1:** **nerves pass between two heads of muscles**, not between bones (radial—supinator; median—pronator teres; ulnar—flexor carpi ulnaris).
- **Rule 2:** **arteries may pass between bones** (posterior interosseous artery between radius and ulna).
- Pronator teres: **median nerve between** its heads, **radial artery superficial**, **ulnar artery deep**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cubital fossa: venipuncture, blood pressure, supracondylar fracture injuries.
- Extend the elbow for venipuncture: tense bicipital aponeurosis protects artery and nerve.
- Brachial pulse is felt medial to the biceps tendon.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the anatomy of the cubital fossa clinically important?</summary>

**Answer:** It is a common site for venipuncture, the site for recording blood pressure (brachial artery), and its contents are liable to injury in supracondylar fracture of the humerus.

**Why:** Three reasons from the lab.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why must the elbow be kept extended during venipuncture of the median cubital vein?</summary>

**Answer:** The median cubital vein lies superficial to the bicipital aponeurosis, which separates it from the median nerve and brachial artery. With the elbow extended the aponeurosis becomes tense and forms a firm barrier that protects the artery and nerve from the needle.

**Why:** A classic "why" question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the relations of the two heads of pronator teres.</summary>

**Answer:** The median nerve passes between the two heads; the radial artery passes superficial to both heads; the ulnar artery passes deep to both heads.

**Why:** Between / superficial / deep.
</details>

## 7. Elbow region — clinical questions

### Pronation vs supination
| | Pronation | Supination |
|---|---|---|
| Initiated by | **brachioradialis** (from full supination to midprone) | **brachioradialis** (from full pronation to midprone) |
| Completed by | **pronator teres, pronator quadratus** | **supinator** (elbow extended), **biceps** (elbow flexed) |
| Gravity | **helps** (unscrewing) | **antigravity** (screwing) → more powerful muscles |
| Nerve | **median** | **radial** |

- ⭐ **Median nerve lesion at or above the elbow → loss of pronation**; **radial nerve lesion → no loss of supination** (biceps, musculocutaneous nerve, still supinates).
- **Wrist flexors** arise from the **medial epicondyle**; **wrist extensors** from the **lateral epicondyle**.

### Tennis & golfer's elbow (epicondylitis)
- Problems where a **tendon attaches to bone**, from **repetitive stress** → interstitial tearing → inflammation, soreness, swelling, pain.
- ⭐ **Lateral epicondylitis = tennis elbow** — sports with **twisting and extension of the wrist against resistance**.
- ⭐ **Medial epicondylitis = golfer's elbow** — repetitive overuse, aggravated by wrist movement.

### Popeye elbow
**Olecranon bursitis** — an inflamed, fluid-filled bursa at the **tip of the elbow** from **trauma, infection or inflammation** → pain, swelling, tenderness and a **lump**; hard to rest the elbow on a surface. (The bursa normally protects the joint and reduces friction.)

### The bony triangle
- **Supracondylar fracture:** lies **above** the olecranon and epicondyles → **triangle preserved**.
- **Elbow dislocation:** olecranon comes **in line** with the epicondyles → **triangle disturbed**.

### Dislocation & olecranon fracture
- **Elbow dislocation:** usually **posterior**, from an **indirect force (fall on the hand)**; complications: **ulnar nerve** and **brachial artery** injury.
- **Olecranon fracture:** **direct violence** or **avulsion by triceps**; fragments **widely separated** → need **surgical repair**.

### Pulled elbow (1–3 years)
- **Tubular annular ligament** + **small, underdeveloped radial head** → sudden lifting with the arm **pronated** → the head slips partly out of the annular ligament → **subluxation**.
- **Reduction:** **direct pressure posteriorly on the radial head** while **supinating and extending** the forearm.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Median lesion → loss of pronation; radial lesion → supination preserved (biceps).
- Tennis = lateral epicondylitis (wrist extensors); golfer's = medial (wrist flexors).
- Popeye elbow = olecranon bursitis.
- Supracondylar fracture keeps the triangle; dislocation (usually posterior) disturbs it.
- Olecranon fracture: triceps separates fragments → surgery. Pulled elbow: pressure on radial head + supination + extension.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are tennis elbow and golfer's elbow?</summary>

**Answer:** Lateral and medial epicondylitis — problems where the forearm tendons attach to the epicondyles, caused by repetitive stress with interstitial tearing, inflammation, soreness, swelling and pain. Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis) follows sports requiring twisting and extension of the wrist against resistance; golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis) follows repetitive overuse aggravated by wrist movement.

**Why:** Definition + cause + which side.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does a median nerve lesion abolish pronation while a radial nerve lesion does not abolish supination?</summary>

**Answer:** Pronation is completed by pronator teres and pronator quadratus, both supplied by the median nerve, so a lesion at or above the elbow abolishes it. Supination can still be done by biceps, supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve, when the radial nerve (supinator) is injured.

**Why:** Two muscles for supination — one survives.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are children aged 1–3 years prone to pulled elbow, and how is it reduced?</summary>

**Answer:** Their annular ligament is tubular and the radial head is small and not fully developed, so when the child is suddenly lifted with the forearm pronated, the radial head slips partially out of the annular ligament. It is reduced by applying direct pressure posteriorly on the radial head while supinating and extending the forearm.

**Why:** Anatomy + mechanism + reduction.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- Conjoint tendon = **pectoralis minor + short head of biceps**, divided **2 cm below** the coracoid.
- Deltoid injection in the **lower half** (axillary nerve is above).
- Superficial veins do **not** accompany arteries; deep veins are **venae comitantes**.
- Basilic vein joins the axillary vein **in line**; cephalic joins at a **right angle with a valve**.
- Radial nerve in the groove → **wrist drop but elbow extension preserved**.
- Ulnar claw spares the **index and middle** fingers (lumbricals 1–2 from the median nerve).
- **Tennis = lateral**, **golfer's = medial**.
- Supracondylar fracture **preserves** the bony triangle; dislocation **disturbs** it.

9. **Median at elbow:** no pronation, ape + benediction hand; **ulnar at elbow:** partial claw, hypothenar flattening.
10. **Cubital fossa:** venipuncture (elbow extended), BP, supracondylar injuries.
11. **Tennis (lateral) / golfer's (medial)**; **Popeye = olecranon bursitis**.
12. **Pulled elbow:** tubular annular ligament + small radial head; reduce with pressure + supination + extension.
