## 1. Body composition & fluid compartments

In the **average young adult male**:

| Component | % of body weight |
|---|---|
| **Protein and related substances** | **18%** |
| **Minerals** | **7%** |
| **Fat** | **15%** |
| ⭐ **Water (total body water)** | **60%** |

**Total body water = intracellular fluid (ICF) + extracellular fluid (ECF):**

```
Total body water        60% of body weight
├── ICF                 ≈ 40%
└── ECF                 ≈ 20%
     ├── Plasma         ≈ 5%
     └── Interstitial   ≈ 15%
```

### Factors affecting body water
- The **percentage of total body water is greater in newborns** and **lower in adult females, elderly persons, and adults with a lot of adipose tissue**.

### Plasma vs interstitial fluid
- They are separated only by **highly permeable capillary membranes**, so their **ionic compositions are similar** and they are often considered **one large homogeneous compartment**.
- ⭐ **The most important difference = higher protein concentration in plasma**, because **capillaries have a low permeability to plasma proteins**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Young adult male: protein 18%, minerals 7%, fat 15%, water 60%.
- Water 60% = ICF 40% + ECF 20% (plasma 5% + interstitial 15%).
- Water % is higher in newborns, lower in females, elderly and obese people.
- Plasma vs interstitial fluid: same ions, but plasma has more protein (capillaries are poorly permeable to proteins).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the body composition of an average young adult male and the fluid compartments.</summary>

**Answer:** Protein and related substances make up 18% of body weight, minerals 7%, fat 15% and water 60%. Total body water is divided into intracellular fluid (about 40% of body weight) and extracellular fluid (about 20%), which is subdivided into plasma (about 5%) and interstitial fluid (about 15%).

**Why:** Numbers: 18 / 7 / 15 / 60 and 40 / 20 / 5 / 15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which people have a lower percentage of total body water?</summary>

**Answer:** Adult females, elderly persons and adults with a large amount of adipose tissue; the percentage is greater in newborns.

**Why:** Fat contains little water.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the most important difference between plasma and interstitial fluid, and why does it exist?</summary>

**Answer:** Plasma contains a higher concentration of protein, because the capillary membranes have a low permeability to the plasma proteins; otherwise the two fluids have similar ionic compositions and are separated only by highly permeable capillary membranes.

**Why:** Basis of colloid osmotic pressure.
</details>

## 2. Ionic composition of ECF & ICF

- **ECF:** major **cation = Na⁺**, major **anions = Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻**.
- **ICF:** major **cations = K⁺ and Mg²⁺**, major **anions = proteins and organic phosphates** (**ATP, ADP, AMP**).

### Concentrations (mmol/L)
| Ion | **Plasma** | **Interstitial** | **Intracellular** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Na⁺** | **142** | 139 | **14** |
| **K⁺** | 4.2 | 4.0 | **140** |
| **Ca²⁺** | 1.3 | 1.2 | 0 |
| **Mg²⁺** | 0.8 | 0.7 | **20** |
| **Cl⁻** | **108** | 108 | **4** |
| **HCO₃⁻** | 24 | 28.3 | 10 |
| **Protein** | 1.2 | 0.2 | 4.0 |
| **HPO₄²⁻** | 2.0 | 2.0 | 11 |

- **ECF also carries:** O₂, glucose, fatty acids, amino acids (nutrients) and wastes — **CO₂, urea, uric acid, excess water and ions**.
- **ICF has high concentrations of proteins.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ECF: Na⁺ (142), Cl⁻ (108), HCO₃⁻ (24). ICF: K⁺ (140), Mg²⁺ (20), proteins, phosphates (ATP, ADP, AMP).
- Na⁺ is 10× higher outside; K⁺ is about 33× higher inside.
- Plasma protein 1.2 vs interstitial 0.2 mmol/L.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the ionic composition of ECF and ICF.</summary>

**Answer:** The ECF has Na⁺ as the major cation (142 mmol/L) with Cl⁻ (108) and HCO₃⁻ (24) as major anions, and low K⁺ (4.2). The ICF has K⁺ (140) and Mg²⁺ (20) as major cations, with Na⁺ only 14, and proteins and organic phosphates (ATP, ADP, AMP) as major anions, with very low Cl⁻ (4) and HCO₃⁻ (10).

**Why:** Two columns: Na-Cl outside, K-proteins inside.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What nutrients and wastes does the ECF carry?</summary>

**Answer:** Nutrients — oxygen, glucose, fatty acids and amino acids; wastes — carbon dioxide, urea, uric acid, excess water and ions.

**Why:** ECF is the "transport" compartment.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the concentrations of Na⁺ and K⁺ in plasma and in the cell.</summary>

**Answer:** Na⁺: 142 mmol/L in plasma vs 14 mmol/L intracellular; K⁺: 4.2 mmol/L in plasma vs 140 mmol/L intracellular.

**Why:** These gradients are maintained by the Na⁺-K⁺ pump.
</details>

## 3. Isotonic, hypertonic & hypotonic solutions

| Solution | Definition | Effect on the cell |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Isotonic** | **no osmotic force** develops across the cell membrane; **same osmolarity as the cell** | cells **neither shrink nor swell**; examples: **0.9% NaCl** and **5% glucose** |
| ⭐ **Hypertonic** | **higher osmotic concentration** than the cell | **water flows out** of the cell → ICF concentrated, **cell shrinks (crenation)** |
| ⭐ **Hypotonic** | **lower osmotic concentration** than the cell | **water flows into** the cell by osmosis until ICF equals the solution, **or the cell bursts** from excessive swelling |

Lecture figure: **A** normal red blood cell in isotonic solution; **B** swollen red cell in hypotonic solution; **C** shrunken (crenated) red cell in hypertonic solution.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Isotonic: 0.9% NaCl, 5% glucose; no change in cell volume.
- Hypertonic: water leaves → shrinks (crenation). Hypotonic: water enters → swells/bursts.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define isotonic, hypertonic and hypotonic solutions.</summary>

**Answer:** An isotonic solution has the same osmolarity as the cell, so no osmotic force develops and the cell does not shrink or swell (e.g. 0.9% NaCl, 5% glucose). A hypertonic solution has a higher osmotic concentration than the cell, so water flows out and the cell shrinks. A hypotonic solution has a lower osmotic concentration, so water flows into the cell until equilibrium or until the cell bursts.

**Why:** Three definitions with the effect on the cell.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What happens to a red blood cell placed in 5% NaCl? In distilled water?</summary>

**Answer:** In 5% NaCl (hypertonic) water leaves the cell and it shrinks (crenation). In distilled water (hypotonic) water enters the cell, which swells and may burst (haemolysis).

**Why:** Applies the definitions to two examples.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give two examples of isotonic solutions.</summary>

**Answer:** 0.9% sodium chloride and 5% glucose solution.

**Why:** The two standard IV fluids in the lecture.
</details>

## 4. Transport across the cell membrane

| Type | Direction | Energy | Carrier | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Simple diffusion** | **downhill** (high → low) along an electrochemical gradient | **none** (passive) | **no** | O₂, CO₂ |
| ⭐ **Facilitated diffusion** | **downhill** | **none** (passive) | **yes**, and **faster** than simple diffusion | **glucose** in muscle and adipose cells (needs **insulin**-dependent carriers) |
| ⭐ **Primary active transport** | **uphill** (against the gradient) | **direct ATP** | **yes** | **Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase** |
| ⭐ **Secondary active transport** | one solute **downhill** (usually **Na⁺**) drives another **uphill** | **indirect** (Na⁺ gradient) | **yes** | **Na⁺-glucose** cotransport; **Na⁺-Ca²⁺** and **Na⁺-H⁺** exchange |

### Simple diffusion — rate depends on
1. **Electrical potential and chemical concentration differences** across the membrane.
2. **Surface area** of the membrane.
3. **Permeability** of the membrane for the solute — **inversely related to solute size and membrane thickness**.

### Facilitated diffusion in diabetes
- In **diabetes mellitus**, glucose uptake and use by muscle and adipose cells are **impaired** because the carriers for facilitated diffusion of glucose **require insulin**.

### Primary active transport — Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase
- Moves **Na⁺ out** (ICF → ECF) and **K⁺ in** (ECF → ICF); **both against their gradients**; **ATP** provides the energy.
- Maintains **low intracellular Na⁺ and high intracellular K⁺**.

### Secondary active transport
- **Coupled** transport of two or more solutes.
- **Cotransport (symport):** solutes move in the **same direction** — e.g. **Na⁺-glucose cotransport** in the small intestine.
- **Counter-transport (antiport / exchange):** solutes move in **opposite directions** — e.g. **Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchange**, **Na⁺-H⁺ exchange**.
- ⭐ **Inhibition of Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase** → less Na⁺ pumped out → **smaller Na⁺ gradient** → eventually **inhibits secondary active transport**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Passive (downhill, no ATP): simple diffusion (no carrier), facilitated diffusion (carrier, faster; glucose needs insulin).
- Active (uphill): primary (direct ATP; Na⁺-K⁺ pump) and secondary (indirect, Na⁺ gradient).
- Symport = same direction (Na⁺-glucose); antiport = opposite directions (Na⁺-Ca²⁺, Na⁺-H⁺).
- Blocking the Na⁺-K⁺ pump eventually blocks secondary active transport.
- Diffusion rate ↑ with concentration/electrical difference, surface area, permeability.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare simple and facilitated diffusion.</summary>

**Answer:** Both occur down an electrochemical gradient ("downhill") and need no metabolic energy, so both are passive. Simple diffusion is not carrier-mediated; facilitated diffusion is carrier-mediated and more rapid (e.g. glucose into muscle and adipose cells, which needs insulin-dependent carriers).

**Why:** Carrier-mediated is the key difference.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What factors determine the rate of simple diffusion?</summary>

**Answer:** The electrical potential and chemical concentration differences across the membrane, the surface area of the membrane, and the permeability of the membrane for the solute, which is inversely related to the size of the solute and the thickness of the membrane.

**Why:** Three factors.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain primary and secondary active transport with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Primary active transport moves a solute against its electrochemical gradient using ATP directly; e.g. the Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase pumps Na⁺ out and K⁺ in, keeping intracellular Na⁺ low and K⁺ high. Secondary active transport couples two solutes: one (usually Na⁺) moves downhill and provides the energy for the uphill movement of the other, with energy supplied indirectly by the Na⁺ gradient; examples are Na⁺-glucose cotransport (symport) in the small intestine and Na⁺-Ca²⁺ or Na⁺-H⁺ exchange (antiport).

**Why:** Direct vs indirect energy.
</details>

## 5. Osmosis

- **Osmosis** = flow of **water across a semipermeable membrane from a solution of low solute concentration to one of high solute concentration**.
- Body **cell membranes are highly permeable to water**: wherever the solute concentration is higher on one side, **water rapidly diffuses toward the region of higher solute concentration**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Osmosis = movement of water through a semipermeable membrane toward the higher solute concentration.
- Cell membranes are highly permeable to water.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define osmosis.</summary>

**Answer:** The flow of water across a semipermeable membrane from a solution with a low solute concentration to a solution with a high solute concentration.

**Why:** Always state the direction.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does water move rapidly across body cell membranes?</summary>

**Answer:** Because cell membranes are highly permeable to water, so whenever there is a higher solute concentration on one side, water quickly diffuses toward that side.

**Why:** High water permeability.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Toward which side does water move if one side of a semipermeable membrane has more solute?</summary>

**Answer:** Toward the side with the higher solute concentration.

**Why:** The same statement as the definition.
</details>

## 6. Fluid filtration across capillaries & colloid osmotic pressure

### Starling forces (lecture version)
- **Capillary hydrostatic pressure** tends to force **fluid and dissolved substances through the capillary pores into the interstitial spaces** (outward).
- **Colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure** caused by the **plasma proteins** tends to move fluid by osmosis **from the interstitial space into the blood** (inward).
- ⭐ The osmotic pressure of the plasma proteins **normally prevents significant loss of fluid volume from the blood into the interstitial spaces**.

### Plasma colloid osmotic pressure
- **Proteins in the plasma** cause colloid osmotic pressure.
- Only molecules or ions that **fail to pass through the pores** of a semipermeable membrane exert osmotic pressure.
- **Proteins are the only dissolved constituents of plasma and interstitial fluid that do not readily pass through the capillary pores**, so they alone are responsible for the osmotic pressures on the two sides of the capillary membrane.
- It is called **colloid osmotic pressure (oncotic pressure)** to distinguish it from the osmotic pressure at the cell membrane.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out of the capillary; colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure pulls it back in.
- Oncotic pressure is due to plasma proteins that cannot cross the capillary pores; it prevents loss of plasma volume.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the forces that move fluid across the capillary membrane.</summary>

**Answer:** The hydrostatic pressure in the capillary forces fluid and dissolved substances out through the pores into the interstitial space. The colloid osmotic pressure produced by the plasma proteins draws fluid by osmosis from the interstitial space back into the blood, and it normally prevents significant loss of fluid volume from the blood.

**Why:** Two opposing forces.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why do plasma proteins, but not small ions, generate a significant osmotic force across the capillary wall?</summary>

**Answer:** Only molecules or ions that fail to pass through the pores of a semipermeable membrane exert an osmotic pressure; proteins are the only dissolved constituents of plasma and interstitial fluid that do not readily pass through the capillary pores, while small ions pass freely and so exert no net osmotic force.

**Why:** Impermeable solutes create the pressure.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is colloid osmotic pressure and why is it also called oncotic pressure?</summary>

**Answer:** It is the osmotic pressure caused by the plasma proteins across the capillary membrane; it is called colloid osmotic or oncotic pressure to distinguish it from the osmotic pressure occurring across the cell membrane.

**Why:** Naming question.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Water = 60%**, ICF **40%**, ECF **20%** (plasma **5%**, interstitial **15%**).
- **Isotonic ≠ same ions** — it means **same osmolarity** (0.9% NaCl or 5% glucose).
- **Hypertonic → shrink; hypotonic → swell/burst.**
- **Facilitated diffusion** is passive (no ATP) but **carrier-mediated** (needs insulin for glucose in muscle/fat).
- **Secondary active transport** uses **no ATP directly** — it depends on the **Na⁺ gradient**.
- **Symport = same direction; antiport = opposite directions.**
- **Plasma vs interstitial:** the difference is **protein**, not ions.
- **Oncotic pressure moves fluid INTO the capillary**; hydrostatic pressure moves it OUT.
