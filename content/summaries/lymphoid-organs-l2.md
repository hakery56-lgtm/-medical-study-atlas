## 1. The thymus: location, origin and structure

- ⭐ **Bilateral organ in the mediastinum**, peak development in **youth**; a **central (primary) lymphoid organ** because **T lymphocytes form there.**
- **Dual origin:** lymphoblasts from the **bone marrow** invade **endoderm-derived epithelium** (all other lymphoid organs are purely **mesodermal**).
- **Capsule → incomplete lobules** (cortex and medulla continuous between lobules). **Cortex** = peripheral, **dark** (rich in small lymphocytes); **medulla** = central, **light.**
- **Cortex:** **thymocytes (T lymphoblasts)** and **macrophages** in a stroma of **epithelial reticular cells** (stellate, long processes, euchromatic nuclei, **desmosomes** → **cytoreticulum**).

| Cortical TEC | Features |
|---|---|
| **Squamous TECs (layer)** | Desmosomes and occluding junctions; line capsule and septa, surround vessels → **isolated cortical compartment, blood-thymus barrier** |
| **Stellate TECs** | **Cytoreticulum**; **APCs (MHC I and II)**; secrete **cytokines** for T-cell development |
| **Other squamous TECs** | MHC II; sheet forming the **corticomedullary barrier** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Thymus = T-cell production; dual origin (marrow + endoderm).
- Cortex dark with thymocytes; medulla light.
- Stellate TECs are the cortical APCs.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the thymus a primary lymphoid organ and what is its origin?</summary>

**Answer:** T lymphocytes form there; it has a dual origin (marrow lymphoblasts invading endoderm-derived epithelium) unlike the other lymphoid organs, which are purely mesodermal.

**Why:** Slide "Thymus".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the thymic cortex and medulla.</summary>

**Answer:** The cortex is peripheral, dark and rich in small lymphocytes (thymocytes and macrophages in an epithelial reticular stroma); the medulla is central and light.

**Why:** Slides 3-4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the types of cortical thymic epithelial cells.</summary>

**Answer:** Squamous TECs (blood-thymus barrier), stellate TECs (cytoreticulum, APCs, cytokines) and other squamous TECs (corticomedullary barrier).

</details>

## 2. Thymic medulla and blood-thymus barrier

- ⭐ **Medulla:** cytoreticulum of epithelial reticular cells; **less dense, fewer, more differentiated T cells**, dendritic cells, macrophages; **Hassall's corpuscles** (characteristic).
- **Medullary TECs form:** **a second corticomedullary boundary layer**; **a cytoreticulum** expressing proteins of **other organs**; **Hassall corpuscles** (aggregates up to **100 μm**, **unique to the medulla**, cytokines for **regulatory T cells and peripheral tolerance**).
- **Blood-thymus barrier (cortex):** **arterioles and capillaries sheathed by epithelial reticular cells with tight junctions**, **continuous endothelium with thick basal lamina** → **blocks circulating antigens.** **No barrier in the medulla**; **mature T cells exit through medullary venules.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hassall's corpuscles = medulla only.
- Barrier in the cortex; exit through the medulla.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe Hassall's corpuscles.</summary>

**Answer:** Large, sometimes concentric aggregates of thymic epithelial cells up to 100 μm in the medulla only; they secrete cytokines controlling dendritic cells and promoting regulatory T cells for peripheral tolerance.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the blood-thymus barrier.</summary>

**Answer:** Cortical arterioles and capillaries are sheathed by flattened epithelial reticular cells with tight junctions and have continuous endothelium with a thick basal lamina, preventing most circulating antigens from entering the cortex; there is no barrier in the medulla.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Where do mature T cells leave the thymus?</summary>

**Answer:** Through venules in the medulla, where there is no barrier.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

## 3. T-cell maturation and the adult thymus

- ⭐ Maturing thymocytes express **CD4 and CD8** and undergo **positive and negative selection.**
- **Positive selection (cortex):** thymocytes presented with antigens on **MHC I and II**; those whose **TCR cannot bind MHC** are nonfunctional (**up to 80%**) → **apoptosis.**
- **Negative selection (medulla):** thymocytes that **bind MHC + self-peptides strongly** are deleted to prevent **autoimmunity.**
- **Only 2%** survive and leave as **immunocompetent T cells**; the rest die by **apoptosis** and are cleared by **macrophages.**
- **Adult thymus:** maximal relative to body weight **just after birth**; **involution after puberty**, yet lymphocytes are produced until old age; the adult has remnants of lymphoid tissue in **adipose tissue.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Positive selection removes non-binders (80%); negative selection removes self-reactive cells.
- 2% survive.
- Involution after puberty; fat replaces tissue.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain positive selection.</summary>

**Answer:** Cortical thymocytes whose TCRs cannot bind MHC are nonfunctional (up to 80%) and undergo apoptosis.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain negative selection.</summary>

**Answer:** Medullary thymocytes that bind MHC with self-peptides strongly are deleted because they could cause autoimmune damage; only 2% of thymocytes survive both tests.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the adult thymus.</summary>

**Answer:** It involutes after puberty and in adults only remnants of lymphoid tissue remain, surrounded by abundant adipose tissue, though lymphocyte production continues until old age.

**Why:** Slide "Adult thymus".
</details>

## 4. The spleen: structure and pulp

- ⭐ **Largest single accumulation of lymphoid tissue**; **the only lymphoid organ filtering blood**; **main site of destruction of aged erythrocytes**; makes **antibodies and activated lymphocytes.**
- **Capsule** of dense connective tissue with **trabeculae** subdividing the **splenic pulp.**
- **Pulp** = reticular tissue (reticular cells, lymphocytes, blood cells, macrophages, APCs):

| Pulp | Components |
|---|---|
| **White pulp** | **Lymphoid nodules** and **periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths (PALS)** |
| **Red pulp** | **Blood-filled sinusoids** and **splenic cords (of Billroth)** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Spleen filters blood and destroys old RBCs.
- White pulp = nodules + PALS; red pulp = cords + sinusoids.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the functions of the spleen.</summary>

**Answer:** Filtration of blood (the only lymphoid organ to do so), destruction of aged erythrocytes, and production of antibodies and activated lymphocytes.

**Why:** Slide "Spleen".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare white and red pulp.</summary>

**Answer:** White pulp has lymphoid nodules and periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths; red pulp has blood-filled sinusoids and splenic cords of Billroth.

**Why:** Slide "Splenic Pulp".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the stroma of the spleen?</summary>

**Answer:** A dense connective tissue capsule with trabeculae and reticular tissue (reticular cells, lymphocytes, blood cells, macrophages and APCs).

**Why:** Slides 12-13.
</details>

## 5. Splenic circulation and the white pulp

- ⭐ **Splenic artery → trabecular arteries → central arterioles** (sheathed by **PALS = T cells**) → branches to white pulp → **penicillar arterioles** → **closed circulation** (straight into **sinuses**) **or open circulation** (into **splenic cords**).
- **PALS** = mostly **T cells**; **nodules** = proliferating **B cells**; in nodules the **central arteriole is eccentric** (displaced by the germinal center).
- **Marginal zone:** sinuses at the **white/red pulp boundary** = entry of circulating **T and B cells and blood-borne antigens.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PALS = T cells; nodules = B cells.
- Central arteriole becomes penicillar; closed or open circulation.
- Marginal zone = entry site.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Trace the splenic blood supply.</summary>

**Answer:** Splenic artery, trabecular arteries, central arterioles surrounded by PALS, penicillar arterioles, then closed circulation into sinuses or open circulation into splenic cords.

**Why:** Slides 14-15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the white pulp.</summary>

**Answer:** Lymphoid tissue around the central arterioles: the PALS of T lymphocytes and nodules of proliferating B cells, with the central arteriole displaced eccentrically by germinal centers.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the marginal zone?</summary>

**Answer:** Small sinuses at the border of white and red pulp through which circulating T and B lymphocytes and blood-borne antigens enter the parenchyma.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

## 6. The red pulp and removal of old red cells

- ⭐ **Red pulp** = **splenic cords + sinusoids**; cords hold **T and B cells, macrophages, plasma cells, erythrocytes, platelets, granulocytes.**
- **Sinusoids:** wide, irregular, lined by elongated endothelial **stave cells.**
- **Open circulation:** cells dumped into the cords squeeze **between stave cells** back into the vessels and leave through the **splenic vein.** **Stiff or swollen RBCs at their normal 120-day life span** are blocked and **removed by macrophages.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Stave cells line the sinusoids.
- Aged 120-day RBCs get trapped and phagocytosed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the red pulp.</summary>

**Answer:** Splenic cords (T and B cells, macrophages, plasma cells, erythrocytes, platelets, granulocytes) separated by wide sinusoids lined by stave cells.

**Why:** Slide "The red pulp".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does the spleen remove aged red cells?</summary>

**Answer:** Aged, stiff or swollen RBCs (120-day life span) cannot squeeze between the stave cells and are phagocytosed by macrophages.

**Why:** Slide 17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are stave cells?</summary>

**Answer:** Elongated endothelial cells lining the splenic sinusoids.

**Why:** Slide 17.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Hassall's corpuscles are in the medulla, not the cortex.**
- **Blood-thymus barrier is cortical; the medulla has none.**
- **Positive selection ≈ 80% die; only 2% survive overall.**
- **Spleen: PALS = T cells; nodules = B cells.**
- **Thymus is purely T-cell maturation; spleen is the only lymphoid organ filtering blood.**
