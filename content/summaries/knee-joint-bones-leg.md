## 1. Distal femur and patella

### Femoral condyles
| Feature | **Medial condyle** | **Lateral condyle** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | **Narrower, more curved** | **Broader, flatter, less curved** |
| Main role | **Rotatory movements** of the knee | **Body weight transmission** |
| Projection | **More downwards** | **More forwards** |
| Landmark | **Medial epicondyle**; **adductor tubercle** at the lower end of the supracondylar line | **Lateral epicondyle**; **popliteal sulcus** below it |

### Patella
- ⭐ **Largest sesamoid bone** in the body; **triangular**.
- **Base:** quadriceps tendon (rectus femoris + vastus intermedius). **Apex:** patellar ligament. **Borders:** lateral and medial vastal retinacula.
- **Anterior surface:** rough, subcutaneous. **Posterior surface:** lower ⅓ rough non-articular; upper ⅔ smooth, divided by a ridge into a **large lateral facet** (lateral condyle) and a **small medial facet** (medial condyle).
- **Functions:** reduces friction; **pulley** for the quadriceps (improves leverage in extension); stabilizes the knee in the trochlear groove.
- **Femoro-patellar articulation:** patella with the patellar surface (trochlear groove) of the femur; **synovial, plane**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Medial condyle = rotation, more curved, adductor tubercle; lateral = weight, flatter, popliteal sulcus.
- Patella = largest sesamoid; base quadriceps tendon, apex patellar ligament; large lateral + small medial facet.
- Patella acts as a pulley and stabilizes the knee; femoro-patellar joint is synovial plane.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the medial and lateral femoral condyles.</summary>

**Answer:** The medial condyle is narrower and more curved, projects more downwards, is concerned with rotatory movements and carries the medial epicondyle and adductor tubercle. The lateral condyle is broader, flatter, projects more forwards, transmits body weight and carries the lateral epicondyle with the popliteal sulcus below it.

**Why:** Slide "Femoral condyles".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the patella and its attachments.</summary>

**Answer:** The largest sesamoid bone, triangular; the base takes the quadriceps tendon, the apex the patellar ligament, the borders the vastal retinacula. Its posterior upper two thirds is divided by a ridge into a large lateral and a small medial articular facet.

**Why:** Slide "Patella".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the functions of the patella.</summary>

**Answer:** It reduces friction between knee structures, improves leverage by acting as a pulley for the quadriceps tendon (stronger, more efficient extension), and stabilizes and aligns the knee by fitting into the trochlear groove, preventing sideways shifting.

**Why:** Slide "Patella – function".
</details>

## 2. Tibia and fibula

### Tibia
- **Weight-bearing** bone; takes part in the **knee and ankle** joints; **subcutaneous** surface → common site of **compound fracture**.
- **Subcutaneous parts:** condyles, **tibial tuberosity**, shin and medial surface, **medial malleolus**.
- **Proximal end:** two flat plateaus (**AP diameter medial > lateral**); median part with two non-articular areas and the **intercondylar eminence** (medial and lateral tubercles; main attachment of ligaments and menisci). **Tuberosity:** upper smooth part (infrapatellar bursa), lower rough part (patellar ligament).
- **Shaft:** **3 borders** (anterior = shin, ends at medial malleolus; interosseous = ends at fibular notch, interosseous membrane; medial) and **3 surfaces** (lateral, medial — subcutaneous except the upper part where **S.G.S** insert, posterior — **soleal line, vertical line, nutrient foramen** at the upper end of the vertical line).
- **Lower end — 5 surfaces:** lateral (**fibular notch**), medial (**medial malleolus**, groove for tibialis posterior + flexor digitorum longus), anterior, posterior (groove for flexor hallucis longus), inferior (concave, with the malleolus articulates with the **talus**).

### Fibula
- Muscle attachments; **NOT weight bearing**; takes part in the **ankle joint only**; **distal fracture → ankle dislocation**.
- **Subcutaneous parts:** head, lateral malleolus.
- **Head:** styloid process + articular facet (lateral tibial condyle). **Neck:** related laterally to the **common peroneal nerve**.
- **Shaft:** 3 borders (anterior, interosseous, posterior), 3 surfaces (anterior/extensor narrow; lateral/peroneal wide; posterior/flexor divided by the **medial crest**).
- **Lateral malleolus:** projects **lower** than the medial; groove for **peroneus longus**; medial surface has the talar facet and the **malleolar fossa** (posterior tibiofibular + posterior talofibular ligaments).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Tibia = weight bearing, subcutaneous (open fractures), intercondylar eminence anchors ligaments/menisci.
- S.G.S insert on the upper medial tibial surface; nutrient foramen at the upper end of the vertical line.
- Fibula = not weight bearing, ankle only; neck related to the common peroneal nerve; lateral malleolus lower than medial.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the tibia a common site for compound fractures, and what are its subcutaneous parts?</summary>

**Answer:** It has a subcutaneous surface with little soft-tissue cover. Subcutaneous parts: the medial and lateral condyles, tibial tuberosity, shin and medial surface, and the medial malleolus.

**Why:** Slide "Tibia".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the proximal end of the tibia.</summary>

**Answer:** Medial and lateral condyles with flat articular plateaus (medial AP diameter larger), a median part with anterior and posterior non-articular areas and the intercondylar eminence (medial and lateral tubercles) for ligaments and menisci, and the tibial tuberosity (upper smooth part related to the infrapatellar bursa, lower rough part for the patellar ligament).

**Why:** Slide "Tibia – proximal end".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the tibia and the fibula.</summary>

**Answer:** The tibia bears weight and forms part of both the knee and ankle joints; the fibula only provides muscle attachment and joins only the ankle, with its lateral malleolus lower than the medial. Fibular head: styloid process and tibial facet; neck: common peroneal nerve; distal fibular fracture is usually associated with ankle dislocation.

**Why:** Slides "Tibia" and "Fibula".
</details>

## 3. Knee joint — type, articulations, capsule, synovial membrane

- ⭐ **Type:** atypical **synovial, compound, complex**; **variety:** **modified hinge (bicondylar), biaxial**; **primary function: weight bearing**.
- **Articular surfaces:** two femoral condyles, upper surfaces of the tibial condyles, back of the patella. **The fibula takes no part.**
- **Articulations:** **femoro-tibial** and **femoro-patellar** (plane).
- **Surfaces:** femoral condyles highly convex (more concave posteriorly); medial = larger, oval, long AP diameter; lateral = smaller, circular, shorter AP diameter. Tibial plateaus flat, with the same medial/lateral difference.

### Fibrous capsule
- ⭐ **Incomplete:** weak and thin, present on the sides and posteriorly; **deficient anteriorly**, replaced by the **quadriceps tendon, patella and patellar ligament**; a hole posteriorly.
- **Posteriorly:** attached to the margins of the femoral and tibial condyles; **perforated by popliteus tendon** (so intracapsular). **Laterally:** just above the groove for popliteus; **the two epicondyles are extracapsular**.
- **Reinforced:** anteriorly by medial and lateral **patellar retinacula**; laterally by the **iliotibial tract**; posteriorly by the **popliteal ligament**.

### Synovial membrane
- Lines the capsule and covers all intra-articular structures (e.g. cruciates) **except the bony articular surfaces**; forms the **suprapatellar bursa** under the quadriceps tendon.
- **Popliteus tendon:** inside the capsule but outside the synovial membrane → **intracapsular but extrasynovial**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Knee = atypical synovial, compound, complex; modified hinge, biaxial; weight bearing; no fibula.
- Capsule is incomplete: deficient anteriorly (quadriceps tendon, patella, patellar ligament), perforated posteriorly by popliteus.
- Popliteus tendon = intracapsular but extrasynovial; epicondyles extracapsular.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify the knee joint and list its articulations.</summary>

**Answer:** Atypical synovial, compound, complex joint of the modified hinge (bicondylar), biaxial variety whose primary function is weight bearing. Articulations: femoro-tibial (femoral condyles with tibial plateaus) and femoro-patellar (plane). The fibula is not involved.

**Why:** Slide "Knee joint".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the unique features of the knee capsule.</summary>

**Answer:** It is incomplete, weak and thin: present at the sides and behind, absent in front where the quadriceps tendon, patella and patellar ligament replace it, and perforated behind by the popliteus tendon. It is reinforced by the patellar retinacula (front), iliotibial tract (lateral) and popliteal ligament (back).

**Why:** Slide "Fibrous capsule".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the relation of the popliteus tendon to the capsule and synovial membrane?</summary>

**Answer:** It pierces the posterior capsule, so it lies inside the fibrous capsule but outside the synovial membrane: intracapsular but extrasynovial.

**Why:** Slides "Synovial membrane" and "Fibrous capsule".
</details>

## 4. Ligaments of the knee

- **Two groups:** **extracapsular (5)** and **intracapsular (6 listed)**. Note: one slide heading says "Intracapsular (7)" while the list contains six items.

### Extracapsular (5)
1. **Patellar ligament** — continuation of the quadriceps tendon, apex of patella → tibial tuberosity; replaces the anterior capsule.
2. **Fibular (lateral) collateral ligament (LCL)** — lateral epicondyle → head of fibula; **cord-like**; prevents **adduction**; separated from the lateral meniscus by the popliteus tendon.
3. **Tibial (medial) collateral ligament (MCL)** — medial epicondyle → upper medial tibia (deep to S.G.S); **flat band**; prevents **abduction**; **firmly attached to the medial meniscus** (often torn together in football players).
4. **Oblique popliteal ligament** — from the semimembranosus tendon, upwards and laterally; prevents **hyperextension**.
5. **Arcuate popliteal ligament** — from the fibular head, arches over the posterolateral capsule; prevents **hyperextension**.

### MCL vs LCL
| | **MCL** | **LCL** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Strong, **flat, wide, triangular** | Strong, **rounded cord** |
| Attachments | Medial epicondyle → medial tibial condyle and upper medial surface (behind S.G.S) | Lateral epicondyle → head of fibula; between the splits of **biceps tendon** |
| Capsule/meniscus | **Firmly attached** | **Not adherent** |
| Limits | **Abduction** | **Adduction** |

Both become tense in extension and give side-to-side stability.

### Intracapsular (6)
**ACL, PCL, transverse ligament** (joins the anterior horns of the menisci; no bony attachment), **coronary ligaments** (2; lax; the only knee ligaments not tight in extension), **meniscofemoral ligaments** (anterior and posterior; posterior horn of lateral meniscus → medial femoral condyle, one in front of and one behind the PCL).

### Cruciate ligaments
| | **ACL** | **PCL** |
|---|---|---|
| Tibial attachment | **Anterior** intercondylar area (between anterior horns of menisci) | **Posterior** intercondylar area (behind the menisci's posterior horns) |
| Femoral attachment | **Medial surface of lateral condyle** | **Lateral surface of medial condyle** |
| Direction | Upwards, **backwards, laterally** | Upwards, **forwards, medially** |
| Stretched in | **Extension** | **Flexion** |
| Prevents | **Hyperextension; anterior dislocation of the tibia** | **Posterior dislocation of the tibia** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five extracapsular: patellar, LCL (prevents adduction), MCL (prevents abduction, fixed to medial meniscus), oblique and arcuate popliteal (prevent hyperextension).
- Intracapsular: ACL, PCL, transverse, coronary (lax), 2 meniscofemoral.
- ACL tight in extension (stops anterior tibial displacement); PCL tight in flexion (stops posterior tibial displacement).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the medial and lateral collateral ligaments.</summary>

**Answer:** MCL: flat, wide triangular band from the medial epicondyle to the medial tibial condyle behind S.G.S, firmly attached to capsule and medial meniscus, limits abduction. LCL: rounded cord from the lateral epicondyle to the fibular head between the splits of the biceps tendon, not attached to capsule or meniscus, limits adduction. Both are tense in extension.

**Why:** Slides "Medial (tibial) collateral ligament" and "Lateral (fibular) collateral ligament".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments.</summary>

**Answer:** ACL: anterior intercondylar area to the medial surface of the lateral femoral condyle, running up, back and laterally, stretched in extension, prevents hyperextension and anterior tibial dislocation. PCL: posterior intercondylar area to the lateral surface of the medial femoral condyle, running up, forwards and medially, stretched in flexion, prevents posterior tibial dislocation.

**Why:** Slides "Cruciate ligaments".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which knee ligaments prevent hyperextension and what reinforces the posterior capsule?</summary>

**Answer:** The oblique popliteal ligament (extension of semimembranosus), the arcuate popliteal ligament (from the fibular head) and the ACL prevent hyperextension; the oblique and arcuate popliteal ligaments reinforce the posterior and posterolateral capsule.

**Why:** Slides "Extracapsular" and "Anterior cruciate ligament".
</details>

## 5. Menisci

- **Curved fibrocartilage plates** on the upper tibial condyles; each has **2 horns** (anterior, posterior) and a body; **thick outer border, thin inner border**; **upper surface convex, lower flat**; attached by the horns to the intercondylar area.
- **Functions:** deepen the tibial surfaces; **shock absorbers**; adapt the tibial surface to the changing femoral curvature; **spread synovial fluid**; essential for **rotatory movements** (move with the femur on the tibia); **proprioception** (nerve supply).

| | **Medial meniscus** | **Lateral meniscus** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | **C-shaped, larger** | **Circular, smaller** |
| Anterior horn | Most anterior intercondylar area, **in front of ACL** | **Just in front of lateral intercondylar tubercle** |
| Posterior horn | Behind lateral meniscus horn, **in front of PCL** | **Behind lateral intercondylar tubercle** |
| Mobility | **Less mobile** (fixed to MCL and capsule) → **more injured** | **More mobile** (popliteus tendon separates it from capsule and LCL) → less injured |

### Other intracapsular structures
Two menisci, two cruciates, transverse ligament, popliteus origin tendon (with its synovial sheath), **infrapatellar fat pad**, **suprapatellar bursa**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Menisci: fibrocartilage, shock absorbers, deepen the tibial surface, spread synovial fluid, proprioception.
- Medial = C-shaped, larger, fixed to MCL → more injured; lateral = circular, smaller, mobile via popliteus.
- Intracapsular list includes fat pad and suprapatellar bursa.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the functions of the menisci.</summary>

**Answer:** Deepen the tibial articular surfaces, act as shock absorbers, adapt the tibial surfaces to the changing curvature of the femoral condyles, spread synovial fluid, permit rotatory movements by moving with the femur on the tibia, and give rise to proprioceptive impulses.

**Why:** Slide "Semilunar cartilages (menisci)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the medial and lateral menisci.</summary>

**Answer:** The medial is C-shaped and larger, fixed to the tibial collateral ligament and capsule (less mobile, more liable to injury); the lateral is circular and smaller, more mobile because the popliteus tendon separates it from the capsule and fibular collateral ligament (less injured). Their horns attach to the intercondylar area as described.

**Why:** Slides "Medial meniscus" and "Lateral meniscus".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the medial meniscus injured more often?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it is firmly fixed to the tibial collateral ligament and the capsule, it cannot escape and is more likely to be trapped between the moving femoral and tibial condyles.

**Why:** Slide "Medial meniscus – mobility".
</details>

## 6. Stability, nerve supply, movements, locking and unlocking

- **Stability:** **bony factor negligible** (flat tibial plateau); **main factors: ligaments and muscles** — quadriceps and its tendons, MCL and LCL, ACL and PCL.
- **Nerve supply:** **3 genicular nerves from the tibial nerve + 3 from the common fibular nerve** + branches from the **femoral, obturator and sciatic nerves** (as the hip) → **hip disease may refer pain to the knee**. **Arterial supply:** anastomosis around the knee.

### Movements
| Movement | Muscles |
|---|---|
| **Flexion** | Mainly the **3 hamstrings**; assisted by popliteus, sartorius, gracilis, gastrocnemius |
| **Extension** | **Quadriceps femoris only** |
| **Lateral rotation** | **Biceps femoris only** |
| **Medial rotation** | Mainly **popliteus**; assisted by sartorius, gracilis, semitendinosus, semimembranosus |

- **Flexion-extension:** transverse axis, **meniscofemoral** compartment. **Rotation:** vertical axis, **meniscotibial** compartment.
- **Voluntary rotation:** semiflexed knee, ~50–70°. **Involuntary:** 5–10° medial rotation at the end of extension (**locking**) and 5–10° lateral rotation at the start of flexion (**unlocking**).

### Locking and unlocking
| | **Locking** | **Unlocking** |
|---|---|---|
| When | Last 5–10° of extension | First 5–10° of flexion |
| Foot on ground | **Medial rotation of femur** | **Popliteus laterally rotates femur** |
| Foot off ground | **Lateral rotation of tibia** | **Popliteus medially rotates tibia** |
| Ligaments | **Taut** (the slide states all except the PCL; coronary ligaments are lax) | Loosened |
| Muscles | **Mostly passive** (iliotibial tract: biceps femoris, gluteus maximus, tensor fasciae latae) | **Active: popliteus** |

⭐ **Lock = femur rotates medially; unlock = femur rotates laterally.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Stability depends on ligaments and muscles, not bone.
- Extension = quadriceps only; lateral rotation = biceps femoris only; medial rotation = mainly popliteus.
- Locking: passive medial femoral rotation; unlocking: popliteus (active).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the factors of stability of the knee?</summary>

**Answer:** The bony factor is negligible because the tibial plateau is flat. The main factors are ligamentous and muscular: the quadriceps and its tendons, the medial and lateral collateral ligaments, and the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments.

**Why:** Slide "Stability of the knee joint".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the nerve supply of the knee and its clinical meaning.</summary>

**Answer:** Three genicular nerves from the tibial nerve, three from the common fibular nerve, and branches from the femoral, obturator and sciatic nerves, as for the hip. Hence hip disease can cause pain referred to the knee.

**Why:** Slide "Nerve supply of the knee joint".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain the locking and unlocking mechanism.</summary>

**Answer:** In the last 5-10 degrees of extension the femur rotates medially (foot on the ground), tightening the ligaments and locking the knee passively. To flex, popliteus contracts and laterally rotates the femur (or medially rotates the tibia if the foot is off the ground), unlocking the knee.

**Why:** Slides "Locking and unlocking of the knee joint".
</details>

## 7. Bursae and relations

- **Bursa:** synovial-fluid-filled sac between moving structures; lubricated cushioning; **10 bursae** around the knee in **3 groups** (anterior, medial, lateral).

| Group | Bursae |
|---|---|
| **Anterior** | **Suprapatellar** (communicates with the joint; distension = patella "floating", **water on the knee**); **infrapatellar** (subcutaneous: skin–tibial tuberosity; deep: patellar ligament–upper tibia); **subcutaneous prepatellar** (skin–patella; **housemaid's knee** from kneeling) |
| **Medial** | Medial head of gastrocnemius–capsule (communicates); **semimembranosus–medial tibial condyle** (**Baker cyst**, commonest popliteal swelling); tendons of **S.G.S**–capsule (**anserine bursa**, communicates) |
| **Lateral** | Lateral head of gastrocnemius–capsule; biceps tendon–fibular collateral ligament; **popliteus tendon–lateral femoral condyle** (communicates) |

- **Communicating bursae (slide list):** suprapatellar, gastrocnemius–semimembranosus (deep to the medial head of gastrocnemius), popliteus and anserine.

### Relations
- **Anterior:** extensor apparatus (anteromedial: medial patellar retinaculum; anterolateral: lateral patellar retinaculum).
- **Posterior:** **tibial nerve and popliteal vessels = the "at risk" structures.**
- **Posteromedial:** semimembranosus tendon, with sartorius and gracilis tendons superficial. **Posterolateral:** biceps femoris tendon and **common peroneal nerve** outside the joint; **popliteus tendon inside** the joint.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 10 bursae: anterior, medial, lateral groups; suprapatellar communicates (water on the knee).
- Prepatellar = housemaid's knee; semimembranosus bursitis = Baker cyst; S.G.S = anserine bursa.
- At risk behind the knee: tibial nerve and popliteal vessels; common peroneal nerve lies posterolaterally with biceps femoris.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the bursae on the anterior aspect of the knee and their clinical conditions.</summary>

**Answer:** Suprapatellar (between quadriceps tendon and femur; communicates with the joint; distension makes the patella float: water on the knee), infrapatellar (subcutaneous and deep), and subcutaneous prepatellar (swollen by frequent kneeling: housemaid's knee).

**Why:** Slide "Bursae on the anterior aspect".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the Baker cyst?</summary>

**Answer:** Semimembranosus bursitis (bursa between semimembranosus and the medial tibial condyle), the commonest swelling in the popliteal fossa.

**Why:** Slide "Bursae on the medial aspect".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the posterior relations of the knee.</summary>

**Answer:** Posteriorly: the tibial nerve and popliteal vessels (the structures at risk). Posteromedially: semimembranosus tendon with sartorius and gracilis superficial to it. Posterolaterally: biceps femoris tendon and the common peroneal nerve outside the joint, and the popliteus tendon inside the joint.

**Why:** Slide "Relations".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Fibula takes NO part in the knee joint and is not weight bearing.**
- **Medial condyle = rotation; lateral condyle = weight transmission.**
- **MCL prevents ABduction (and is attached to the medial meniscus); LCL prevents ADduction (and is not attached to the meniscus).**
- **ACL tight in EXTENSION (stops anterior tibial displacement); PCL tight in FLEXION (stops posterior displacement).**
- **Popliteus tendon: intracapsular but extrasynovial.**
- **Capsule is deficient anteriorly (quadriceps tendon, patella, patellar ligament).**
- **Lock = femur rotates medially (passive); unlock = popliteus rotates femur laterally (active).**
- **Extension = quadriceps only; lateral rotation = biceps femoris only.**
- **Medial meniscus: C-shaped, fixed, more injured; lateral: circular, mobile.**
- Slide inconsistencies: "Intracapsular (7)" with six items listed, and the slides differ on which bursae communicate (suprapatellar and popliteus are common to both lists); do not rely on these details.
