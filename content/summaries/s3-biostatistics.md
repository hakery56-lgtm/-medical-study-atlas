## 2. Types of statistics

### 1. Descriptive statistics
- **Summarize or describe the important characteristics of a known set of data**; methods producing **quantitative summaries** (**measures of central tendency, measures of dispersion**).
- A **data set** = a collection of responses/observations from a sample or entire population.
- Characteristics reported:
  - **Distribution** — the **frequency of each value**.
  - **Central tendency** — the **averages** of the values.
  - **Variability (dispersion)** — **how spread out** the values are.
- Researchers collect data on a **"sample"** from a much larger **"population"**.

### 2. Inferential statistics
- Use **sample data to make inferences (generalizations) about a population**: **guesses (estimates or decisions) about a population based on sample data, with an evaluation of the certainty of the guess**.
- **Statistical inference** = drawing conclusions regarding a population by studying only a portion of its members.
- Most accurate when the subpopulation is **homogenous (uniform)**, **truly representative**, and **chosen randomly to eliminate sampling error or bias**.
- **Two main uses:**
  1. **Making estimates** about populations (e.g. **mean BMI in adults**).
  2. **Testing hypotheses** about populations (e.g. relation between **BMI and family income**).
- **Order of analysis:** first **describe** the data (e.g. average age, relation of age to creativity), then use **inferential statistics** to decide whether the data **confirm or refute the hypothesis** and whether it is **generalizable**.

| | **Descriptive** | **Inferential** |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | **summarize** a data set | **conclude/predict about a population** from a sample |
| Tools | **distribution, central tendency, dispersion** | **estimation, hypothesis testing** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Descriptive: summarize data — distribution, central tendency, variability.
- Inferential: sample → population: estimates + hypothesis testing; best when the sample is homogenous, representative, random.
- Step 1 describe, step 2 infer.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate descriptive from inferential statistics.</summary>

**Answer:** Descriptive statistics summarize and describe the important characteristics of a data set (distribution, central tendency, variability). Inferential statistics use sample data to make estimates or test hypotheses about the population from which the sample was taken, with an evaluation of the certainty.

**Why:** Summary vs generalization.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. When is statistical inference most accurate?</summary>

**Answer:** When the studied subpopulation is homogenous (uniform), truly representative of the larger group, and chosen randomly to eliminate sampling error or bias.

**Why:** Conditions for valid generalization.
</details>

**Answer:** Making estimates about populations (e.g. mean BMI in adults) and testing hypotheses to draw conclusions about populations (e.g. the relationship between BMI and family income).

</details>

## 3. Population, sample, data and variables

### Population
- **The entire group you want to study and about which you draw conclusions** (also **reference, target or study population**); **not limited to people** (any collection of objects, e.g. **all hospitals in a country**).

### Sample
- **The specific group that actually participates in the research**; **a subgroup drawn from the population (selected members)**.
- It must **truly represent the target population**; **interest is in what it tells about the population**.
- Because of **chance, different samples give different results**.
- **Example:** **5000 undergraduate students (population)** → **300 volunteers from three colleges (sample)**.

### Data
- **The raw material of statistics** = numbers that can be put into categories.
| | **Quantitative (numerical) data** | **Qualitative (categorical) data** |
|---|---|---|
| Deals with | **numbers** | **descriptions** |
| Nature | **measured, counted or recorded** | **observed but NOT measured** |
| Examples | **age, weight, height, family income, cell count, body temperature, BP** | **gender, race, residence, education, socioeconomic status** |

### Variable
- **Any characteristic of an individual that varies among the members of the population**; can be **measured (BP), counted (number of patients) or recorded (age, gender)**; **varies between people and over time in the same person**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Population = the whole target group (people or objects); sample = selected subgroup that must represent it.
- Data: quantitative (measured/counted) vs qualitative (described).
- Variable = characteristic that varies.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define population and sample with an example.</summary>

**Answer:** The population is the entire group (people or objects) to be studied and about which conclusions are drawn; the sample is the subgroup of selected members that actually participates and must represent the population. Example: 5000 university undergraduates (population) and 300 volunteers from three colleges (sample).

**Why:** Slide "Population and sample".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate quantitative from qualitative data with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Quantitative data deal with numbers that can be measured, counted or recorded (age, weight, height, income, cell count, temperature); qualitative data deal with descriptions that are observed but not measured (gender, race, residence, education, socioeconomic status).

**Why:** Two kinds of data.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define a variable.</summary>

**Answer:** Any characteristic of an individual that varies among members of a population; it can be measured (blood pressure), counted (number of patients) or recorded (age or gender), and varies between people and times.

**Why:** Slide "Data and variables".
</details>

## 4. Types of variables

### 1. Numerical variables (counts and measurements)
| Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Discrete** | **countable distinct values; whole numbers/integers** | **number of children, number of pregnancies, asthma attacks per week** |
| ⭐ **Continuous** | **measured on a continuous scale; any value in a range** | **blood pressure, temperature, age, weight, Hb level** |
- **Quantitative data = discrete or continuous.**

### 2. Categorical variables
- **Data one can name and put into categories; cannot be measured; each individual belongs to one of several mutually exclusive classes; ALWAYS discrete.**
| Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Nominal** (unordered) | categories **cannot be arranged/ranked** | **Dichotomous (binary):** gender, survival, exposure state; **Multichotomous:** blood group, marital status, occupation, eye colour |
| ⭐ **Ordinal** (ordered) | categories have a **natural order/rank** | **score ranking:** birth order; **category ranking:** disease stage (mild–moderate–severe), social class (high, middle, low), education level (primary, secondary, university) |

- **Classification:** data → **quantitative (numerical): discrete, continuous**; **qualitative (categorical): nominal, ordinal**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Numerical: discrete (countable integers) vs continuous (any value in a range).
- Categorical (always discrete): nominal (unordered; dichotomous or multichotomous) vs ordinal (ordered, ranked).
- Examples: children number = discrete; BP/age/Hb = continuous; blood group = nominal; disease stage = ordinal.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify variables with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Numerical variables: discrete (number of children, pregnancies, asthma attacks per week) and continuous (blood pressure, temperature, age, weight, Hb). Categorical variables: nominal (gender, blood group, marital status, occupation, eye colour) and ordinal (disease stage, social class, education level, birth order).

**Why:** Figure 1.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate nominal from ordinal data.</summary>

**Answer:** Nominal data are categories with no natural order (dichotomous such as gender or survival; multichotomous such as blood group, marital status); ordinal data are categories with a natural order or rank (mild–moderate–severe stage, social class, education level, birth order).

**Why:** Order is the key difference.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate discrete from continuous variables.</summary>

**Answer:** Discrete variables take only countable, distinct whole-number values (number of children, pregnancies, asthma attacks); continuous variables are measured on a continuous scale and can take any value in a range (blood pressure, temperature, age, weight, Hb).

**Why:** Counted vs measured.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Descriptive = summarize; inferential = generalize/estimate/test hypotheses.**
- **Sample must be representative and random** (reduces sampling error).
- **Population is not limited to people.**
- **Quantitative = measured/counted (discrete or continuous); qualitative = described (nominal or ordinal).**
- **Categorical variables are ALWAYS discrete.**
- **Age = continuous; number of children = discrete; blood group = nominal; disease stage = ordinal.**
- **Dichotomous = 2 categories (gender, survival, exposure).**
- **"Qualitative data are discrete" (as stated on the slide).**
- **Different samples give different results (chance).**
