# Axillary Nerve: Course & Effects of Lesion — Exam Night Review

A short, high-yield lecture: **where the axillary nerve comes from, where it runs, what it supplies, and what happens when it is injured.** After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

---

## 1. Overview

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Root values** | **C5–C6** |
| **Origin** | **posterior cord** of the brachial plexus |
| **Motor** | **deltoid** and **teres minor** |
| **Sensory** | skin over the **lateral shoulder** — the ⭐ **"regimental badge" area** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Axillary nerve: C5–C6, from the posterior cord.
- Motor: deltoid + teres minor. Sensory: regimental badge area (lateral shoulder).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give the root value, origin and distribution of the axillary nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** Root values C5–C6; it arises from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus; it supplies the deltoid and teres minor (motor) and the skin over the lateral shoulder — the regimental badge area (sensory).

**Why:** Roots, cord, motor, sensory — the four facts of every nerve answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the "regimental badge" area?</summary>

**Answer:** The skin over the lateral shoulder (lower part of the deltoid) supplied by the axillary nerve through the upper lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm; pin-prick sensation here is tested to assess the axillary nerve.

**Why:** It is where a soldier's badge sits on the sleeve.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which muscles does the axillary nerve supply?</summary>

**Answer:** Deltoid and teres minor.

**Why:** Only two muscles — easy marks.
</details>

## 2. Course of the axillary nerve

1. Arises from the **posterior cord (C5–C6)** in the axilla.
2. Passes through the ⭐ **quadrangular space** together with the **posterior circumflex humeral vessels**.
3. **Winds around the surgical neck of the humerus**.
4. Divides into:
   - **Anterior branch → deltoid**.
   - **Posterior branch → teres minor, posterior deltoid** and the **upper lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm** (regimental badge area).

### Boundaries of the quadrangular space
| Boundary | Structure |
|---|---|
| **Above** | **teres minor** |
| **Below** | **teres major** |
| **Medially** | **long head of triceps** |
| **Laterally** | **surgical neck of humerus** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Posterior cord → quadrangular space (with posterior circumflex humeral vessels) → around the surgical neck.
- Quadrangular space: teres minor above, teres major below, long head of triceps medially, surgical neck laterally.
- Anterior branch → deltoid; posterior branch → teres minor, posterior deltoid, upper lateral cutaneous nerve.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the course of the axillary nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** It arises from the posterior cord (C5–C6), passes backwards through the quadrangular space with the posterior circumflex humeral vessels, winds around the surgical neck of the humerus, and divides into an anterior branch to the deltoid and a posterior branch to teres minor, the posterior deltoid and the upper lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm.

**Why:** Exam summary line: C5–C6 → posterior cord → quadrangular space → around surgical neck.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the boundaries and contents of the quadrangular space.</summary>

**Answer:** Boundaries: teres minor above, teres major below, long head of triceps medially and surgical neck of humerus laterally. Contents: the axillary nerve and the posterior circumflex humeral vessels.

**Why:** Four boundaries + two contents.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the axillary nerve at risk in fractures of the surgical neck of the humerus?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it winds directly around the surgical neck of the humerus (in contact with the bone), so the fracture can stretch or injure it.

**Why:** Anatomy explains the injury.
</details>

## 3. Functions

- **Muscles:** deltoid and teres minor.
- **Motor function:** ⭐ **abduction of the arm at the shoulder beyond the first 15°** (deltoid) — the first 15° is started by supraspinatus. Teres minor helps **external (lateral) rotation**.
- **Sensory:** skin over the shoulder (regimental badge area).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Deltoid abducts the arm beyond the first 15°.
- Teres minor → external rotation.
- Sensation: lateral shoulder.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the motor functions of the axillary nerve?</summary>

**Answer:** Through the deltoid it produces abduction of the arm at the shoulder beyond the first 15°; through teres minor it helps external rotation of the shoulder.

**Why:** Deltoid = abduction after 15°; teres minor = external rotation.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why can a patient with axillary nerve palsy still start abduction?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the first 15° of abduction is initiated by supraspinatus (suprascapular nerve), not the deltoid; the deltoid is needed beyond that.

**Why:** Classic "explain" question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which nerve supplies teres minor and what is its action?</summary>

**Answer:** The axillary nerve (posterior branch); teres minor laterally (externally) rotates the arm.

**Why:** Teres minor is the rotator cuff muscle supplied by the axillary nerve.
</details>

## 4. Effects of axillary nerve lesion

- **Deltoid weakness → impaired shoulder abduction beyond the initial 15°**.
- **Teres minor weakness → reduced external rotation** may occur.
- **Sensory loss:** numbness / paraesthesia over the lateral shoulder (**regimental badge area**).
- **Long-standing lesion → deltoid wasting** and a ⭐ **flattened shoulder contour** ("flat shoulder").

### Common causes
- ⭐ **Anterior shoulder dislocation**.
- ⭐ **Surgical-neck fracture of the humerus**.
- **Traction** or **surgical injury**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Lesion → weak abduction beyond 15°, reduced external rotation, regimental badge sensory loss.
- Chronic → deltoid wasting, flat shoulder.
- Causes: anterior dislocation, surgical-neck fracture, traction, surgery.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the effects of axillary nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Paralysis/weakness of the deltoid → impaired abduction of the shoulder beyond the first 15°; weakness of teres minor → reduced external rotation; sensory loss (numbness/paraesthesia) over the regimental badge area; in long-standing lesions, deltoid wasting with a flattened shoulder contour.

**Why:** Motor + sensory + late sign.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the common causes of axillary nerve injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Anterior shoulder dislocation, fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus, traction injury and surgical injury.

**Why:** The first two are the classic ones.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does the shoulder look flat in a long-standing axillary nerve lesion?</summary>

**Answer:** The paralysed deltoid wastes (atrophies), so the rounded contour of the shoulder is lost and the acromion becomes prominent.

**Why:** The deltoid gives the shoulder its round shape.
</details>

## 5. Clinical examination

1. **Test deltoid power** by **resisted shoulder abduction**.
2. **Assess sensation** (pin-prick) over the **lateral deltoid / regimental badge area**.
3. **Look for deltoid atrophy** or **loss of shoulder contour**.
4. ⭐ After shoulder trauma or dislocation, **document axillary nerve function before and after reduction**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Resisted abduction (deltoid), pin-prick in the regimental badge area, look for wasting.
- Always document axillary nerve function before and after reducing a dislocation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How do you examine the axillary nerve?</summary>

**Answer:** Test deltoid power by resisted shoulder abduction; test pin-prick sensation over the lateral deltoid (regimental badge area); inspect for deltoid atrophy or loss of the shoulder contour.

**Why:** Motor, sensory, inspection.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why must axillary nerve function be documented before and after reducing a dislocated shoulder?</summary>

**Answer:** The nerve can be injured by the dislocation itself or during the reduction manoeuvre; documenting it before and after shows when the injury occurred and is important clinically and medico-legally.

**Why:** A practical point stressed in the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which sensory test assesses the axillary nerve?</summary>

**Answer:** Pin-prick sensation over the regimental badge area on the lateral shoulder.

**Why:** Its autonomous sensory area.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- Roots are **C5–C6** from the **posterior** cord (not lateral or medial cord).
- Deltoid abducts **beyond 15°** — the first 15° is supraspinatus.
- Quadrangular space: **teres minor above, teres major below** (don't swap), long head of triceps **medially**, humerus **laterally**.
- Vessels with the nerve: **posterior** circumflex humeral (not anterior).
- Classic causes: **anterior** dislocation and **surgical-neck** fracture.
