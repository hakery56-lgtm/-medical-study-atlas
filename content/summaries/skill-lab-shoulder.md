# Skill Lab (Week 2): Shoulder Joint Examination — Exam Night Review

This skill lab is **practical**: the anatomy you need at the bedside, **rotator cuff impingement**, how to **examine the shoulder (look, feel, move, special tests)**, the **OSCE checklist**, the causes of shoulder pain, and **clinical reasoning with illness scripts**. After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

### Objectives of the session
1. Describe the **surface anatomy** of the shoulder joint.
2. Explain the **function and innervation of the rotator cuff** muscles.
3. Take a **focused history** from a patient with shoulder pain.
4. Perform a **physical examination** of the shoulder joint.
5. **Interpret** the history and examination findings (clinical reasoning).
6. **Differentiate** common **non-traumatic** causes of shoulder pain and manage them using **illness scripts**.

## 1. The shoulder joint complex & the rotator cuff

The **shoulder** is made of:
- the **glenohumeral joint**,
- the **acromioclavicular joint**,
- the **subacromial space**,
- plus movement **between the scapula and the chest wall** (scapulothoracic).

### The rotator cuff (SITS)
| Muscle | Nerve |
|---|---|
| **Supraspinatus** | suprascapular nerve |
| **Infraspinatus** | suprascapular nerve |
| **Teres minor** | axillary nerve |
| **Subscapularis** | upper and lower subscapular nerves |

They and their **tendinous insertions** give **stability and movement**, particularly **abduction at the glenohumeral joint**.

### Scapulohumeral rhythm (lecture figure)
- **Early abduction:** most movement is at the **glenohumeral joint**.
- As the arm rises, the **scapula begins to rotate** on the thorax.
- **Last phase:** movement is almost entirely **scapulothoracic**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Shoulder = glenohumeral + acromioclavicular + subacromial space + scapulothoracic movement.
- Rotator cuff = supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis → stability + abduction.
- Scapulohumeral rhythm: glenohumeral first, then scapular rotation, finally scapulothoracic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What structures make up the shoulder joint complex?</summary>

**Answer:** The glenohumeral joint, the acromioclavicular joint and the subacromial space; movement also occurs between the scapula and the chest wall.

**Why:** First line of the lab — often the first viva question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name the rotator cuff muscles, their nerve supply and function.</summary>

**Answer:** Supraspinatus and infraspinatus (suprascapular nerve), teres minor (axillary nerve) and subscapularis (upper and lower subscapular nerves). Together with their tendons they stabilize the glenohumeral joint and assist its movement, particularly abduction.

**Why:** Objective 2 of the session.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the scapulohumeral rhythm.</summary>

**Answer:** In the early phase of abduction most of the movement is at the glenohumeral joint; as the arm rises the scapula begins to rotate on the thorax; in the last phase of abduction the movement is almost entirely scapulothoracic.

**Why:** Explains why full abduction to 180° needs both the joint and the scapula.
</details>

## 2. Rotator cuff impingement syndrome

- A **painful disorder** thought to arise from **repetitive compression or rubbing of the tendons** — mainly ⭐ **supraspinatus** — **under the coracoacromial arch**.
- **Normally**, when the arm is abducted, the conjoint tendon slides smoothly under the coracoacromial arch.
- If the arm is held **persistently in abduction** and moved **to and fro in internal and external rotation** — e.g. ⭐ **cleaning a window, painting a wall, polishing a flat surface** — the cuff is **compressed and irritated** against the **anterior edge of the acromion** and the **coracoacromial ligament**.
- The attitude **abduction + slight flexion + internal rotation** = the ⭐ **"impingement position"**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Impingement = repetitive compression of the cuff (mainly supraspinatus) under the coracoacromial arch.
- Structures pressing on the cuff: anterior edge of acromion + coracoacromial ligament.
- Impingement position: abduction, slight flexion, internal rotation (window cleaning, painting, polishing).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the pathology of rotator cuff impingement syndrome.</summary>

**Answer:** Normally the conjoint tendon slides under the coracoacromial arch during abduction. If the arm is held persistently in abduction and moved to and fro in internal and external rotation, the rotator cuff (mainly supraspinatus) is repeatedly compressed and rubbed against the anterior edge of the acromion and the coracoacromial ligament, becoming irritated and painful.

**Why:** Mechanism + the structures involved.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the "impingement position"? Give examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Abduction, slight flexion and internal rotation of the arm, as used in cleaning a window, painting a wall or polishing a flat surface.

**Why:** Links the occupation in the case (house painter) to the diagnosis.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which tendon is mainly affected in impingement and why?</summary>

**Answer:** Supraspinatus, because its tendon passes directly under the coracoacromial arch (acromion + coracoacromial ligament) in the subacromial space.

**Why:** Location explains the vulnerability.
</details>

## 3. Physical examination — Look, Feel, Move

Ask the patient to **sit or stand** and **expose the shoulder completely**.

### Look
- **Size and position of the scapula:** elevated, depressed or ⭐ **"winged"**.
- Figures: **right anterior glenohumeral dislocation → loss of the normal shoulder contour**; **winging of the scapula → paralysis of the nerve to serratus anterior** (long thoracic nerve).

### Feel (palpate in order)
**Sternoclavicular joint → along the clavicle → acromioclavicular joint → acromion → coracoid process** (⭐ **2 cm inferior and medial to the clavicle tip**) **→ spine of scapula → biceps tendon in the bicipital groove.**
- ⭐ **Extend the shoulder** to bring **supraspinatus anterior to the acromion** → allows palpation of the **supraspinatus tendon**.

### Move
**Flexion, extension, internal and external rotation, abduction and adduction** (and circumduction).
- Abduction goes up to **180°**; rotation is tested **in neutral adduction** and **in 90° abduction**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Look: scapula elevated/depressed/winged; loss of contour in dislocation.
- Feel: SC joint → clavicle → AC joint → acromion → coracoid (2 cm below and medial to clavicle tip) → scapular spine → biceps tendon.
- Extend the shoulder to palpate the supraspinatus tendon.
- Move: flexion, extension, IR, ER, abduction, adduction (+ circumduction).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What do you palpate in the shoulder examination, and in what order?</summary>

**Answer:** The sternoclavicular joint, along the clavicle to the acromioclavicular joint, the acromion and coracoid processes (coracoid 2 cm inferior and medial to the clavicle tip), the spine of the scapula and the biceps tendon in the bicipital groove; then extend the shoulder to bring supraspinatus in front of the acromion and palpate its tendon.

**Why:** Medial to lateral, bone by bone.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do you palpate the supraspinatus tendon?</summary>

**Answer:** Extend the shoulder; this brings the supraspinatus tendon anterior to the acromion process where it can be palpated.

**Why:** Normally it is hidden under the acromion.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What causes winging of the scapula and a loss of the shoulder contour?</summary>

**Answer:** Winging is caused by paralysis of the nerve to serratus anterior (long thoracic nerve); loss of the normal shoulder contour is seen in anterior glenohumeral dislocation.

**Why:** Both are figures in the lab.
</details>

## 4. Special tests for impingement

### Painful arc
- **Passively and fully abduct** the arm, then ask the patient to **lower (adduct) it slowly**.
- ⭐ Pain **between 60° and 120°** of abduction = **painful arc**.
- Also: passively abduct the **internally rotated** arm to **30–45°** and ask them to continue — pain on active movement, **especially against resistance**, suggests impingement.

### Neer test
- Patient **sits relaxed** with the **elbow fully extended**.
- The examiner **prevents scapular rotation** with one hand and with the other **abducts (passively flexes) the arm in internal rotation**.
- The **greater tuberosity impinges against the acromion** → **pain = positive**.
- Passive flexion **from 90° to 180°** narrows the passage made by the **acromion, coracoacromial ligament and humeral head** → compresses the **supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons**.
- ⭐ A **negative** Neer test is somewhat useful to **exclude** impingement.

### Hawkins–Kennedy test
- Patient sitting or standing, **shoulder flexed 90°** and **elbow flexed 90°**, supported by the examiner for maximal relaxation.
- The examiner **forcefully rotates the arm internally**.
- **Reproduction of the patient's pain = positive**.
- It narrows the **coracoacromial space**, worsening existing impingement; it also elicits tenderness in **rotator cuff tendonitis**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Painful arc = pain between 60° and 120° of abduction.
- Neer: elbow extended, scapula fixed, passive flexion/abduction in internal rotation → greater tuberosity hits acromion; negative test helps exclude impingement.
- Hawkins–Kennedy: shoulder 90° + elbow 90°, forceful internal rotation → pain = positive.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe how to perform the painful arc test and interpret it.</summary>

**Answer:** Passively and fully abduct the patient's arm and ask them to lower it slowly. Pain occurring between 60° and 120° of abduction is a painful arc, suggesting impingement (the inflamed tendon passes under the acromion in this range). Pain on active abduction, especially against resistance, also suggests impingement.

**Why:** Technique + range + meaning.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the Neer test.</summary>

**Answer:** With the patient sitting relaxed and the elbow fully extended, the examiner prevents scapular rotation with one hand and with the other passively raises the arm (flexion from 90° to 180°) in internal rotation. This drives the greater tuberosity against the acromion, narrowing the space under the acromion and coracoacromial ligament and compressing the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons. Pain = positive. A negative test is somewhat useful to exclude impingement.

**Why:** Position, manoeuvre, mechanism, interpretation.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the Hawkins–Kennedy test.</summary>

**Answer:** The patient sits or stands with the shoulder flexed to 90° and the elbow flexed to 90°, supported by the examiner. The examiner forcefully rotates the arm internally; reproduction of the patient's pain is positive. It narrows the coracoacromial space and worsens existing impingement, and elicits tenderness in rotator cuff tendonitis.

**Why:** 90/90 + internal rotation — easy to remember.
</details>

## 5. OSCE checklist for shoulder examination (total 10 marks)

| Step | Items | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| **Introduction & rapport** | greets and introduces self; **washes hands before and after**; asks how the patient wishes to be addressed; explains the examination; **obtains consent and ensures privacy** | 1 |
| **Look** | inspects all around the joint for discoloration, scars, bony/joint swellings or deformities; **compares with the other side**; inspects from the **front, sides (subacromial), back and above** | 1 |
| **Feel** | palpates the surface landmarks; palpates **supraspinatus, infraspinatus, deltoid and biceps** areas for tenderness; comments on **temperature, swelling, tenderness** | 2 |
| **Move – active** | forward flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, external rotation, **circumduction** | 2 |
| **Move – passive** | tests passive ROM | 1 |
| **Special tests** | **painful arc, Neer test** | 2 |
| **Closure** | examines the **joint above and joint below**; **checks distal pulse**; **neurological assessment**; thanks the patient | 1 |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Order: introduction → look → feel → move (active then passive) → special tests → closure.
- Closure = joint above and below (neck and elbow), distal (radial) pulse, neurological assessment, thank the patient.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Outline the steps of the shoulder examination as in the OSCE checklist.</summary>

**Answer:** Introduction and rapport (greet, wash hands, consent, privacy); Look (all around, compare sides, from front, sides, back and above); Feel (landmarks, supraspinatus/infraspinatus/deltoid/biceps tenderness, temperature, swelling); Move (active ROM in all directions including circumduction, then passive ROM); Special tests (painful arc, Neer); Closure (examine the joint above and below, check distal pulse, neurological assessment, thank the patient).

**Why:** The checklist is exactly what the examiner scores.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is done in the closure of a shoulder examination?</summary>

**Answer:** Examine the joint above (cervical spine) and the joint below (elbow), check the distal pulse (radial), perform a neurological assessment and thank the patient.

**Why:** Easy marks that students forget.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. From which directions do you inspect the shoulder?</summary>

**Answer:** From the front, the sides (subacromial area), the back and from above, comparing with the other side.

**Why:** Part of the "Look" mark.
</details>

## 6. Shoulder pain — causes

Shoulder pain is **one of the most common musculoskeletal complaints**. The shoulder is one of the **most complex and mobile joints**, crossed by muscle, tendon and bone and surrounded by major **neurovascular structures** — all possible sources of **local and referred pain**.

### Causes of shoulder girdle pain (Table 13.15)
| Structure | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Rotator cuff** | degeneration; tendon rupture; **tendonitis**; calcification |
| **Subacromial bursa** | inflammation (inflammatory arthritis, injury or overuse); calcification |
| **Capsule** | inflammation — ⭐ **adhesive capsulitis**, polymyalgia rheumatica |
| **Head of humerus** | tumour; osteonecrosis; fracture/dislocation |
| **Joints** | **glenohumeral & sternoclavicular:** inflammatory arthritis, osteoarthritis, dislocation, infection; **acromioclavicular:** subluxation, osteoarthritis |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Structures that cause pain: rotator cuff, subacromial bursa, capsule, humeral head, joints (GH, SC, AC).
- Cuff: degeneration, rupture, tendonitis, calcification. Capsule: adhesive capsulitis, PMR.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the causes of shoulder girdle pain according to the structure involved.</summary>

**Answer:** Rotator cuff — degeneration, tendon rupture, tendonitis, calcification; subacromial bursa — inflammation (inflammatory arthritis, injury, overuse), calcification; capsule — adhesive capsulitis, polymyalgia rheumatica; head of humerus — tumour, osteonecrosis, fracture/dislocation; joints — glenohumeral and sternoclavicular (inflammatory arthritis, osteoarthritis, dislocation, infection) and acromioclavicular (subluxation, osteoarthritis).

**Why:** Table 13.15 organised by structure.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is shoulder pain so common and varied?</summary>

**Answer:** The shoulder is one of the most complex and mobile joints; it is traversed by muscle, tendon and bone and surrounded by major neurovascular structures, all of which can be sources of local and referred pain.

**Why:** Straight from the lab introduction.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which capsular causes of shoulder pain are listed?</summary>

**Answer:** Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder) and polymyalgia rheumatica.

**Why:** Capsule = inflammation.
</details>

## 7. Clinical reasoning — the cases

### Case 1 — Ali Hamza (standardized patient)
| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Patient | **36-year-old male** |
| Complaint | **right shoulder pain, 4 weeks** |
| Aggravating / relieving | **overhead activity, lying on the affected side**; **rest decreases pain** |
| Character / severity | **dull**, **6/10** |
| Course | **increased over the last 2 weeks** |
| Associated | **no** stiffness, swelling, redness, warmth, fever or rash |
| Occupation | ⭐ **house painter for 10 years** |
| PMH / FH / drugs | none; **no trauma or surgery** |
| Social | **smoker**, not alcoholic |
| Examination | no swelling, hotness or redness; **tenderness at the anterior edge of the acromion**; **pain and limitation of active abduction and external rotation, no weakness**; ⭐ **normal passive motion**; **positive painful arc, Neer and Hawkins tests**; normal neck and elbow, radial pulse, tone and bulk |

➡️ **Interpretation:** overhead occupation (painting = impingement position) + anterior acromial tenderness + painful arc + positive Neer and Hawkins + **normal passive motion** + **no weakness** → **rotator cuff (supraspinatus) impingement / tendinopathy**. Normal passive motion argues **against adhesive capsulitis**; no weakness argues **against a full-thickness cuff tear**.

### Case 2 — illness script exercise
A **42-year-old diabetic man** with **progressive shoulder pain for 4 weeks**, worse **lying on the affected side** and **lifting the arm overhead**; no swelling, but **tenderness and painful limitation of motion**; normal neck, elbow, pulse, tone and bulk.
- You fill an **illness script** for **three differentials**: epidemiology, time course, clinical presentation, pathophysiology, labs/imaging → final problem representation.
- Differentials to consider (from the causes table): **rotator cuff impingement/tendinopathy**, **adhesive capsulitis** (⭐ associated with **diabetes**; passive movement is also restricted), **subacromial bursitis**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Focused history: site, duration, aggravating/relieving factors, character, severity, course, associated symptoms, occupation, PMH, drugs, social.
- Case 1 (painter, positive impingement tests, normal passive ROM, no weakness) → rotator cuff impingement.
- Illness script = epidemiology, time course, presentation, pathophysiology, labs/imaging for each differential.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the most likely diagnosis in Ali Hamza (36-year-old house painter) and why?</summary>

**Answer:** Rotator cuff (supraspinatus) impingement syndrome. He works in the impingement position (painting overhead for 10 years), has dull pain worse with overhead activity and lying on the shoulder, tenderness at the anterior edge of the acromion, painful limitation of active abduction and external rotation, and positive painful arc, Neer and Hawkins tests. Passive motion is normal (against frozen shoulder) and there is no weakness (against a complete cuff tear), with no signs of inflammation or trauma.

**Why:** Clinical reasoning = linking each finding to the diagnosis.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the components of an illness script?</summary>

**Answer:** For each differential diagnosis: epidemiology, time course, clinical presentation, pathophysiology and labs/imaging, leading to a final problem representation/diagnosis.

**Why:** The table used in the lab.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does normal passive movement help in the differential diagnosis of shoulder pain?</summary>

**Answer:** In impingement/tendinopathy, pain limits active movement but passive range is normal. In adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder) the capsule is contracted, so both active and passive movements are restricted.

**Why:** Active vs passive range is the key discriminator.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- Painful arc range is **60°–120°** (not 0–60 or 120–180).
- **Neer**: elbow **extended**, scapula **fixed**, arm in **internal rotation**. **Hawkins–Kennedy**: shoulder **90°**, elbow **90°**, **forceful internal rotation**.
- A **negative** Neer test helps **exclude** impingement.
- The coracoid is **2 cm inferior and medial** to the clavicle tip.
- To palpate supraspinatus, **extend** the shoulder.
- Winged scapula = **nerve to serratus anterior**, not axillary nerve.
- Normal **passive** motion → impingement, not frozen shoulder.
