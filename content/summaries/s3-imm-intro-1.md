## 1. Course outline and what immunity is

- ⭐ **Immunity** (Latin immunis = exempt) is the **body's ability to resist or eliminate potentially harmful foreign materials or abnormal cells.**
- **Course (first semester):** innate immunity, adaptive immunity, cells and organs, antigen recognition molecules, B- and T-cell receptors, MHC, antigen processing and presentation, antibody-mediated immunity, complement, cell-mediated immunity.
- **References:** Kuby Immunology (8th ed, 2019), Jawetz Medical Microbiology (28th ed, 2019), Review of Medical Microbiology and Immunology (15th ed, 2018).
- **Objectives:** immunity and its types, immunological terms and symbols, the organs and cells involved.
- The immune system recognizes and responds to **non-self antigen**; **diseased, damaged, distressed or dying cells** are recognized and eliminated.
- It has **two complementary arms: innate and adaptive.** Adaptive components are **B and T lymphocytes and their effector cells.**

### Appropriate versus inappropriate responses
| Appropriate | Inappropriate |
|---|---|
| **Defense against pathogens** (viruses, bacteria) | **Allergies** (response to harmless substances) |
| **Removal of worn-out cells** (old RBCs) and **tissue debris** | **Autoimmune diseases** |
| **Destruction of abnormal or mutant cells** (primary defense against **cancer**) | |
| **Rejection of foreign cells** (organ transplant) | |

- **The immune system is a functional system, not an organ system:** skin and mucous membranes (physical barriers), secretions (tears, mucus), blood cells and WBCs, bone marrow, **liver (makes complement proteins)**, lymphatic system and lymphoid organs, and resident immune cells in most tissues.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Immunity = resistance to or elimination of harmful foreign material or abnormal cells.
- Two arms: innate (first line) and adaptive (B and T cells).
- Appropriate: defense, clearing old cells, anti-cancer, graft rejection. Inappropriate: allergy and autoimmunity.

</div>

## 2. Innate immunity: general features

- ⭐ **Innate immunity = first line of natural defense** against infectious agents.
- **Barriers:** **anatomic/physical** (skin, mucous membranes, normal flora); **physiologic** (temperature, pH, antimicrobial substances, cytokines); **complement**; **cellular** (phagocytes, granulocytes) and **inflammation**.
- **Common characteristics:** present **with or without previous stimulation**; **limited specificity** for shared microbe structures; **limited diversity** (few PRRs); **not enhanced on re-exposure → no memory.**
- **PAMP** = a specific molecule on harmful germs that helps detect infection. **PRRs** recognize structures on pathogens, apoptotic host cells and damaged senescent cells; expressed by **dendritic cells, macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils** and epithelial cells.

### Innate versus adaptive
| Feature | Innate (1° line) | Adaptive (2° line) |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | **No lag** (rapid) | **Lag period** (slower) |
| **Specificity** | **Not antigen specific** | **Antigen specific** |
| **Memory** | **None** | **Memory develops** |

- **Typical response:** innate = rapid, broad range of microbes (external: skin, mucous membranes, secretions; internal: phagocytes, antimicrobial proteins, inflammation, NK cells, complement). Acquired = slower, specific: **humoral (antibodies)** and **cell-mediated (cytotoxic lymphocytes).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Innate = fast, non-specific, no memory; adaptive = slower, specific, memory.
- PRRs recognize PAMPs and are found mainly on innate cells.
- Four innate barriers: anatomic, physiologic, complement, cellular + inflammation.

</div>

## 3. Anatomical barriers (external defenses)

| Barrier | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Mechanical** | **Skin**; flushing action of **saliva, tears, urine**; **mucociliary escalator** (cilia); **desquamation** of skin epithelium |
| **Chemical** | **Dermcidin** in sweat; **lysozyme** in tears and saliva (cleaves **peptidoglycan**); **HCl** in stomach (digests protein, kills bacteria) |
| **Biological** | **Normal flora** (> 1000 bacterial species) **competes with pathogens** for nutrients and space |

- Most microorganisms are **detected and destroyed within minutes or hours** by innate immunity.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Mechanical = skin, flushing, cilia; chemical = dermcidin, lysozyme, HCl; biological = normal flora.
- Lysozyme attacks bacterial peptidoglycan.
- Most microbes are cleared by innate defenses within minutes to hours.

</div>

## 4. Internal defenses: blood, neutrophils and phagocytosis

- **Blood components:** **plasma (55%)** carries **complement proteins, coagulation proteins and cytokines** (extracellular); the **buffy coat** has WBCs and platelets; **RBCs 45%.**
- **WBCs** shown: macrophages (from monocytes), B lymphocytes, T lymphocytes, NK cells, mast cells.
- ⭐ **Neutrophils:** the **most abundant WBCs (50-60%)**, the **first to arrive**, efficient phagocytes; they **die by apoptosis** and are cleared by macrophages. Functions: **degranulation, ROS, phagocytosis, NETs.** The bone marrow makes about **10¹¹ neutrophils/day.**
- **Phagocytosis** (phago = eat, cyte = cell) steps: **1** chemotaxis and adherence → **2** ingestion → **3** phagosome → **4** fusion with lysosome = **phagolysosome** → **5** enzymatic digestion → **6** residual body → **7** discharge of waste.
- **Granules** contain **lysozyme** and other antimicrobial proteins. Neutrophils also **trigger the inflammatory response.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Plasma carries complement, coagulation proteins and cytokines; WBCs are in the buffy coat.
- Neutrophils: most abundant, first responders, phagocytes, die by apoptosis.
- Phagocytosis: chemotaxis → ingestion → phagosome → phagolysosome → digestion → residual body → discharge.

</div>

## 5. Monocytes, macrophages, NK cells and TLRs

- **Monocytes** (~5% of WBCs) migrate into tissues and become **macrophages** ("big eaters": phagocytosis in tissue and **antigen presentation**; neutrophils are only in blood).
- ⭐ **NK cells:** not B or T lymphocytes; **innate killers of virus/bacteria-infected cells and cancer cells.** They **spare cells carrying MHC class I** (inhibitory receptor) and kill cells where surface proteins are missing, by releasing **granules with perforins and proteases.**
- **TLRs:** transmembrane proteins on **macrophages, dendritic cells** and a few others; **innate immune sensors** that look out for microbes, bind them and trigger a cascade. Binding leads to **inflammation, cytokine/interferon secretion, phagocytosis and apoptosis of the infected cell.**
- Other PRR examples: collectins, pentraxins, ficolins, lipid transferases, PGRs, LRR.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Monocyte → macrophage in tissue; macrophages phagocytose and present antigen.
- NK cells kill when MHC class I is missing (virus-infected, tumor); perforins and proteases.
- TLR binding: inflammation, cytokines/IFN, phagocytosis, apoptosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How do NK cells distinguish and kill their targets?</summary>

**Answer:** Normal cells display MHC class I, which engages the NK inhibitory receptor so they are spared; infected or tumor cells lacking MHC class I are killed by granule release containing perforins and proteases.

**Why:** Slides "NK cells differentiate..." and "How does the killer kill?".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are TLRs and what happens when they bind a microbe?</summary>

**Answer:** Transmembrane innate immune sensors on macrophages and dendritic cells; binding triggers inflammation, secretion of cytokines/interferon, phagocytosis and apoptosis of the infected cell.

**Why:** Slides "Toll-like receptors" and "What happens when a TLR binds a microbe?".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the difference between monocytes and macrophages?</summary>

**Answer:** Monocytes circulate in blood (~5% of WBCs); when they migrate into tissues they become macrophages, the big eaters that phagocytose microbes and present antigen.

**Why:** Slides "Monocytes" and "Macrophages".

## 6. Primary versus secondary immune response

| Feature | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| **Occurs** | First contact with antigen | Second and later exposures |
| **Responding cell** | **Naive B and T cells** | **Memory cells** |
| **Lag phase** | Longer (**4-7 days**, sometimes weeks/months) | Shorter (**1-4 days**) |
| **Antibody peak** | **7-10 days** | **3-5 days** |
| **Main antibody** | **IgM** (some IgG) | **IgG** (also IgA; IgE in allergy) |
| **Amount** | Low, depends on antigen | **100-1000 times more** |
| **Decline / affinity** | Declines rapidly; lower affinity | Stays high; greater affinity |
| **Site** | Lymph nodes and spleen | Bone marrow, then spleen and lymph nodes |
| **Antigens** | Thymus-dependent and independent | **Only thymus-dependent** |

- **Graph:** first exposure at day 0 (IgM then small IgG), **second exposure at day 28** giving a much higher, longer IgG response; memory lies in between.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Primary = IgM, slow, low; secondary = IgG, fast, 100-1000 times more.
- Memory cells drive the secondary response.
- Only thymus-dependent antigens give a secondary response.

</div>
