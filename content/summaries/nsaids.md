## 1. Eicosanoids, COX and how they are inhibited

- **Eicosanoids** are **lipid-derived autacoids**.
- **Autacoids** are **endogenous** compounds formed **by the tissues on which they act** → they act as **local hormones**.
- Eicosanoids include **prostaglandins**, **thromboxanes** and **leukotrienes**.
- **Two COX isozymes** convert **arachidonic acid** into prostaglandins:
  - **COX-1**: generates prostanoids for **"housekeeping"**, e.g. **gastric epithelial cytoprotection**.
  - **COX-2**: **the major source of prostanoids in inflammation and cancer**.
- The products of COX enzymes are called **prostanoids**.

### Who inhibits what?
- **Corticosteroids** block **all** known pathways of eicosanoid synthesis because they inhibit **phospholipase** and **COX-2** — they are **first-line drugs for acute rejection**.
- **NSAIDs** (e.g. indomethacin, ibuprofen) block **prostaglandin and thromboxane** formation by **reversibly** inhibiting COX.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Corticosteroids have the **broader** effect (phospholipase + COX-2); NSAIDs act only on COX. NSAIDs **do not inhibit leukotrienes** — important in asthma, as we'll see.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Eicosanoids = lipid-derived autacoids (local hormones): prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes.
- COX-1 = housekeeping (gastric cytoprotection); COX-2 = inflammation and cancer. COX products = prostanoids.
- Corticosteroids block all eicosanoid pathways (inhibit phospholipase + COX-2); NSAIDs reversibly inhibit COX.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Why:** The two definitions are linked and are asked together.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare COX-1 and COX-2.</summary>

</details>

**Why:** The site of action (phospholipase vs COX) is the key difference.
</details>

## 2. Classification of NSAIDs

NSAIDs are a group of **chemically dissimilar** agents that differ in their **antipyretic, analgesic and anti-inflammatory** activity. They are used for inflammatory disorders such as arthritis and RA.

| Class | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Salicylic acid** derivatives | **aspirin**, diflunisal, salsalate |
| **Propionic acid** | **ibuprofen**, fenoprofen, flurbiprofen, ketoprofen, **naproxen**, oxaprozin |
| **Acetic acid** | **diclofenac**, etodolac, **indomethacin**, **ketorolac**, nabumetone, sulindac, tolmetin |
| **Enolic acid** | **meloxicam**, **piroxicam** |
| **Fenamates** | **mefenamic acid**, meclofenamate |
| **Selective COX-2 inhibitor** | **celecoxib** |

- Main mechanism: inhibition of **cyclooxygenase**, which catalyses the first step of prostanoid synthesis → ↓prostaglandins → both benefits and side effects.
- Differences in **safety and efficacy** come from **relative selectivity for COX-1 or COX-2**.

> 💡 **Memory aid:** "**Pro**pionic = ibu**pro**fen, na**pro**xen". "**Acetic** = diclofenac, indomethacin, ketorolac".

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Salicylates: aspirin, diflunisal, salsalate. Propionic: ibuprofen, naproxen… Acetic: diclofenac, indomethacin, ketorolac… Enolic: meloxicam, piroxicam. Fenamates: mefenamic acid. Selective COX-2: celecoxib.
- Differences in safety/efficacy come from relative COX-1 vs COX-2 selectivity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify NSAIDs with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Salicylic acid derivatives (aspirin, diflunisal, salsalate); propionic acid derivatives (ibuprofen, naproxen, ketoprofen…); acetic acid derivatives (diclofenac, indomethacin, ketorolac…); enolic acid (meloxicam, piroxicam); fenamates (mefenamic acid, meclofenamate); selective COX-2 inhibitor (celecoxib).

**Why:** A classic classification question — give at least two examples per class.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the general mechanism of action of NSAIDs?</summary>

**Answer:** They inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes that catalyse the first step of prostanoid biosynthesis, decreasing prostaglandin synthesis, which produces both beneficial and unwanted effects.

**Why:** The same mechanism explains both benefit and harm.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why do NSAIDs differ in safety and efficacy?</summary>

**Answer:** Because of their relative selectivity for the COX-1 or COX-2 enzyme.

**Why:** A one-line answer that still gets asked.
</details>

## 3. Aspirin: mechanism and the three therapeutic actions

- **Aspirin** is the traditional NSAID. Its **anti-inflammatory** effect appears only at **high doses** that are rarely used; it's used much more at **low doses** to prevent **stroke** and **MI**.
- ⭐ **Key difference**: aspirin inhibits COX **irreversibly**; the other NSAIDs are **reversible** inhibitors.
- Aspirin is a **weak organic acid** that **irreversibly acetylates** cyclooxygenase.

### The three actions (not all NSAIDs are equally effective at each)
1. **Anti-inflammatory**: ↓prostaglandins. In arthritis, NSAIDs reduce inflammation **but neither arrest disease progression nor induce remission**.
2. **Analgesic**: **PGE2** sensitizes nerve endings to bradykinin and histamine; lowering it reduces pain. Because **COX-2** is expressed during inflammation, **COX-2 inhibition is responsible for the analgesic effect**. All NSAIDs have **equivalent analgesic efficacy**, for **mild–moderate** pain (**somatic**, musculoskeletal). **Ketorolac** can be used for more severe pain but **only short-term**.
3. **Antipyretic**: fever = a raised **set-point** in the **anterior hypothalamus**, caused by **PGE2** produced in response to **pyrogens (cytokines)** from activated WBCs (infection, hypersensitivity, malignancy, inflammation). NSAIDs lower temperature quickly by blocking PGE2 and increasing heat loss (**peripheral vasodilation + sweating**). ⭐ **They have no effect on normal body temperature**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX; other NSAIDs are reversible.
- Low-dose aspirin for CV prevention; anti-inflammatory only at high doses.
- Anti-inflammatory: ↓PGs, but no arrest of arthritis progression or remission.
- Analgesic: ↓PGE2 (sensitizes nerves to bradykinin/histamine); COX-2 inhibition; equal efficacy; mild–moderate somatic pain; ketorolac for severe pain, short term.
- Antipyretic: ↓PGE2 in hypothalamus + vasodilation/sweating; no effect on normal temperature.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does aspirin differ from other NSAIDs in its mechanism?</summary>

**Answer:** Aspirin is a weak organic acid that irreversibly acetylates and inactivates cyclooxygenase, whereas the other NSAIDs are reversible COX inhibitors.

**Why:** "Irreversible acetylation" is the golden phrase.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the analgesic action of NSAIDs.</summary>

**Answer:** PGE2 sensitizes nerve endings to bradykinin, histamine and other mediators released during inflammation; NSAIDs decrease PGE2 synthesis and so reduce pain. As COX-2 is expressed during inflammation and injury, COX-2 inhibition is responsible for the analgesic activity. All NSAIDs have equivalent analgesic efficacy for mild–moderate somatic pain; ketorolac can be used for more severe pain for a short duration.

**Why:** Mechanism + COX-2 + clinical use.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain the antipyretic action of NSAIDs.</summary>

**Answer:** Fever occurs when the set-point of the anterior hypothalamic thermoregulatory centre is raised by PGE2, whose synthesis is stimulated by pyrogens (cytokines) released from activated WBCs (infection, hypersensitivity, malignancy, inflammation). NSAIDs lower temperature by impeding PGE2 synthesis and increasing heat dissipation through peripheral vasodilation and sweating; they have no effect on normal body temperature.

**Why:** Don't forget "no effect on normal body temperature".
</details>

## 4. Therapeutic uses

### Anti-inflammatory & analgesic
- **Osteoarthritis, gout, RA**, plus headache, arthralgia, myalgia and **dysmenorrhea**.
- **With opioids** for cancer pain → **opioid-sparing effect** (lower opioid doses).
- **Salicylates** are analgesic at low doses and **anti-inflammatory only at high doses**.

### Antipyretic
- **Aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen**.
- ⚠️ **Avoid aspirin** in patients **under 12 years** with **viral infections** (e.g. **varicella / chickenpox** or **influenza**) → risk of **Reye's syndrome** = **fulminating hepatitis with cerebral edema**, often fatal.

### Cardiovascular
- Aspirin inhibits **COX-1 → TXA2** → less vasoconstriction and **platelet aggregation**.
- The effect lasts **for the life of the platelet**; chronic use keeps inhibiting new platelets.
- **Low dose (75–162 mg, commonly 81 mg)** to prevent recurrent CV events, **TIAs**, stroke and death in patients with previous MI/TIA/stroke.
- Given acutely in **acute MI** and some revascularization procedures.

### External (topical)
| Drug | Use |
|---|---|
| **Salicylic acid** | acne, corns, calluses, warts |
| **Methyl salicylate** | **counterirritant** in liniments (arthritis creams, sports rubs) |
| **Diclofenac** (gel/solution) | osteoarthritis of knees or hands |
| **Ketorolac** (ocular) | seasonal allergic conjunctivitis; pain/inflammation after eye surgery |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Uses: OA, gout, RA, headache, dysmenorrhea; with opioids → opioid-sparing effect.
- Antipyretics: aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen; no aspirin under 12 with viral infection (Reye's syndrome).
- Low-dose aspirin (75–162 mg, usually 81 mg): inhibits COX-1/TXA2 for the platelet's life; secondary prevention of MI, TIA, stroke.
- Topical: salicylic acid (acne, warts), methyl salicylate (counterirritant), diclofenac (OA knees/hands), ocular ketorolac.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is aspirin contraindicated in children with viral infections?</summary>

**Answer:** In children under 12 with viral infections such as varicella (chickenpox) or influenza, aspirin can cause Reye's syndrome — fulminating hepatitis with cerebral edema, often leading to death. Acetaminophen is used instead.

**Why:** One of the most repeated pharmacology questions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the cardioprotective effect of low-dose aspirin.</summary>

**Answer:** Aspirin irreversibly inhibits COX-1–mediated production of TXA2, reducing vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation; the effect persists for the life of the platelet and chronic use inhibits new platelets. Low doses (75–162 mg, commonly 81 mg) are used to prevent recurrent cardiovascular events, TIAs, stroke and death in patients with previous MI, TIA or stroke.

**Why:** Mechanism + dose + indication.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the opioid-sparing effect?</summary>

**Answer:** Combining NSAIDs with opioids for pain caused by malignancy is effective and allows lower doses of opioids to be used.

**Why:** A short direct definition.
</details>

## 5. Pharmacokinetics (aspirin & NSAIDs)

- Aspirin is rapidly **deacetylated** by **esterases** to **salicylate**.
- **Unionized salicylates** are absorbed passively, mainly from the **upper small intestine**.
- Salicylates (**except diflunisal**) cross the **BBB** and **placenta**, and are absorbed through intact skin (especially **methyl salicylate**).
- The liver converts salicylate to water-soluble conjugates cleared by the kidney → **first-order elimination**, **t½ = 3.5 h**.
- ⭐ At anti-inflammatory doses (**> 4 g/day**) the hepatic pathway **saturates** → **zero-order kinetics** → **t½ ≥ 15 h**.
- Salicylate affects **uric acid excretion** → avoid in **gout** or with **probenecid**.
- **Alkalinization of urine** increases salicylate excretion (**ion trapping**).
- Other NSAIDs: well absorbed, **highly protein-bound**, hepatic metabolism to inactive metabolites — except **nabumetone** and **sulindac** (active metabolites); excreted mainly in urine.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** First-order (t½ 3.5 h) at low doses → **zero-order** (t½ ≥ 15 h) above 4 g/day. A favourite question.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Aspirin → salicylate (esterases); absorbed from upper small intestine; crosses BBB & placenta (except diflunisal); skin absorption (methyl salicylate).
- First-order, t½ 3.5 h; > 4 g/day → saturation → zero-order, t½ ≥ 15 h.
- Affects uric acid excretion — avoid in gout/probenecid; urine alkalinization ↑excretion (ion trapping).
- Other NSAIDs: highly protein bound, hepatic metabolism; nabumetone & sulindac have active metabolites.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the elimination kinetics of salicylates at low and high doses.</summary>

**Answer:** At usual doses, salicylate is conjugated in the liver and cleared by the kidney with first-order elimination and a half-life of 3.5 hours. At anti-inflammatory doses (more than 4 g/day) the hepatic pathway becomes saturated and zero-order kinetics occur, with a half-life of 15 hours or more.

**Why:** The three numbers (3.5 h, 4 g, 15 h) are what's wanted.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why should aspirin be avoided in gout?</summary>

**Answer:** Salicylate is secreted into the urine and can affect uric acid excretion, so it should be avoided in gout, if possible, and in patients taking probenecid.

**Why:** The link between salicylate and uric acid.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does urinary alkalinization affect salicylate excretion?</summary>

**Answer:** Alkalinization of urine increases the rate of excretion of salicylate and its water-soluble conjugates by ion trapping, which is also used to reduce toxicity.

**Why:** The same principle returns in toxicity management.
</details>

## 6. Adverse effects

Rule: use the **lowest effective dose for the shortest possible duration**.

### Gastrointestinal (⭐ most common)
- From **dyspepsia** to **bleeding**.
- Normally **PGI2** inhibits gastric acid, and **PGE2 + PGF2α** stimulate protective mucus.
- **COX-1** inhibition → ↑acid, ↓mucus, ↑**GI bleeding and ulceration**.
- The higher the COX-1 selectivity, the higher the risk.
- Take **with food**; in high-risk patients give **PPIs** or **misoprostol**.

### Bleeding (antiplatelet)
- Aspirin blocks TXA2 **for the platelet's lifetime (3–7 days)** → **prolonged bleeding time** → stop it **at least 1 week before surgery**.
- Other NSAIDs can also prolong bleeding time, especially with **anticoagulants**.
- ⚠️ NSAIDs can **stop aspirin binding** to COX → patients on cardioprotective aspirin should avoid NSAIDs or take aspirin **30 minutes before** the NSAID.

### Renal
- Block **PGE2 and PGI2**, which maintain **renal blood flow** → **Na⁺ and water retention**, **edema**.
- Higher risk in **heart failure** and **kidney disease**; reduced effect of antihypertensives; possible **acute kidney injury**.

### Cardiac
- Low-dose aspirin is **COX-1 selective** → protective (↓TXA2).
- **COX-2-selective** agents → **↑MI and stroke** by ↓**PGI2**.
- In cardiovascular disease, **naproxen** is the least likely to be harmful.

### Others
- **Asthma**: NSAIDs inhibit PGs **but not leukotrienes** → **shift toward leukotrienes** → asthma exacerbation.
- **CNS**: headache, **tinnitus**, dizziness.
- **Hypersensitivity**: ~**15%** of aspirin users (urticaria, bronchoconstriction, angioedema) → should avoid all NSAIDs.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- GI (most common): COX-1 inhibition → ↑acid, ↓mucus (PGI2, PGE2, PGF2α) → ulcers/bleeding; give with food; PPI or misoprostol if high risk.
- Aspirin: antiplatelet for platelet lifetime (3–7 days) → stop 1 week before surgery; take aspirin 30 min before other NSAIDs.
- Renal: ↓PGE2/PGI2 → Na⁺/water retention, edema, AKI; risk in HF/CKD.
- Cardiac: COX-2 selective → ↑MI/stroke (↓PGI2); naproxen least harmful.
- Asthma (shift to leukotrienes), CNS (tinnitus), hypersensitivity 15%.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the GI adverse effects of NSAIDs and how to prevent them.</summary>

**Answer:** Normally PGI2 inhibits gastric acid secretion and PGE2 and PGF2α stimulate protective mucus. COX-1 inhibition reduces these prostaglandins, increasing acid, diminishing mucus and raising the risk of GI bleeding and ulceration (higher with greater COX-1 selectivity). NSAIDs should be taken with food; in high-risk patients a proton pump inhibitor or misoprostol is given.

**Why:** Mechanism + prevention = the complete answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why must NSAIDs be used with caution in asthma?</summary>

**Answer:** NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis but not leukotrienes, so arachidonic acid metabolism shifts toward leukotriene production, increasing the risk of asthma exacerbations.

**Why:** "Shift toward leukotrienes" is the key phrase.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the renal and cardiac adverse effects of NSAIDs.</summary>

**Answer:** Renal: NSAIDs prevent synthesis of PGE2 and PGI2 that maintain renal blood flow, causing sodium and water retention, edema, reduced effect of antihypertensives and acute kidney injury in susceptible patients (heart failure, kidney disease). Cardiac: COX-2-selective agents increase MI and stroke risk by decreasing PGI2; low-dose aspirin is cardioprotective; naproxen is least likely to harm patients with cardiovascular disease.

**Why:** Two parts in one question — write one paragraph each.
</details>

## 7. Drug interactions, toxicity & pregnancy

### Drug interactions
- Salicylate is **80–90% protein-bound (albumin)** — it can be displaced, raising its concentration.
- It can also displace other drugs such as **warfarin, phenytoin, valproic acid** → higher free levels.

### Toxicity
- **Mild = salicylism**: nausea, vomiting, headache, **marked hyperventilation**, confusion, dizziness, **tinnitus**.
- **Severe**: restlessness, delirium, hallucinations, convulsions, coma, **respiratory and metabolic acidosis**, death from **respiratory failure**.
- Children are especially prone — **10 g** of aspirin can be fatal.
- **Management**: **activated charcoal**; correct electrolytes, **hypoglycemia** and acid–base balance; **benzodiazepines** for seizures; **vitamin K** for bleeding; cooling for hyperthermia; increase excretion by **urine alkalinization + forced diuresis** and **hemodialysis**.

### Pregnancy
- Use only if benefits outweigh risks. **Acetaminophen is preferred**.
- ⭐ **Third trimester**: avoid NSAIDs because of **premature closure of the ductus arteriosus**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Salicylate 80–90% albumin-bound; displaces warfarin, phenytoin, valproic acid.
- Salicylism: tinnitus, hyperventilation, nausea, confusion. Severe: acidosis, convulsions, coma, respiratory failure; 10 g can kill a child.
- Treatment: charcoal, correct electrolytes/glucose/acid–base, benzodiazepines, vitamin K, cooling, urine alkalinization + forced diuresis, hemodialysis.
- Pregnancy: acetaminophen preferred; avoid NSAIDs in the third trimester (ductus arteriosus closure).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe salicylate toxicity and its management.</summary>

**Answer:** Mild toxicity (salicylism): nausea, vomiting, headache, marked hyperventilation, confusion, dizziness and tinnitus. Severe toxicity: restlessness, delirium, hallucinations, convulsions, coma, respiratory and metabolic acidosis and death from respiratory failure; children are especially prone (10 g can be fatal). Management: activated charcoal, correction of electrolytes, hypoglycemia and acid–base balance, benzodiazepines for seizures, vitamin K for bleeding, cooling, and enhanced excretion by urine alkalinization with forced diuresis or hemodialysis.

**Why:** A long, near-certain question — structure it mild → severe → treatment.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why are NSAIDs avoided in the third trimester of pregnancy?</summary>

**Answer:** Because of the risk of premature closure of the ductus arteriosus; acetaminophen is preferred when analgesia or antipyresis is needed in pregnancy.

**Why:** "Ductus arteriosus" is the required word.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain the protein-binding interactions of aspirin.</summary>

**Answer:** Salicylate is 80–90% bound to albumin and can be displaced, increasing its concentration; alternatively aspirin can displace other highly protein-bound drugs such as warfarin, phenytoin or valproic acid, raising their free concentrations.

**Why:** Displacement works in both directions.
</details>

## 8. Celecoxib & acetaminophen

### Celecoxib (selective COX-2 inhibitor)
- **Reversible**, much more selective for COX-2.
- **Uses**: RA, OA, acute pain — pain efficacy similar to other NSAIDs.
- **PK**: metabolized in the liver by **CYP2C9**, **t½ ≈ 11 h** (once or twice daily); reduce dose in moderate hepatic impairment; avoid in severe hepatic or renal disease.
- **Adverse effects**: headache, dyspepsia, diarrhea, abdominal pain.
- **Less GI bleeding** than other NSAIDs, but **the benefit is lost when aspirin is added**. Patients at high ulcer risk who need aspirin should avoid celecoxib.
- Patients with **anaphylactoid reactions** to aspirin/NSAIDs may react to it too.
- **CYP2C9 inhibitors** (e.g. **fluconazole**) raise its levels.

### Acetaminophen (paracetamol)
- Inhibits prostaglandin synthesis **in the CNS** → **antipyretic + analgesic**.
- Weak effect on peripheral COX → **weak anti-inflammatory** activity.
- **No effect on platelets** or bleeding time. ⭐ **Not considered an NSAID**.
- **Uses**: fever and pain; patients with gastric risk; ⭐ **the analgesic/antipyretic of choice for children with viral infections or chickenpox**.
- **PK**: significant first-pass metabolism; liver forms inactive **glucuronide / sulfate** conjugates. A portion becomes **NAPQI** (toxic) — at normal doses it is detoxified by **glutathione**.
- **Toxicity**: in overdose glutathione is depleted → NAPQI binds hepatic proteins → **hepatic necrosis**. Higher risk with liver disease, viral hepatitis, **alcoholism**.
- ⭐ **Antidote: N-acetylcysteine**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Acetaminophen is **not an NSAID**, has little anti-inflammatory action and doesn't affect platelets. NAPQI toxicity = glutathione depletion.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Celecoxib: selective reversible COX-2 inhibitor; RA, OA, acute pain; CYP2C9, t½ ≈ 11 h; less GI bleeding (lost if aspirin added); fluconazole ↑levels.
- Acetaminophen: central PG inhibition → antipyretic + analgesic, weak anti-inflammatory, no platelet effect, not an NSAID; drug of choice in children with viral infections.
- Overdose: glutathione depletion → NAPQI → hepatic necrosis; antidote N-acetylcysteine.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the mechanism of acetaminophen hepatotoxicity and its antidote.</summary>

**Answer:** Acetaminophen is mainly conjugated in the liver to inactive glucuronide or sulfate metabolites; a portion is hydroxylated to NAPQI, a highly reactive metabolite that is normally detoxified by glutathione. With large doses, glutathione is depleted and NAPQI reacts with sulfhydryl groups of hepatic proteins, causing hepatic necrosis. N-acetylcysteine is the antidote.

**Why:** NAPQI + glutathione + N-acetylcysteine — three keywords.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is acetaminophen not considered an NSAID?</summary>

**Answer:** It inhibits prostaglandin synthesis mainly in the CNS (antipyretic and analgesic) but has little effect on peripheral cyclooxygenase, so it has only weak anti-inflammatory activity; it also does not affect platelet function or bleeding time.

**Why:** Central vs peripheral action is the reason.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the advantages and limitations of celecoxib?</summary>

**Answer:** Celecoxib is a selective, reversible COX-2 inhibitor used for RA, osteoarthritis and acute pain, with efficacy similar to other NSAIDs and less GI bleeding and dyspepsia. However, this GI benefit is lost when aspirin is added; patients with aspirin/NSAID anaphylactoid reactions may react to it; it is metabolized by CYP2C9 (fluconazole raises its levels) and should be avoided in severe hepatic or renal disease.

**Why:** Balance the benefits against the limitations.
</details>
