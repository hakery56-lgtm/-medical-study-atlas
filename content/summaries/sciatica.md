## 1. Definitions and applied anatomy

- ⭐ **Sciatica:** **pain radiating from the back or buttock into the posterior thigh and leg, usually below the knee;** a **symptom complex, not a single disease,** usually from **compression or chemical irritation of a lumbosacral nerve root.** **90% of people have low back pain at some time; 30% of those develop leg pain.**
- **Lumbar radiculopathy** = radicular pain **plus objective neurological deficit.** **Referred pain** = **non-dermatomal** pain from spinal structures. **Neurogenic claudication** = **bilateral** leg symptoms worse with **walking or extension** (**spinal stenosis**).
- **Anatomy:** **L1-L5 then sacrum;** discs = **nucleus pulposus + annulus fibrosus;** **cord ends at L1-L2;** **cauda equina** in the lumbar cistern below the **conus medullaris;** **sciatic nerve = L4-S3 roots,** through the **greater sciatic foramen (usually below piriformis),** divides into **tibial and common peroneal nerves** in the posterior thigh.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sciatica = radicular pain below the knee; radiculopathy = pain + deficit.
- Sciatic nerve L4-S3; cord ends L1-L2.

</div>

## 2. Causes and pathophysiology

- ⭐ **Commonest cause: lumbar disc herniation, mainly L4-L5 and L5-S1.** Others: **lumbar spinal stenosis, degenerative spondylolisthesis, foraminal stenosis, instability.**
- **Why lower levels:** **~75% of lumbar flexion-extension at the lumbosacral junction, 20% at L4/5, 5% upper levels → 90% of prolapses at the lower two levels (L5/S1 most frequent).**
- **Less common:** **tumour, infection (spondylodiscitis, epidural abscess, TB), trauma, epidural haematoma (after anticoagulation or spinal procedure), piriformis/deep gluteal syndrome, sacroiliac and hip disease, peripheral neuropathy.**
- ⭐ **Pathophysiology:** **disc degeneration and annular tears → nucleus herniation → mechanical compression + chemical inflammation + venous congestion and ischaemia; central sensitization in persistent pain.** **Posterolateral herniation compresses the next lower root** (L4-L5 → L5; L5-S1 → S1).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Disc herniation L4-L5 and L5-S1 (most at L5/S1).
- Posterolateral herniation hits the next lower root.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** Lumbar disc herniation (commonest, at L4-L5 and L5-S1), spinal stenosis, degenerative spondylolisthesis, foraminal stenosis and instability.

**Why:** Slide "Common causes".
</details>

## 3. Clinical presentation and root syndromes

- **Presentation:** **unilateral leg pain below the knee; sharp, burning, shooting or electric; leg pain predominates; worse with flexion, lifting, coughing, sneezing, straining; dermatomal numbness; weakness (foot drop, great toe, plantar flexion); sphincter loss in severe cases.**

| Root | Disc level | Sensory | Motor | Reflex |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **L4** | **L3-L4** | **Anteromedial leg, medial foot** | **Quadriceps weakness** | **Knee jerk reduced** |
| **L5** | **L4-L5** | **Lateral leg, dorsum of foot, great toe** | **Weak dorsiflexion/great-toe extension; foot drop** | **Usually no change** |
| **S1** | **L5-S1** | **Posterior thigh, calf, lateral foot and sole** | **Weak plantar flexion (toe walking)** | **Ankle jerk reduced** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- L4: knee jerk; L5: foot drop, no reflex change; S1: ankle jerk.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical presentation of sciatica.</summary>

**Answer:** Unilateral sharp, burning or shooting leg pain radiating below the knee, worse with flexion, lifting, coughing or straining, with dermatomal numbness, possible weakness (foot drop) and, in severe cases, sphincter loss.

**Why:** Slide "Clinical presentation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare L5 and S1 root syndromes.</summary>

**Answer:** L5 (L4-L5 disc): lateral leg, dorsum of foot and great toe sensory loss, weak dorsiflexion/foot drop, usually no reflex change; S1 (L5-S1 disc): posterior thigh, calf, lateral foot and sole, weak plantar flexion, reduced ankle jerk.

**Why:** Slide "Common root syndromes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the findings of the L4 syndrome?</summary>

**Answer:** Disc level L3-L4, anteromedial leg and medial foot sensory loss, quadriceps weakness and reduced knee jerk.

**Why:** Slide "Common root syndromes".
</details>

## 4. Examination and provocative tests

- **Examination:** **inspect gait, posture, alignment, scars; palpate midline tenderness, spasm, step deformity; lumbar movements; power, dermatomal sensation, knee and ankle reflexes; compare both limbs; pulses if vascular disease suspected.**

| Test | Key point |
|---|---|
| **Straight-leg raise (Lasègue)** | **Reproduces radicular pain; L5 and S1** |
| **Crossed SLR** | **Less sensitive, more specific for disc herniation** |
| **Bragard** | **Ankle dorsiflexion during SLR increases root tension** |
| **Femoral nerve stretch (reverse SLR)** | **Prone, knee bent, hip extended; anterior thigh pain = upper lumbar (L2-L4) root** |
| **Slump** | **Seated neural tension test** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- SLR: L5/S1; femoral stretch: L2-L4.
- Crossed SLR: specific, not sensitive.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the provocative tests and their uses.</summary>

**Answer:** SLR (L5 and S1), crossed SLR (more specific for disc herniation), Bragard (dorsiflexion adds root tension), femoral nerve stretch (upper lumbar roots) and slump (seated neural tension).

**Why:** Slide "Provocative tests".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is the femoral nerve stretch test done?</summary>

**Answer:** Patient prone; the examiner bends the knee and extends the hip; positive if pain or tingling occurs in the front of the thigh, suggesting L2-L4 root irritation.

**Why:** Slide image "Femoral nerve stretch test".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does the examination include?</summary>

**Answer:** Inspection of gait, posture and alignment, palpation, lumbar movements, power, sensation, reflexes, comparing both limbs and pulses when vascular disease is suspected.

**Why:** Slide "Physical examination".
</details>

## 5. Red flags and cauda equina syndrome

- ⭐ **Red flags:** **saddle anaesthesia/reduced perianal sensation; urinary retention or incontinence; faecal incontinence/loss of anal tone; bilateral leg weakness or rapidly progressive deficit; progressive weakness incl. foot drop; fever, rigors, immunosuppression, IV drug use, recent spinal procedure; malignancy history, weight loss, night pain, significant trauma.**
- ⭐ **Cauda equina syndrome:** **compression of lumbosacral roots below the conus medullaris; neurosurgical emergency; saddle anaesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, bilateral radiculopathy or weakness; urgent MRI and immediate neurosurgical referral; delayed decompression → permanent disability.**
- **Differential:** **mechanical low back pain; stenosis with neurogenic claudication; piriformis/deep gluteal syndrome; sacroiliac dysfunction; hip osteoarthritis or trochanteric pain; PAD (vascular claudication); peripheral neuropathy; infection, tumour, fracture, inflammatory disease.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Saddle anaesthesia or urinary retention = cauda equina until proven otherwise.
- Urgent MRI and neurosurgery.

</div>

**Why:** Slide "Red flags".

## 6. Investigations and management

- **Investigations:** **uncomplicated sciatica needs no early imaging (most improve in 6-12 weeks);** **MRI = modality of choice; urgent MRI for cauda equina, progressive deficit, infection, tumour, fracture;** **X-ray limited (fracture, deformity, instability);** **CT for bone or if MRI contraindicated;** **blood tests if infection, malignancy or inflammation suspected.**
- **Conservative:** **reassurance; avoid prolonged bed rest; activity within pain limits; posture and lifting education; physiotherapy; heat, gentle mobilization.**
- **Drugs:** **paracetamol and NSAIDs first-line; short-term opioids cautiously; gabapentinoids or tricyclics in selected patients; short-term muscle relaxants; corticosteroids not routine.**
- ⭐ **Interventional/surgical:** **epidural steroid injection (short-term relief).** **Surgery** for **persistent disabling radicular pain after 6-12 weeks of conservative care with matching imaging, earlier for significant/progressive deficit; urgent for cauda equina.** **Microdiscectomy = standard for disc herniation;** **laminectomy/decompression for central or lateral recess stenosis;** **fusion for instability or spondylolisthesis.** **Surgery gives faster early relief but long-term outcomes may be similar.**
- **Prognosis:** **most improve within 6-12 weeks; reassess persistent symptoms; follow-up pain, mobility, neurology, bladder/bowel.**
- **Sample case:** **38-year-old, left buttock pain to posterior calf and lateral foot after lifting, worse with coughing, ↓ S1 sensation, weak plantar flexion, absent ankle jerk, SLR positive at 35° → left S1 radiculopathy from L5-S1 herniation; conservative care unless red flags.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- No early imaging for uncomplicated sciatica; MRI if needed.
- Paracetamol/NSAIDs first; steroids not routine.
- Microdiscectomy after 6-12 weeks failure; emergency for cauda equina.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. When is imaging indicated and which modality?</summary>

**Answer:** Not for uncomplicated sciatica; MRI is the modality of choice and is urgent in cauda equina, progressive deficit, infection, tumour or fracture; X-ray has limited use and CT is for bone or when MRI is contraindicated.

**Why:** Slide "Investigations".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Outline conservative and pharmacological management.</summary>

**Answer:** Reassurance, avoiding bed rest, staying active, education and physiotherapy; paracetamol and NSAIDs first-line, short-term opioids cautiously, gabapentinoids or tricyclics selectively, short-term muscle relaxants, no routine corticosteroids.

**Why:** Slides "Conservative management" and "Pharmacological management".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When and how is surgery done?</summary>

**Answer:** For persistent disabling radicular pain after 6-12 weeks of conservative care with concordant imaging, earlier for significant or progressive deficit, urgently for cauda equina; microdiscectomy is standard for disc herniation, decompression for stenosis, fusion for instability.

**Why:** Slides "Interventional and surgical management".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Radiculopathy = pain + deficit; sciatica = symptom, not disease.**
- **L5 syndrome: usually NO reflex change; S1: ankle jerk reduced; L4: knee jerk reduced.**
- **Crossed SLR: less sensitive, MORE specific.**
- **Corticosteroids are NOT routine.**
- **Saddle anaesthesia = cauda equina → urgent MRI + surgery.**
