## 1. Overview and glial cells

- **Neuron = basic unit**; **~100 billion neurons**; **glia ≈ 10–50× neurons**; **~40% of human genes** contribute to CNS formation.
- **Glia** (Greek "glue"): structural, chemical, anatomical support; can influence communication.
- **CNS glia:** **astrocytes** (most abundant; support, nutrient/waste exchange, guide migration, BBB via **end-feet inducing tight junctions**), **microglia** (migrate to injury, become **macrophages**, scavengers), **ependymal cells** (line cavities, **aid CSF movement**), **oligodendrocytes** (**myelinate multiple CNS axons**).
- **PNS glia:** **Schwann cells** (myelin + **neurilemma**, aid regeneration, **one axon each**) and **satellite cells** (surround ganglion cell bodies, ≈ astrocytes).
- **Key difference:** **oligodendrocytes** myelinate **multiple axons, no neurilemma, secrete inhibitory proteins → arrest regeneration**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- CNS glia: astrocytes, microglia, ependymal, oligodendrocytes.
- Schwann = PNS, one axon, regeneration.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name the four CNS glial cells and their roles.</summary>

**Answer:** Astrocytes (support and BBB), microglia (scavenger macrophages), ependymal cells (CSF movement) and oligodendrocytes (CNS myelin).

**Why:** Slides 2–3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare Schwann cells with oligodendrocytes.</summary>

**Answer:** Schwann cells myelinate one axon and form a neurilemma that aids regeneration; oligodendrocytes myelinate several axons, lack a neurilemma and secrete proteins that inhibit regeneration.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What do satellite cells do?</summary>

**Answer:** They surround cell bodies in PNS ganglia and share many functions with astrocytes.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

## 2. Neuron architecture

- **Types:** **unipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar, multipolar.**
- **Soma (perikaryon):** nucleus, **metabolic center**.
- **Dendrites:** usually **< 2 mm**, arborize like antennae; **input zones**; conduct **toward** the soma.
- **Axon:** single, up to **1 m**; conducts **away** from the soma; arises from the **axon hillock**; **initial segment** = site of propagated AP generation; **collaterals**; **myelin** (Schwann/oligodendrocyte) = insulation; **nodes of Ranvier** → **saltatory conduction**; **terminals (boutons)** store transmitter vesicles.
- **Functional zones:** **(1) receptor/dendritic zone — integrates local potentials; (2) AP generation site — initial segment (spinal motor neurons), initial node of Ranvier (cutaneous sensory neurons); (3) axonal process — conducts; (4) nerve endings — AP causes transmitter release.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Dendrites in, axon out.
- AP generation at initial segment.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structural parts of a neuron.</summary>

**Answer:** Soma with nucleus, dendrites (input), axon from the axon hillock with initial segment, collaterals, myelin, nodes of Ranvier and terminal boutons.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the four functional zones.</summary>

**Answer:** Receptor/dendritic zone, AP generation site, axonal process and nerve endings.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What do nodes of Ranvier do?</summary>

**Answer:** They are gaps in the myelin sheath that allow saltatory conduction.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

## 3. Nerve-fiber classification

| Type | Myelin | Diameter | Conduction | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Aα** | **Myelinated** | **Largest** | **Fastest** | **Somatic motor, proprioception** |
| **Aβ** | **Myelinated** | **Medium–large** | **Fast** | **Fine touch, pressure** |
| **Aγ** | **Myelinated** | **Medium** | **Moderate** | **Motor to muscle spindles** |
| **Aδ** | **Myelinated** | **Small–medium** | **Moderate–fast** | **Acute pain, cold, crude touch** |
| **B** | **Myelinated** | **Small** | **Moderate–slow** | **Preganglionic autonomic** |
| **C** | **Unmyelinated** | **Smallest** | **Slowest** | **Chronic pain, heat, postganglionic sympathetic** |

- **Velocity ↑ with diameter and myelin.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Aα fastest; C slowest.
- Aδ acute pain; C chronic pain.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What determines conduction velocity?</summary>

**Answer:** Larger diameter and the presence of an insulating myelin sheath increase velocity.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the functions of Aα, Aδ and C fibers.</summary>

**Answer:** Aα: somatic motor and proprioception; Aδ: acute pain, cold and crude touch; C: chronic pain, heat and postganglionic sympathetic.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which fibers are unmyelinated?</summary>

**Answer:** C fibers, the smallest and slowest.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

## 4. Blood–brain barrier and kernicterus case

- **BBB:** **astrocytic end-feet induce tight junctions in capillary endothelial cells** → prevents passive entry of blood-borne chemicals, toxins and large molecules.
- **Newborn:** **BBB incompletely developed** → brain vulnerable.
- **Case:** **3-day-old full-term neonate**, jaundice, lethargy, poor feeding; **unconjugated bilirubin 24 mg/dL**; **hypertonia, retrocollis, high-pitched cry** → **unconjugated bilirubin crosses the immature BBB into the basal ganglia → kernicterus (permanent neurological damage).**
- **Take-home:** glia are active partners; **BBB immaturity matters**; **fiber size dictates speed** (large myelinated Aα fast; small unmyelinated C slow chronic pain).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Immature BBB → bilirubin enters basal ganglia.
- Kernicterus = permanent damage.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does the BBB work?</summary>

**Answer:** Astrocytic end-feet induce tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells, blocking passive entry of toxins and large molecules.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does the neonate in the case develop brain injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Unconjugated bilirubin (24 mg/dL) crosses the immature BBB into the basal ganglia, causing kernicterus.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the long-term outcome if untreated?</summary>

**Answer:** Permanent neurological damage.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Oligodendrocyte (CNS) myelinates MANY axons; Schwann cell (PNS) myelinates ONE.**
- **Aδ = acute (fast) pain; C = chronic (slow) pain.**
- **Satellite cell ≈ astrocyte (PNS vs CNS).**
