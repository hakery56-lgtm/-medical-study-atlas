# Shoulder Dislocation (Ortho 1) — Exam Night Review

A short lecture where almost every line is an exam point: anatomy, the three types, examination, imaging, associated injuries, treatment, complications, and a case to finish. After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

## 1. Anatomy — why does the shoulder dislocate so easily?

- The **glenohumeral joint**: the **humeral head** articulates with the **glenoid**.
- **Static stabilizers**: **labrum**, **capsule**, **glenohumeral ligaments**.
- **Dynamic stabilizers**: **rotator cuff** and **scapular muscles**.
- ⭐ A **large humeral head with a relatively shallow glenoid** allows extensive motion — at the cost of stability.

In short: the shoulder trades security for freedom — the more it moves, the less stable it is.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Glenohumeral joint = humeral head + glenoid.
- Static stabilizers: labrum, capsule, glenohumeral ligaments. Dynamic: rotator cuff + scapular muscles.
- Large head + shallow glenoid → extensive motion but instability.

</div>

**Answer:** Static: glenoid labrum, joint capsule and glenohumeral ligaments. Dynamic: rotator cuff and scapular muscles.

**Why:** The static/dynamic split is what's asked.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the shoulder joint prone to dislocation?</summary>

**Answer:** A large humeral head articulates with a relatively shallow glenoid, allowing extensive motion at the expense of stability.

**Why:** The mobility–stability trade-off.
</details>

## 2. Classification & types

| Type | Frequency | Mechanism | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior** | ⭐ **most common — 95%** | **abduction + external rotation**; fall, sports, direct trauma | humeral head moves **anterior/inferior** to the glenoid |
| **Posterior** | uncommon, ⭐ **frequently missed** | **seizures**, **electrical shock**, significant trauma | arm held in **adduction + internal rotation**; **AP X-ray may be misleading** → need an **axillary or scapular-Y view** |
| **Inferior (luxatio erecta)** | **rare** | **forced hyperabduction** | **high risk of neurovascular and soft-tissue injury** |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Posterior = **seizure / electric shock** + arm in **adduction & internal rotation** + missed on the AP view. This triad is asked a lot.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Anterior 95%: abduction + external rotation; head anterior/inferior.
- Posterior: seizures, electric shock; arm adducted + internally rotated; missed on AP — need axillary/scapular-Y view.
- Inferior (luxatio erecta): forced hyperabduction; high neurovascular risk.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify shoulder dislocations with their mechanisms.</summary>

**Answer:** Anterior (most common, ~95%): abduction plus external rotation from falls, sports or direct trauma; the head moves anterior/inferior to the glenoid. Posterior (uncommon, frequently missed): seizures, electrical shock or significant trauma. Inferior (luxatio erecta, rare): forced hyperabduction, with high risk of neurovascular and soft-tissue injury.

**Why:** The three types with frequency and mechanism.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is posterior dislocation frequently missed and how is it diagnosed?</summary>

**Answer:** It is uncommon and the AP radiograph may be misleading; the arm is held in adduction and internal rotation. An axillary or scapular-Y view is important to identify it.

**Why:** Naming the correct view earns the mark.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is luxatio erecta and why is it dangerous?</summary>

**Answer:** Inferior dislocation of the shoulder, usually from forced hyperabduction; it carries a high risk of associated neurovascular and soft-tissue injury.

**Why:** Name + mechanism + danger.
</details>

## 3. Clinical presentation & neurovascular examination

### Presentation
- **Severe shoulder pain** and **inability to move the arm**.
- **Visible deformity** — ⭐ **flattening of the lateral shoulder** (loss of the normal rounded contour).
- The patient often **supports the affected arm**.

### Neurovascular examination — ⭐ **document before and after reduction**
- **Axillary nerve**: **sensation over the lateral shoulder** (regimental badge area) + **deltoid function**.
- **Radial pulse** and **distal perfusion**.
- Motor and sensory function of the whole limb when appropriate.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** "What must be documented before reduction?" → **neurovascular status, especially the axillary nerve (lateral shoulder sensation + deltoid) and the radial pulse**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Severe pain, inability to move, flattened lateral shoulder, patient supports the arm.
- Document neurovascular status BEFORE and AFTER reduction.
- Axillary nerve: lateral shoulder sensation + deltoid; radial pulse & distal perfusion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical presentation of shoulder dislocation.</summary>

**Answer:** Severe shoulder pain with inability to move the arm, visible deformity with flattening of the lateral shoulder, and the patient often supports the affected arm.

**Why:** Three main signs.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is the axillary nerve assessed in a dislocated shoulder?</summary>

**Answer:** By testing sensation over the lateral shoulder and deltoid function, documented before and after reduction.

**Why:** The axillary nerve is the most commonly injured nerve.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What neurovascular examination is required before and after reduction?</summary>

**Answer:** Axillary nerve (lateral shoulder sensation and deltoid function), radial pulse and distal perfusion, and motor and sensory function of the entire limb when appropriate — all documented before and after reduction.

**Why:** "Document before and after" is essential.
</details>

## 4. Imaging & associated injuries

### Imaging
- **X-ray**: **AP plus axillary or scapular-Y views** improve characterization.
- **CT**: **complex fracture/dislocation**.
- **MRI**: selected **soft-tissue/labral** or **rotator-cuff** assessment.

### Associated injuries
| Lesion | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Bankart lesion** | **labral injury** (anteroinferior labrum) |
| **Hill-Sachs lesion** | **humeral-head impaction** (posterolateral head) |
| **Greater tuberosity fracture** | |
| **Rotator-cuff injury** | ⭐ **especially in older patients** |
| **Axillary nerve or vascular injury** | |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** **Bankart = labrum** (glenoid side), **Hill-Sachs = humeral head** (impaction). Don't swap them.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- X-ray AP + axillary/scapular-Y; CT for complex fracture-dislocation; MRI for labrum/cuff.
- Bankart = labral injury; Hill-Sachs = humeral head impaction; greater tuberosity fracture; cuff tear in older patients; axillary nerve/vascular injury.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the injuries associated with shoulder dislocation.</summary>

**Answer:** Bankart lesion (labral injury), Hill-Sachs lesion (humeral head impaction), greater tuberosity fracture, rotator-cuff injury (especially in older patients) and axillary nerve or vascular injury.

**Why:** Five injuries from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate Bankart and Hill-Sachs lesions.</summary>

**Answer:** A Bankart lesion is an injury (avulsion) of the glenoid labrum; a Hill-Sachs lesion is an impaction fracture of the humeral head produced as it strikes the glenoid rim during dislocation.

**Why:** One is on the glenoid side, the other on the humerus.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the role of each imaging modality in shoulder dislocation?</summary>

**Answer:** X-ray (AP plus axillary or scapular-Y views) is the initial study; CT for complex fracture-dislocation; MRI for selected soft-tissue, labral or rotator-cuff assessment.

**Why:** Each modality and when to use it.
</details>

## 5. Management, surgery & complications

### Initial management
- **General assessment + appropriate analgesia** first.
- Then **closed reduction** — the lecture shows **Kocher's method** (briefly: **external rotation, then adduction of the elbow, then internal rotation** of the arm).

### Surgical indications
1. **Recurrent instability**
2. **Significant bone loss**
3. **Selected Bankart/labral injuries**
4. **Fracture-dislocation requiring fixation**
5. **Failure of appropriate nonoperative management**

### Complications
- **Recurrent dislocation/instability**
- **Axillary nerve injury**
- **Fracture or fracture-dislocation**
- **Rotator-cuff injury**
- **Post-traumatic stiffness**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- First: general assessment + analgesia; then reduction (Kocher's method).
- Surgery: recurrent instability, significant bone loss, selected Bankart lesions, fracture-dislocation needing fixation, failed conservative care.
- Complications: recurrence, axillary nerve injury, fracture, cuff injury, stiffness.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the indications for surgery in shoulder dislocation.</summary>

**Answer:** Recurrent instability, significant bone loss, selected Bankart/labral injuries, fracture-dislocation requiring fixation, and failure of appropriate nonoperative management.

**Why:** Five direct points.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the complications of shoulder dislocation?</summary>

**Answer:** Recurrent dislocation/instability, axillary nerve injury, fracture or fracture-dislocation, rotator-cuff injury and post-traumatic stiffness.

**Why:** The lecture's complication list.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Outline the initial management of an acute shoulder dislocation.</summary>

**Answer:** General assessment and appropriate analgesia, documentation of neurovascular status, imaging (AP plus axillary/scapular-Y), then closed reduction (e.g. Kocher's method) with repeat neurovascular examination afterwards.

**Why:** Brings all the steps together in order.
</details>

## 6. Case discussion (from the lecture)

**Case:** A young athlete falls during a contact sport with the arm **abducted and externally rotated**. Examination: **painful deformity** and **limited movement**.

1. **Likely direction?** → **Anterior dislocation** (the classic position + the most common type, 95%).
2. **Views needed?** → **AP + axillary or scapular-Y view**.
3. **What must be documented before reduction?** → **neurovascular status**: axillary nerve (lateral shoulder sensation + deltoid), radial pulse, distal perfusion.
4. **Associated lesions?** → **Bankart**, **Hill-Sachs**, **greater tuberosity fracture**, rotator-cuff injury, axillary nerve/vascular injury.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Abduction + external rotation injury in a young athlete → anterior dislocation.
- Views: AP + axillary/scapular-Y. Document neurovascular status before reduction.
- Consider Bankart, Hill-Sachs, greater tuberosity fracture, cuff and nerve injury.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. A young athlete falls with the arm abducted and externally rotated. What is the likely diagnosis and which views do you request?</summary>

**Answer:** Anterior shoulder dislocation; request an AP view plus an axillary or scapular-Y view.

**Why:** The same case as in the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. In the same patient, what must be documented before reduction?</summary>

**Answer:** Neurovascular status — axillary nerve function (lateral shoulder sensation and deltoid), radial pulse and distal perfusion, and limb motor/sensory function.

**Why:** The most important step before any reduction.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which associated lesions should be considered in this case?</summary>

**Answer:** Bankart lesion, Hill-Sachs lesion, greater tuberosity fracture, rotator-cuff injury and axillary nerve or vascular injury.

**Why:** The case's fourth question.
</details>
