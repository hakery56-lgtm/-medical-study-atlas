## 1. Goals of data analysis

| | **Descriptive statistics** | **Inferential statistics** |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | **Describe (summarize) what was observed in the sample** | **Use sample patterns to draw inferences about the population** |
| **Tools** | **Mean, SD, range, percentiles (median)** for continuous variables; **raw and relative frequencies** for categorical variables; **tables and graphs** | **Confidence intervals; hypothesis tests and p-values; correlation and associations; regression** (relationships, effects, predictions) |

- ⭐ **Three types of descriptive statistics:** **distribution** (frequency of each value), **central tendency** (averages), **variability/dispersion** (spread).
- **First step:** organize the data in a **frequency distribution** = organization into **exclusive intervals** so the **number or percent** in each is apparent (tables or graphs).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Descriptive = summarize the sample; inferential = generalize to the population.
- Three descriptive types: distribution, central tendency, variability.
- Frequency distribution = exclusive intervals with counts or percentages.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Distinguish descriptive from inferential statistics.</summary>

**Answer:** Descriptive statistics summarize what was observed in the sample (means, SD, ranges, percentiles, frequencies, tables, graphs); inferential statistics use sample patterns to draw inferences about the population (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, correlation, regression).

**Why:** Slide "Goals of data analysis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name the three types of descriptive statistics.</summary>

**Answer:** Distribution (frequency of each value), central tendency (averages) and variability or dispersion (spread).

**Why:** Slide "Types of descriptive statistics".

<details><summary>Q3. What is a frequency distribution?</summary>

**Answer:** Organization of a data set into exclusive intervals so that the number or percentage of observations in each category or interval is apparent.

## 2. Guidelines and frequency tables

- ⭐ **General guidelines:** **meaningful title** (what, how classified, where and when); **state the number of subjects**; **label rows/columns or axes**; **state units** (e.g. mmHg); **refer to it in the text.** Each table/graph must be **interpretable without the text.** "Chart" = any pictorial display without table cells or graph points.
- **Frequency table:** **two columns** (values/class intervals and frequencies).

| Type | Use and features |
|---|---|
| **Ungrouped (simple)** | **Categorical variables**; count of each value; add percentages. Ordinal: order small→large; nominal: any order |
| **Grouped** | **Quantitative data** in **ordered, non-overlapping intervals** |
| **Relative** | **Proportion (percent)** of each value; for comparing frequencies |
| **Cumulative** | **Sum of frequencies ≤ each value**; for **ordinal or quantitative** data |

- **Table 1:** male **46 (52.3%)**, female **42 (47.7%)**, total **88**.
- **Table 2 (80 transplant referrals):** < 10: 2; 10-19: 5; 20-29: 6; 30-39: 14; **40-49: 21 (26.25%)**; 50-59: 20; ≥ 60: 12.
- **Table 3 (income, 425 households):** frequencies 92, 126, 113, 94; cumulative **92, 218, 331, 425**; cumulative % **21.6, 51.3, 77.9, 100.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Title, N, labels, units, text reference.
- Ungrouped = categorical; grouped = numerical (no overlap); cumulative = running total.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the general guidelines for tables and graphs.</summary>

**Answer:** Meaningful title, state the number of subjects, label rows and columns or axes, state units and refer to the table or graph in the text.

**Why:** Slide "General guidelines".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare ungrouped, grouped, relative and cumulative frequency distributions.</summary>

**Answer:** Ungrouped: counts per value for categorical variables; grouped: counts in non-overlapping intervals for numerical data; relative: proportion of each value; cumulative: running total up to each value for ordinal or quantitative data.

**Why:** Slides "Tables".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the cumulative frequencies of the household income table.</summary>

**Answer:** 92, 218, 331 and 425 (21.6%, 51.3%, 77.9% and 100%).

**Why:** Table 3.
</details>

## 3. Graphs (1): tables versus graphs, line graph, bar chart, histogram

- **Tables:** **precision and flexibility** with complex data, **less technical skill**, **less space.** **Graphs:** **simplicity and clarity, memorable images, show complex relationships.**
- **Line graph:** **change in a continuous variable** (y-axis), often over time; **avoid missing zeros and stretched scales.**
- **Bar chart:** **categorical data**, bars proportional to values; compares categories (e.g. male vs female smokers); vertical = **column chart.**
- **Histogram:** **continuous variable**, **contiguous intervals**, **no gaps**; heights = number or percent.
- ⭐ **Histogram vs bar chart:** histogram = **continuous, no gaps**; bar chart = **discrete/categorical, spaces.**
- **Frequency polygon:** line joining the **middle of each histogram bar**; the **normal distribution** is the bell-shaped example.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Line graph: continuous change over time; bar chart: categories; histogram: continuous, no gaps.
- Frequency polygon joins histogram bar midpoints.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. When do you use a line graph and what should be avoided?</summary>

**Answer:** To show change or difference in a continuous variable (often over time); avoid missing zeros and stretched scales that exaggerate relationships.

**Why:** Slide "Line graphs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Distinguish a histogram from a bar chart.</summary>

**Answer:** A histogram shows a continuous variable with no gaps between bars; a bar chart shows a discrete or categorical variable with spaces between bars.

**Why:** Slide "What is the distinction between a histogram and a bar chart?".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a frequency polygon?</summary>

**Answer:** A line that connects the middle of each bar of the histogram; the bell-shaped normal distribution is an important example.

**Why:** Slide "Frequency polygons".
</details>

## 4. Graphs (2): pie chart, maps, scatter plot, stem and leaf

- **Pie chart:** how a **whole is divided into parts**; order slices **by size from twelve o'clock clockwise.**
- **Spot map:** **cases as points** (John Snow, cholera, **Golden Square, London 1854**); **rate map:** areas **shaded by prevalence, incidence or mortality**, highest = **darkest/brightest.**
- **Scatter plot:** **relationship between two continuous variables** (x and y axes).
- **Stem and leaf plot:** order and display data; **abbreviate to two digits; digit left of decimal = stem, right = leaf.** Steps: **write stems → write leaves as they come → order the leaves.**
- **Example (urinary lead in 15 children):** ordered plot **0 | 1 4 6 8; 1 | 1 2 3 5 7 9 9; 2 | 0 2 6; 3 | 2**; **range 0.1 to 3.2 µmol/24 h** (or 3.1 as a single number). Ordering shows **gaps, humps, irregularities and the range.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pie = parts of a whole; spot map = case points; rate map = shaded rates; scatter = two continuous variables.
- Stem = left of the decimal; leaf = right; then order the leaves.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Distinguish a spot map from a rate map.</summary>

**Answer:** A spot map plots cases as points (John Snow's cholera map); a rate map shades areas according to rates such as prevalence, incidence or mortality.

**Why:** Slides "Spot map and rate map".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. When is a scatter plot used?</summary>

**Answer:** To plot the relationship between two continuous variables, one on each axis.

**Why:** Slide "Scatter plot".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain how to construct a stem and leaf plot.</summary>

**Answer:** Abbreviate values to two digits, write the stems in order, write the leaves as they come, then order the leaves; it shows gaps, humps and the range.

**Why:** Slides "Stem and leaf plots".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Histogram = no gaps (continuous); bar chart = gaps (categorical).**
- **Inferential statistics = p-values and confidence intervals; descriptive = mean, SD, frequencies.**
- **Grouped intervals must NOT overlap.**
- **Cumulative frequency suits ordinal or quantitative variables.**
- **Avoid missing zeros and stretched scales in line graphs.**
