## 1. Functions and types of WBCs

- ⭐ **Functions:** **destroy invading bacteria/viruses by phagocytosis**; **form antibodies or sensitized lymphocytes** that destroy or inactivate the invader.
- **Total count:** 5000-10,000/µL (summary table).

| Cell | Share of WBCs | Features | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neutrophil** | 50-70% | Multi-lobed nucleus, pale granules | **Phagocytize pathogens** |
| **Eosinophil** | 1-4% | Bi-lobed nucleus, **red (acidic)** granules | **Fight parasites, allergy** |
| **Basophil** | 0-1% | **Blue (alkaline)** granules; least common | **Release histamine** |
| **Monocyte** | 2-8% | Large cytoplasm, **kidney/U-shaped nucleus**, no granules | **Become macrophages** |
| **Lymphocyte** | 20-40% | Thin cytoplasm, **round nucleus**, no granules | **B and T cells** |

- **Granulated:** neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils. **Agranulated:** monocytes, lymphocytes.
- **Note:** the summary table on slide 17 gives slightly different ranges (neutrophils 60-70%, eosinophils 2-4%, monocytes 3-8%, lymphocytes 20-25%); the percentages above follow slides 3-4 and no question is built on the differences.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five WBC types: neutrophils (most), lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils.
- Eosinophil = red granules; basophil = blue granules.

</div>

## 2. Genesis and life span

- ⭐ **Granulocytes and monocytes** = **bone marrow.** **Lymphocytes and plasma cells** = partly marrow, **mostly lymph glands, spleen, thymus, tonsils** (stem cells from marrow).
- **Granulopoiesis:** **stimulated by granulopoietin (from macrophages)**; **inhibited by prostaglandin E** and substances from mature neutrophils.
- **Needs** a **vitamin complex, folic acid, amino acids**; formed granulocytes are **stored in the marrow.**

| Cell | Life span |
|---|---|
| **Granulocytes** | **4-8 hours** in blood, then **4-5 days** in tissue |
| **Monocytes** | A few hours in blood, then **months or years** in tissue |
| **Lymphocytes** | **100-300 days, up to years** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Granulopoietin stimulates; PGE inhibits.
- Granulocytes live only hours in blood; lymphocytes live the longest.

</div>

**Why:** Slide "Genesis of leukocytes".

## 3. Defense by neutrophils and macrophages

- ⭐ **Steps:** **chemotaxis → margination → diapedesis → amoeboid motion (up to 40 µ/min) → phagocytosis.**
- **Chemotaxis stimuli:** bacterial toxins, tissue breakdown products, complement and clotting products, leukotrienes, lymph polypeptide.
- **Phagocytosis:** attach to pathogen → pseudopodia fuse into a **phagosome** → **proteolytic enzyme digestion**; macrophages also have **lipases** (TB); undigested bacteria are killed by **superoxide and H2O2.**
- **Macrophage extra roles:** **antigen presenting cell** (stimulates antibody production) and **sensitizes T lymphocytes.**
- **Capacity:** **macrophage up to 100 bacteria**; **neutrophil up to 20**, starts immediately.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Order: chemotaxis, margination, diapedesis, amoeboid motion, phagocytosis.
- Macrophage = APC with lipases; neutrophil = immediate, 20 bacteria.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanism of destruction of bacteria.</summary>

**Answer:** Chemotaxis toward the infection, margination on the capillary endothelium, diapedesis through the wall, amoeboid motion in the tissue and phagocytosis with enzymatic digestion or oxidative killing.

**Why:** Slides "Mechanism of Defense of Neutrophils and Monocytes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe phagocytosis.</summary>

**Answer:** The neutrophil attaches to the pathogen, pseudopodia fuse into a phagosome, proteolytic enzymes digest it (macrophages add lipases for TB) and undigested bacteria are killed by superoxide and hydrogen peroxide.

**Why:** Slide "Phagocytosis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare macrophages and neutrophils.</summary>

**Answer:** Macrophages (activated monocytes) are stronger, engulf up to 100 bacteria plus dead neutrophils, tissue debris, malarial parasites and RBCs, and present antigens; neutrophils are mature, work immediately and engulf up to 20 bacteria before dying.

**Why:** Slides on macrophage functions.
</details>

## 4. Monocyte-macrophage system

- ⭐ **Reticuloendothelial system** = **monocytes, mobile macrophages, fixed tissue macrophages** and a few specialized endothelial cells (marrow, spleen, lymph nodes).

| Organ | Macrophage |
|---|---|
| **Skin** | **Histiocytes** |
| **Spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow** | **Tissue macrophages** |
| **Liver** | **Kupffer cells** |
| **Lung** | **Alveolar cells** |
| **Brain** | **Microglial cells** |

- **Roles:** skin infection → histiocytes multiply; if not controlled, bacteria drain to lymphatics, spleen or marrow; **GIT infection → Kupffer cells filter portal blood**; **lung infection → alveolar cells.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Histiocyte skin; Kupffer liver; alveolar lung; microglia brain.
- Failed local defense drains to lymphatics.

</div>

## 5. Inflammation and the four lines of defense

- ⭐ **Inflammation** = local tissue changes from released **histamine, serotonin, prostaglandin, clotting and complement products, lymphokines**, after injury, trauma, infection, heat or chemicals.
- **Response:** **redness** (vasodilation); **edema** (permeability); **clotting of interstitial fluid = walling-off effect** (leaked fibrinogen); **migration of granulocytes and monocytes**; **swelling of cells.**

| Line | Cells and timing |
|---|---|
| **1st** | **Tissue macrophages** within minutes |
| **2nd** | **Neutrophil invasion** within the first hour (IL-1 and tissue products); **neutrophilia up to 5×** within hours (granulocyte releasing factor mobilizes marrow stores) |
| **3rd** | **Second macrophage invasion**: monocytes mature in **8-12 hours** |
| **4th** | **Marrow production of granulocytes and monocytes** rises; **3-4 days** to leave the marrow |

- **Other neutrophilia:** **tissue destruction** (operation, cancer, coronary thrombosis); **physiological** in exercise (**2-3×**, norepinephrine, gone within **1 hour**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Macrophages (minutes) → neutrophils (1 h) → macrophages (8-12 h) → marrow output (3-4 days).
- Walling-off = fibrinogen clotting.
- Exercise neutrophilia is norepinephrine-mediated.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the components of the inflammatory response.</summary>

**Answer:** Redness, edema, clotting of interstitial fluid (walling-off effect), migration of granulocytes and monocytes, and swelling of tissue cells.

**Why:** Slide "The inflammation response includes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the four lines of defense in inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** Tissue macrophages within minutes; neutrophil invasion within the first hour (with neutrophilia up to 5×); a second macrophage invasion (8-12 h maturation); and increased marrow production of granulocytes and monocytes (3-4 days).

**Why:** Slides "Response of neutrophils and monocytes to inflammation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the causes of neutrophilia?</summary>

**Answer:** Uncontrolled infection (granulocyte releasing factor), tissue destruction without infection and physiological neutrophilia in exercise due to norepinephrine.

**Why:** Slides "Other causes of Neutrophilia".
</details>

## 6. Eosinophils and basophils

- ⭐ **Eosinophils:** **weak phagocytes**, chemotactic effect. **Eosinophilia** in **parasitic infestation (schistosomiasis)** and **allergy (skin allergy, asthma).**
- **Parasites:** too large to phagocytize, so eosinophils **attach and release killing substances.**
- **Allergy:** stimulated by **eosinophil chemotactic factor from mast cells and basophils**; they **detoxify histamine** and **phagocytize allergen-antibody complexes.**
- **IgE** attaches to **mast cells and basophils**; Ag-Ab reaction → cell rupture → release of inflammatory substances → allergic manifestations.
- **Basophils:** like **mast cells**; release **heparin** (prevents coagulation) and **histamine, bradykinin, serotonin, lysosomal enzymes.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Eosinophilia: parasites and allergy.
- IgE binds mast cells and basophils.
- Basophils release heparin and histamine.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What causes eosinophilia?</summary>

**Answer:** Parasitic infestations such as schistosomiasis and allergic reactions such as skin allergy and asthma.

**Why:** Slides "Causes of Eosinophilia".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do eosinophils act in allergy?</summary>

**Answer:** They detoxify histamine and other inflammation-inducing substances and phagocytize allergen-antibody complexes, limiting local inflammation.

**Why:** Slide "Causes of Eosinophilia".

## 7. WBC abnormalities: leukopenia and leukemia

- ⭐ **Leukopenia:** marrow produces few WBCs → **neutropenia** → multiple infections (skin, GIT mucosa, respiratory), possibly lethal; **ulcers 2 days** after marrow stoppage, **death within about 6 days.** **Aplasia causes:** **X-ray or gamma irradiation, drugs (chloramphenicol).**
- **Leukemia:** uncontrolled WBC production from **cancerous mutation of myelogenous or lymphogenous cells.**
- **Lymphogenous:** cancer of lymphoid cells stored in nodes. **Myelogenous:** cancer of young myeloid cells in the marrow.
- **Partially differentiated** cells (neutrophilic, eosinophilic, basophilic, monocytic): **chronic, 10-20 years.** **Undifferentiated:** **acute, death within months.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Aplasia → infections → death in about 6 days.
- Chronic leukemia is slow (10-20 years); undifferentiated is acute.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the consequences of leukopenia?</summary>

**Answer:** Neutropenia with multiple infections (skin, GIT mucosa, respiratory); after marrow stoppage, ulcers appear in 2 days and death occurs within about 6 days.

**Why:** Slide "WBC abnormalities".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What causes bone marrow aplasia?</summary>

**Answer:** X-ray or gamma-ray irradiation and drugs such as chloramphenicol.

**Why:** Slide "WBC abnormalities".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare chronic and acute leukemia.</summary>

**Answer:** Partially differentiated cells give chronic leukemia developing over 10-20 years; undifferentiated cells give acute leukemia with death within months.

**Why:** Slide "Leukemia's".
</details>
