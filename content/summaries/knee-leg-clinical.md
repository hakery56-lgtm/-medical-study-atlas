## 1. Menisci and collateral ligaments

- Knee injuries are common (sport, accidents, degenerative conditions); the two significant types involve **ligaments** (collateral: **MCL, LCL**; cruciate: **ACL, PCL**) and **menisci**.

| | **Medial meniscus** | **Lateral meniscus** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Nearly **semicircular** | Nearly **circular** |
| Attachment | Posterior fibres of the anterior end continue with the **transverse ligament**; **more fixed** (deep part of the tibial collateral ligament + capsule) | Posterior end attached to the femur by **two meniscofemoral ligaments**; **more mobile** (popliteus tendon separates it from capsule and fibular collateral ligament) |

- **Functions of the menisci:** increase the **surface area** of the tibial articular surface, increase **congruence**, increase **stability**, **shock absorber**, **distribute the axial load → decrease stress** (stress = load / area).
- ⭐ **Why is lateral meniscus injury less common than medial?** It is **more mobile**: **not attached to the lateral collateral ligament**; **popliteus sends a few fibres into it**; **attached to the femur by meniscofemoral ligaments** — so it can be pulled out of harm's way during knee movements.

| | **MCL** | **LCL** |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Strong, **flat, wide, triangular** | Strong **rounded cord** |
| Attachment | Medial epicondyle → medial tibial condyle and upper medial surface (behind S.G.S); **firmly attached to capsule and medial meniscus** | Lateral epicondyle → head of fibula; between splits of the biceps tendon; **not adherent to capsule or meniscus** |
| Limits | **Abduction** (torn in violent abduction) | **Adduction** (torn in violent adduction) |

Both are **tense in extension** and give side-to-side stability.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Medial meniscus: semicircular, fixed to the MCL and capsule; lateral: circular, mobile, so injured less.
- Meniscus functions: bigger contact area, congruence, stability, shock absorption, load distribution.
- MCL limits abduction (attached to the meniscus); LCL limits adduction (free).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the lateral meniscus injured less often than the medial?</summary>

**Answer:** It is more mobile: it is not attached to the lateral collateral ligament, popliteus sends fibres into it and the meniscofemoral ligaments attach it to the femur, so it can be pulled out of harm's way during knee movements; the medial meniscus is fixed to the MCL and capsule.

**Why:** Slide "Q/ lateral meniscus injury is less than medial?".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the functions of the menisci.</summary>

**Answer:** Increase the articular surface area of the tibia, increase congruence and stability, act as shock absorbers, and distribute the axial load so that stress (load/area) decreases.

**Why:** Slide "Functions of the menisci".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate the medial and lateral collateral ligaments.</summary>

**Answer:** MCL: flat, wide, triangular, firmly attached to the capsule and medial meniscus, limits abduction. LCL: rounded cord, not adherent to capsule or meniscus, passes between splits of the biceps tendon, limits adduction. Both tense in extension.

## 2. Cruciate ligaments, unhappy triad and clinical tests

| | **ACL** ("**APEX**" ligament) | **PCL** ("**PAIN**" ligament) |
|---|---|---|
| Tibial attachment | **Anterior** intercondylar area, behind the anterior horn of the medial meniscus | **Most posterior** intercondylar area, behind the posterior horn of the medial meniscus |
| Course | Upwards, **backwards, laterally** to the lateral condyle (arises **A**nterior tibia → **P**osteriorly and **EX**ternally) | Upwards, **forwards, medially** to the medial condyle (arises **P**osterior tibia → **A**nteriorly and **IN**ternally) |
| Stretched in | **Extension** | **Flexion** |
| Strength | **Weaker** | **Stronger** |
| Prevents | **Posterior** displacement of the femur on the tibia | **Anterior** displacement of the femur on the tibia |
| Key stabilizer | Weight-bearing **extended** knee (ascending stairs) | Weight-bearing **flexed** knee (descending stairs, walking downhill) |

### Unhappy triad (3 Cs)
- Injury to **C**artilage (menisci), **C**ruciate ligament, **C**ollateral ligament.
- ⭐ A **sudden forcible blow on the lateral aspect of the knee** tenses the MCL → tearing of the **MCL, the medial meniscus (firmly attached to the MCL) and the ACL** (the weaker, hyperextension-resisting ligament).

### Clinical features and tests
- **Cruciate injury:** sudden pain, rapid swelling from blood (**hemarthrosis**), unable to walk; diagnosis by **drawer sign, MRI, arthroscopy**. **Drawer test = cruciates** (ACL stops forward tibial movement; PCL stops backward).
- **Meniscus injury:** sudden severe pain, **locking** (detached part enters the joint), audible **click**; **McMurray's test = menisci**.
- **Valgus-varus test = collateral ligaments.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ACL: tight in extension, weaker, "APEX"; PCL: tight in flexion, stronger, "PAIN".
- Unhappy triad = MCL + medial meniscus + ACL after a lateral blow.
- Tests: drawer (cruciates), McMurray (menisci), valgus-varus (collaterals).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the ACL and PCL.</summary>

**Answer:** ACL: anterior intercondylar area, runs up, back and laterally, stretched in extension, weaker, prevents posterior displacement of the femur on the tibia, key stabilizer when ascending stairs. PCL: most posterior intercondylar area, runs up, forwards and medially, stretched in flexion, stronger, prevents anterior displacement of the femur, key stabilizer when descending stairs.

**Why:** Slide "Differences between anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the unhappy triad and how does it occur?</summary>

**Answer:** Injury to cartilage (menisci), cruciate and collateral ligaments. A sudden forcible blow on the lateral side of the knee tenses and tears the MCL, the medial meniscus (firmly attached to the MCL) and the ACL.

**Why:** Slide "Unhappy triad of knee injuries (3Cs)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe cruciate and meniscal injuries and their clinical tests.</summary>

**Answer:** Cruciate: sudden pain, rapid hemarthrosis, inability to walk; confirmed by the drawer sign, MRI and arthroscopy. Meniscus: sudden severe pain with joint locking and a click; McMurray's test. The valgus-varus test examines the collateral ligaments.

**Why:** Slide "Cruciate ligament injury / Meniscus injury".
</details>

## 3. Cartilage, muscle fibres and bursitis

### Cartilage of the knee
- **Two cartilages:** **articular (hyaline)** and **menisci (fibrocartilage)**.
- ⭐ **Avascular → no regeneration.** After excessive trauma, overweight, overuse or malalignment: wear and tear → meniscus loses its shock-absorbing role and degenerates → **bone rubs on bone**, joint space narrows, alignment lost (**varus = bowing in; valgus = bowing out**) → **antalgic gait** (less weight on the painful knee, **shortened stance phase**).
- **Properties:** rubbery, tough and resilient; **no vessels, nerves or lymphatics**; nutrition by **diffusion from the perichondrium**; chondrocytes + fibers + matrix; resists compression and friction; grows by multiplication of chondroblasts.

| Type | Features | Sites | Ossification in old age |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hyaline** | Glass-like, translucent matrix, numerous small rounded cells | Fetal bones, epiphyseal plates, articular cartilage, costal cartilage and xiphoid, larynx (except epiglottis) | **Occurs** (larynx, certain epiphyseal cartilages) |
| **White fibrocartilage** | Opaque matrix rich in collagen bundles, few cartilage cells | **Intervertebral discs, menisci, articular discs** | Does not occur |
| **Yellow elastic** | Yellow (elastic fibers), abundant cartilage cells | Tip of nose, auricle of ear, epiglottis | Does not occur |

### Soleus vs gastrocnemius
| | **Soleus** | **Gastrocnemius** |
|---|---|---|
| Fibre type | **Red, slow-twitch** | **White, fast-twitch** |
| Role | Sustained running; strong **antigravity**; primary plantar flexor in knee flexion; **venous return (more active)** | **Explosive movement**; powerful flexor of the foot; **knee flexor**; propulsion in walking, jumping, running; venous return (less) |

- **White muscle:** less dense capillaries, few mitochondria, poor myoglobin, glycolytic enzymes, fast isoforms. **Red muscle:** dense capillaries, rich myoglobin, abundant mitochondria, oxidative enzymes, slow isoforms.

### Bursitis
- **Communicating bursae (this lecture):** suprapatellar, deep infrapatellar, popliteus and pes anserinus. **Significance:** pathway for **infection** into the joint; fluid (**knee effusion**) can indicate arthritis or injury.
- **Most commonly affected:** **suprapatellar bursitis** (effusion, patella floats = water on the knee); **prepatellar bursitis = housemaid's knee** (repetitive kneeling or trauma); **semimembranosus bursitis = Baker cyst** (commonest swelling in the popliteal fossa).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cartilage is avascular, does not regenerate; loss leads to varus/valgus and antalgic gait.
- Hyaline may ossify; fibrocartilage (menisci, discs) and elastic cartilage do not.
- Soleus = red slow antigravity; gastrocnemius = white fast explosive; bursitis: water on the knee, housemaid's knee, Baker cyst.

</div>

## 4. Nerve injuries

### Common peroneal nerve
- **Roots:** posterior divisions of the anterior rami **L4–S2**. **Motor:** all muscles of the **anterior and lateral compartments**. **Sensory:** sural communicating nerve (joins the sural nerve mid-calf), lateral cutaneous nerve of the calf, areas of superficial and deep peroneal branches; articular branches to the knee.
- **Course:** smaller terminal branch of the sciatic (lower third of the back of the thigh) → back of the fibular head → winds around the **neck of the fibula** → pierces **peroneus longus** → superficial + deep peroneal nerves (the slide heading says "course in pelvis", a slip).
- ⭐ **Cause:** **fracture of the head/neck of the fibula or plaster-cast pressure**; **the most commonly injured nerve of the lower limb** (winds superficially around the fibular neck).
- **Motor deficit:** **foot drop** (paralysis of all dorsiflexors); foot **inverted** (evertors paralysed, tibialis posterior unopposed) → **equinovarus deformity + steppage gait** (foot lifted high and slapped down, toes drag).
- **Sensory deficit:** upper lateral leg; front and lateral lower leg and dorsum of the foot (superficial peroneal); **1st cleft** (deep peroneal).

### Tibial nerve
- **Roots:** anterior divisions of the anterior rami **L4–S3**. **Motor:** all muscles of the **posterior compartment and the sole**; **sensory:** mainly the sole; articular branches to the knee and ankle.
- **Causes:** fracture of the lower femur or trauma in the popliteal fossa; fracture of the tibia or trauma at the back of the leg.
- **Motor deficit:** paralysis of all back-of-leg muscles (loss of plantar flexion, weak inversion, loss of toe flexion) and of the sole muscles → **calcaneovalgus deformity (dorsiflexion + eversion) + calcaneal gait (walks on the heel)**; **cannot walk on tip-toes**.
- **Sensory deficit:** back of the leg and lateral foot (**sural**), sole (**plantar nerves**).

### Sural nerve graft and tarsal tunnel syndrome
- **Sural nerve:** accompanies the **short saphenous vein**; supplies skin of the lower back of the leg, posterolateral leg and lateral foot. **Autologous nerve grafting is the gold standard for a nerve gap; the sural nerve is a valuable graft source.**
- The saphenous nerve (femoral branch) accompanies the **great saphenous vein** and supplies the skin of the medial leg and foot (diagram).
- **Tarsal tunnel syndrome:** **compression of the tibial nerve in the fibro-osseous tunnel under the flexor retinaculum** → **pain and paresthesia in the sole, worse at night**. Contents (medial → lateral): tibialis posterior, FDL, artery-vein-nerve, FHL (the slide prints "EHL" for FHL).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Common peroneal: L4–S2; fibular neck/plaster; foot drop, equinovarus, steppage gait; sensory loss lateral leg, dorsum, 1st cleft.
- Tibial: L4–S3; popliteal/back-of-leg trauma; calcaneovalgus, calcaneal gait, no tip-toes; sensory loss on the sole.
- Sural nerve = graft source; tarsal tunnel = tibial nerve compression, painful sole at night.

</div>

## 5. Fractures, dislocation and Achilles tendon rupture

- ⭐ **Tibia = commonest site of compound (open) fracture** because it is **subcutaneous anteromedially (the shin)** throughout its course: a blow tears the skin and the fragments protrude.
- **Distal third of the tibia:** complication = **delayed union or non-union**. Reason: **no muscle is attached** → poor blood supply, depends mainly on the **nutrient artery**, which enters the foramen **just distal to the soleal line** and is interrupted by the fracture; the shaft is **narrowest at the junction of the middle and distal thirds** (most frequent fracture site).
- **Blood supply of long bones (3 sources):** nutrient artery; anastomosis of vessels near the ends; periosteal arteries from locally attached muscles.
- **Avulsion fracture of the 5th metatarsal:** forcible **eversion** avulses the tuberosity by the attached tendon of **peroneus brevis**.
- **Dislocation of the knee:** injury to the **popliteal artery** and the **common peroneal and tibial nerves**.
- **Achilles tendon rupture:** causes — **spontaneous** (diabetes mellitus, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE) or **trauma** (minor blow in the elderly; sport injury or overstretch in the young; complete or partial tear). Features — a **pop**, immediate sharp pain at the back of the ankle, inability to walk properly, a **gap on palpation**; plantar flexion is **never lost but painful**. Anatomy — **thickest and strongest tendon, 15 cm, no synovial sheath (no tenosynovitis)**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Tibia is subcutaneous, so open fractures are common; distal-third fractures heal slowly (poor supply, nutrient artery).
- 5th metatarsal avulsion = forcible eversion pulling peroneus brevis; knee dislocation threatens the popliteal artery and its nerves.
- Achilles rupture: pop, gap, painful but preserved plantar flexion; no sheath.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

The distal third has no muscle attached, so it has a poor blood supply and depends on its nutrient artery, which enters just below the soleal line and is interrupted by the fracture, causing delayed union or non-union.
