# Scalp and Face (Lecture 2) — Exam Night Review

Dr. Munqith Mazin's second Head & Neck lecture: **the cranial cavity, the five layers of the scalp (and why the 4th is "dangerous"), the nerves and vessels of the scalp, the facial muscles, and the blood supply, venous drainage, lymphatics and nerve supply of the face (with Bell's palsy).** After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

### Objectives
1. Illustrate the anatomical structure of the **cranial cavity**.
2. Describe the anatomy of the **scalp** with its innervation and blood supply.
3. Define the **face** and **facial muscles** with their innervation and blood supply.

---

## 1. The cranial cavity (recap)

- The **space within the calvaria** that contains the **brain, meninges, proximal parts of the cranial nerves, blood vessels and cranial venous sinuses**.
- **Roof (figure):** frontal crest, groove for the **superior sagittal sinus**, groove for the **anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery**, **granular foveolae**, coronal and sagittal sutures, bregma, lambdoid suture, lambda.

| Fossa | Main features |
|---|---|
| **Anterior** | above the **nasal cavity and orbits**; filled by the **frontal lobes**; **frontal bone, lesser wing of sphenoid, foramen cecum, crista galli, cribriform plate** |
| **Middle** | **optic foramen, foramen rotundum, ovale, spinosum, lacerum, sella turcica, dorsum sellae, greater wing of sphenoid, petrous temporal**; boundary with the anterior fossa in the midline = **anterior edge of the chiasmatic sulcus**; posterior boundary = **anterior surface of the petrous and mastoid temporal** |
| **Posterior** | ⭐ **largest and deepest**; contains the **brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla) and cerebellum**; **internal acoustic meatus, jugular foramen, mastoid foramen, foramen magnum, internal occipital crest**; anterior midline boundary = **dorsum sellae and clivus**; posterior = **squamous occipital** |

Other grooves: **trigeminal impression** (trigeminal ganglion) and **arcuate eminence** (superior semicircular canal) on the petrous temporal; **hypoglossal canal** above the occipital condyle (XII); **grooves for the inferior petrosal, sigmoid and transverse sinuses**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cranial cavity = brain + meninges + cranial nerve roots + vessels + venous sinuses.
- Anterior fossa → frontal lobes; middle fossa → boundary at the chiasmatic sulcus; posterior fossa → largest and deepest (brainstem + cerebellum).
- Posterior fossa boundaries: dorsum sellae and clivus (front), squamous occipital (back).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the cranial cavity and state what it contains.</summary>

**Answer:** The cranial cavity is the space within the calvaria; it contains the brain, meninges, proximal parts of the cranial nerves, blood vessels and cranial venous sinuses.

**Why:** Definition + five contents.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What separates the anterior and middle cranial fossae in the midline, and what bounds the middle fossa posteriorly?</summary>

**Answer:** The anterior edge of the chiasmatic sulcus (the smooth groove between the optic canals across the sphenoid body) separates them; posteriorly the middle fossa is bounded by the anterior surface of the petrous and mastoid parts of the temporal bone.

**Why:** Same boundary question as the skull lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which fossa is the largest and deepest and what does it contain?</summary>

**Answer:** The posterior cranial fossa; it contains the brainstem (midbrain, pons and medulla) and the cerebellum, bounded anteriorly by the dorsum sellae and clivus and posteriorly by the squamous part of the occipital bone.

**Why:** Contents + boundaries.
</details>

## 2. The scalp: definition & layers (SCALP)

- **Extends** from the **superciliary arches** anteriorly to the **external occipital protuberance and superior nuchal lines** posteriorly; **laterally** it continues down to the **zygomatic arch**.
- A **multilayered** structure — the word **S·C·A·L·P** gives the layers:

| # | Layer | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ⭐ **S**kin | **thick (3–8 mm), hairy**, many **sebaceous and sweat glands**; connected to the galea by **numerous vertical septa** that **minimize shearing** of the skin on the skull |
| 2 | ⭐ **C**onnective tissue (dense) | **fibro-fatty layer adherent to the skin and aponeurosis** by fibrous septa; contains **dense connective + fatty tissue, hair follicles, veins, arteries, lymphatics and sensory nerves** |
| 3 | ⭐ **A**poneurotic layer (**galea aponeurotica**) | **thin tendinous sheet** uniting the **frontal and occipital bellies of occipitofrontalis**; attached **laterally to the temporal fascia** and to the **SMAS** of the face |
| 4 | ⭐ **L**oose connective tissue | the **"dangerous layer"**, occupies the **subaponeurotic space** |
| 5 | ⭐ **P**ericranium | **deepest layer** = **periosteum** of the outer calvaria |

- **The first three layers (S, C, A)** are **tightly held together as one unit**, **move together** and are the **tissue torn away in serious scalping injuries**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Scalp: superciliary arches → external occipital protuberance/superior nuchal lines; laterally to the zygomatic arch.
- S–C–A–L–P: Skin, dense Connective tissue, Aponeurosis (galea), Loose connective tissue, Pericranium.
- Layers 1–3 form one unit that moves together and is scalped away.
- Skin 3–8 mm thick, hairy, with sebaceous and sweat glands; vertical septa minimize shearing.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the scalp and give its layers.</summary>

**Answer:** The scalp extends from the superciliary arches anteriorly to the external occipital protuberance and superior nuchal lines posteriorly, and laterally to the zygomatic arch. Its layers (SCALP): Skin, dense Connective tissue, Aponeurotic layer (galea aponeurotica), Loose connective tissue and Pericranium.

**Why:** Boundaries + the acronym.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which layers form a single unit, and why is this clinically important?</summary>

**Answer:** The skin, dense connective tissue and aponeurotic layer (S, C, A) are tightly held together and move with the scalp; in serious scalping injuries these three layers are torn away together.

**Why:** "First three layers" is a favourite MCQ.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the skin and the dense connective tissue layers of the scalp.</summary>

**Answer:** The skin is thick (3–8 mm), hairy and rich in sebaceous and sweat glands, and is connected to the galea by many vertical septa that reduce shearing. The connective tissue layer is a fibro-fatty layer adherent to the skin and aponeurosis by fibrous septa; it contains hair follicles, veins, arteries, lymphatics and the sensory nerves of the scalp.

**Why:** Layers 1 and 2 together.
</details>

## 3. Galea, occipitofrontalis, the dangerous layer & pericranium

### Aponeurotic layer & occipitofrontalis
- **Galea aponeurotica** = thin tendinous sheet uniting the **two bellies** of **occipitofrontalis**; attached laterally to the **temporal fascia**, and to the **SMAS**.
- **Frontal belly:** arises from the **anterior part of the aponeurosis**, inserts into the **skin of the eyebrows** (**no bony attachment**), **blends with orbicularis oculi**; ⭐ **elevates the eyebrows (surprised look)** and produces **transverse wrinkles of the forehead**.
- **Occipital belly:** arises from the **lateral ⅔ of the superior nuchal line** of the occipital bone and the **mastoid part of the temporal bone**.

### Loose connective tissue — the **"dangerous layer"**
- Occupies the **subaponeurotic space**; contains **few arteries** and the important **emissary veins**.
- **Easily dissected** → the **plane of cleavage in avulsion ("scalping") injuries**.
- **Dangerous** because **pus or blood spreads easily** in it:
  - infection can pass **into the bones through the diploic veins → osteomyelitis**;
  - through the **emissary veins into the intracranial venous sinuses → venous sinus thrombosis**;
  - fluid/infection spreads **only into the eyelids and root of the nose**, because the **frontalis inserts into skin, not bone**.

### Pericranium
- **Deepest layer** = **periosteum on the outer surface of the calvaria**, but **removable**; **distinctly separate** from the layer above (the innominate fascia).
- **At the sutures** it is **continuous with the periosteum** on the outer surface of the bones.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Galea unites frontal and occipital bellies; attached to temporal fascia and SMAS.
- Frontal belly: skin of eyebrows (no bone), raises eyebrows, wrinkles forehead; occipital belly: lateral ⅔ of superior nuchal line + mastoid.
- 4th layer = dangerous layer: pus/blood spreads easily → osteomyelitis (diploic veins), sinus thrombosis (emissary veins), spreads to eyelids/root of nose only.
- Pericranium = deepest layer, periosteum of the calvaria; continuous with periosteum at the sutures.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the occipitofrontalis muscle.</summary>

**Answer:** It has a frontal belly anteriorly, an occipital belly posteriorly and the galea aponeurotica connecting them. The frontal belly arises from the anterior aponeurosis, inserts into the skin of the eyebrows with no bony attachment, blends with orbicularis oculi and elevates the eyebrows (surprised look) and wrinkles the forehead transversely. The occipital belly arises from the lateral two-thirds of the superior nuchal line and the mastoid part of the temporal bone.

**Why:** Both bellies + actions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the 4th layer of the scalp called the "dangerous layer"?</summary>

**Answer:** It is loose, lies in the subaponeurotic space and contains few arteries and the emissary veins, so pus or blood spreads easily in it. Infection can reach the bones through the diploic veins (osteomyelitis) or the intracranial venous sinuses through the emissary veins (venous sinus thrombosis). It is also the plane of cleavage in scalping injuries.

**Why:** Two routes + the scalping plane.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does infection in the subaponeurotic space spread to the eyelids and root of the nose but not to the temple or neck?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the frontalis is attached into the skin of the eyebrows and not to the bone, so fluid can pass forward into the eyelids and root of the nose; laterally the galea is anchored to the temporal fascia and posteriorly to the occipital bone limiting spread elsewhere.

**Why:** The attachments decide where fluid can go.
</details>

## 4. Innervation & blood supply of the scalp

### Sensory nerves
| Region | Nerves |
|---|---|
| **Anterior to the ears and the vertex** | **supratrochlear**, **supra-orbital**, **zygomaticotemporal**, **auriculotemporal** (all branches of the trigeminal nerve) |
| **Posterior to the ears and the vertex** | **great auricular**, **lesser occipital**, **greater occipital**, **third occipital** (from cervical nerves) |

### Arteries
| Source | Arteries |
|---|---|
| **A. Ophthalmic artery** (internal carotid system) | **supratrochlear**, **supra-orbital** |
| **B. External carotid artery** | **posterior auricular**, **occipital**, **superficial temporal** |

- **Veins** draining the scalp **follow a pattern similar to the arteries**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Nerves in front of the ears/vertex: supratrochlear, supra-orbital, zygomaticotemporal, auriculotemporal; behind: great auricular, lesser occipital, greater occipital, third occipital.
- Arteries: supratrochlear and supra-orbital (ophthalmic); posterior auricular, occipital, superficial temporal (external carotid).
- Veins accompany the arteries.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the sensory nerves of the scalp.</summary>

**Answer:** Anterior to the ears and the vertex: supratrochlear, supra-orbital, zygomaticotemporal and auriculotemporal nerves. Posterior to the ears and the vertex: great auricular, lesser occipital, greater occipital and third occipital nerves.

**Why:** 4 + 4 nerves — a 2-column list.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the arteries supplying the scalp and their sources.</summary>

**Answer:** From the ophthalmic artery: supratrochlear and supra-orbital arteries. From the external carotid artery: posterior auricular, occipital and superficial temporal arteries.

**Why:** Two sources, five arteries.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do the veins of the scalp run?</summary>

**Answer:** The veins draining the scalp follow a pattern similar to that of the arteries, accompanying them.

**Why:** A one-line fact worth a mark.
</details>

## 5. The face & the muscles of facial expression

- **Face** = the region **between the superciliary arches (above), the lower edge of the mandible (below), and the ears (on each side)**.
- Regions (figure): forehead, eyebrow, eyelids, eyes, cheeks, nose, nostrils, lips, jaw, chin.

### Facial muscles
- Lie in the **superficial fascia**, **origin from bone or fascia, insertion into the skin**.
- Control **facial expression** ("muscles of facial expression") and act as **sphincters and dilators of the orifices** (orbits, nose, mouth).
- ⭐ **All supplied by the facial nerve (VII).**

| Group | Muscles |
|---|---|
| **Orbital** (2) | **orbicularis oculi**, **corrugator supercilii** |
| **Nasal** (3) | **nasalis**, **procerus**, **depressor septi nasi** |
| **Oral** | **orbicularis oris**, **buccinator**, plus an **upper and lower group** of muscles that move the lips and cheek |
| **Auricular** (3) | **anterior** (pulls ear **upward and forward**), **superior** (**elevates**), **posterior** (**retracts and elevates**) |
| **Others** | **platysma** (large thin sheet in the superficial fascia of the **neck**); **occipitofrontalis** (frontal + occipital bellies) |

Muscles shown in the figure: frontalis, procerus, corrugator supercilii, depressor supercilii, orbicularis oculi, levator labii superioris (alaeque nasi), zygomaticus minor and major, nasalis, dilator naris, buccinator, orbicularis oris, risorius, depressor anguli oris, depressor labii inferioris, mentalis, platysma and the auricular muscles.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Face: superciliary arches → lower edge of mandible → ears.
- Facial muscles: superficial fascia, skin insertion; sphincters and dilators of orifices; all by the facial nerve.
- Groups: orbital (2), nasal (3), oral (orbicularis oris, buccinator + upper/lower groups), auricular (3), platysma, occipitofrontalis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define the face and the facial muscles.</summary>

**Answer:** The face is the region between the superciliary arches superiorly, the lower edge of the mandible inferiorly and the ears on either side. The facial muscles lie in the superficial fascia, arise from bone or fascia and insert into the skin; they control facial expression, act as sphincters and dilators of the orifices (orbits, nose, mouth), and are all supplied by the facial nerve.

**Why:** Definition + five features.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the groups of facial muscles with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Orbital group — orbicularis oculi and corrugator supercilii; nasal group — nasalis, procerus and depressor septi nasi; oral group — orbicularis oris and buccinator plus upper and lower groups; auricular muscles — anterior, superior and posterior; plus platysma and occipitofrontalis.

**Why:** Number of muscles per group is often asked.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the three auricular muscles.</summary>

**Answer:** The anterior auricular muscle pulls the ear upward and forward; the superior auricular elevates the ear; the posterior auricular retracts and elevates the ear.

**Why:** One action each.
</details>

## 6. Face: blood supply, venous drainage & lymphatics

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Arterial supply** | **primarily from branches of the external carotid artery**, with some limited supply from a branch of the **internal carotid** artery: **facial, transverse facial, maxillary, dorsal nasal** arteries |
| ⭐ **Venous drainage** | **most returns to the internal jugular vein**; important connections result in drainage through a **clinically relevant intracranial pathway involving the cavernous sinus** |
| **Lymphatic drainage** | towards **three groups of nodes**: **submental, submandibular, pre-auricular and parotid** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Arteries: facial, transverse facial, maxillary (external carotid) and dorsal nasal (limited internal carotid supply).
- Veins → internal jugular vein; connection with the cavernous sinus is clinically important (spread of infection).
- Lymph → submental, submandibular, pre-auricular/parotid nodes.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give the arterial supply of the face.</summary>

**Answer:** Mainly branches of the external carotid artery — the facial, transverse facial and maxillary arteries — with a limited supply from a branch of the internal carotid artery, the dorsal nasal artery.

**Why:** External carotid dominates; one internal carotid branch.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the venous drainage of the face and its clinical importance.</summary>

**Answer:** Most venous blood returns to the internal jugular vein, but important connections allow drainage through an intracranial pathway involving the cavernous sinus, so facial infection can spread to the cavernous sinus.

**Why:** Link between face and cranial cavity.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which lymph node groups drain the face?</summary>

**Answer:** The submental nodes, the submandibular nodes and the pre-auricular and parotid nodes.

**Why:** Three groups.
</details>

## 7. Cutaneous innervation of the face & Bell's palsy

### Sensory (skin) — branches of the **trigeminal nerve (V)**
| Division | Cutaneous branches (figure) |
|---|---|
| **Ophthalmic (V1)** | supratrochlear, supra-orbital, lacrimal, infratrochlear, external nasal |
| **Maxillary (V2)** | zygomaticotemporal, zygomaticofacial, infra-orbital |
| **Mandibular (V3)** | auriculotemporal, buccal, mental |

### Motor = **facial nerve (VII)** → **Bell's palsy**
**Injury to the facial nerve or its branches** → **paralysis of some or all facial muscles on the affected side**:
1. **Sad face**
2. **Smooth forehead**
3. **Everted inferior eyelid**
4. **Corneal dehydration and scarring**
5. **Drooping of the mouth**
6. **Impaired speech**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Skin sensation of the face = trigeminal (V1, V2, V3); muscles = facial nerve (VII).
- Bell's palsy = facial nerve injury → sad face, smooth forehead, everted lower eyelid, corneal dryness/scarring, drooping mouth, impaired speech.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the cutaneous nerve supply of the face.</summary>

**Answer:** It is by branches of the trigeminal nerve: ophthalmic (V1; e.g. supratrochlear, supra-orbital), maxillary (V2; e.g. infra-orbital, zygomaticofacial) and mandibular (V3; e.g. auriculotemporal, buccal, mental).

**Why:** Three divisions with examples.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is Bell's palsy and what are its features?</summary>

**Answer:** Injury to the facial nerve or its branches causing paralysis of some or all of the facial muscles on the affected side. Features: sad face, smooth forehead, everted lower eyelid, corneal dehydration and scarring, drooping of the mouth and impaired speech.

**Why:** Six features from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does a patient with Bell's palsy develop corneal damage?</summary>

**Answer:** The paralysed orbicularis oculi cannot close the eyelid and the lower lid is everted, so the cornea is exposed, dries (dehydrates) and can scar.

**Why:** Link the muscle to the complication.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Face muscles → facial nerve (VII); face skin sensation → trigeminal (V)**.
- **Layers 1–3 move together**; **layer 4 is the dangerous layer**; **layer 5 = pericranium**.
- Frontal belly has **no bony attachment** (inserts into eyebrow skin).
- The dangerous layer spreads to **eyelids and root of the nose** (not to the temple).
- **Emissary veins** → venous sinus thrombosis; **diploic veins** → osteomyelitis.
- **Scalp nerves** in front = trigeminal branches; behind = cervical nerves (great auricular, lesser, greater, third occipital).
- Scalp arteries: **supratrochlear + supra-orbital (ophthalmic)**; **posterior auricular, occipital, superficial temporal (external carotid)**.
- Face lymph: **submental, submandibular, pre-auricular/parotid**.

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. **Cranial cavity** = brain + meninges + cranial nerve roots + vessels + sinuses; **posterior fossa** largest and deepest.
2. **Scalp** boundaries: **superciliary arches → external occipital protuberance/superior nuchal lines → zygomatic arch**.
3. **S-C-A-L-P**: skin (3–8 mm), dense connective tissue, galea aponeurotica, loose connective tissue (**dangerous**), pericranium.
4. **Occipitofrontalis**: frontal belly (no bone, raises eyebrows) + galea + occipital belly (superior nuchal line + mastoid).
5. **Dangerous layer** → diploic veins (osteomyelitis), emissary veins (sinus thrombosis), spreads to eyelids/root of nose.
6. **Scalp nerves**: front (supratrochlear, supra-orbital, zygomaticotemporal, auriculotemporal); back (great auricular, lesser, greater, third occipital).
7. **Scalp arteries**: supratrochlear, supra-orbital; posterior auricular, occipital, superficial temporal.
8. **Face**: superciliary arches → mandible → ears; muscles in superficial fascia, **all by VII**.
9. **Groups**: orbital (2), nasal (3), oral, auricular (3), platysma, occipitofrontalis.
10. **Face vessels**: facial, transverse facial, maxillary, dorsal nasal; veins → IJV + cavernous sinus; lymph → submental, submandibular, pre-auricular/parotid.
11. **Skin sensation**: V1, V2, V3; **Bell's palsy** = sad face, smooth forehead, everted lower lid, corneal scarring, drooping mouth, impaired speech.
