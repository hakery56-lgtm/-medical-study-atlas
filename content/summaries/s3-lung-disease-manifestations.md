## 1. Dyspnoea: definition and pathophysiology

- ⭐ **Dyspnoea** = **subjective** sensation of breathlessness that is **excessive for the level of activity** (an uncomfortable need to breathe); **no defined receptors**, **no localised brain representation.**
- **Causes:** disorders of **airways, lung parenchyma, pleura, respiratory muscles or chest wall.**
- **Respiratory disease stimulates breathing by:** **(1)** stimulating **intrapulmonary sensory nerves** (pneumothorax, interstitial inflammation, PE); **(2)** ↑ **mechanical load** on respiratory muscles (airflow obstruction, fibrosis); **(3)** **hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis** → **chemoreceptors** (peripheral and central).
- **Cardiac failure:** **pulmonary congestion** ↓ compliance and obstructs small airways; ↓ cardiac output → muscle hypoxia, **lactic acidaemia** → central chemoreceptor stimulation.

### Differential diagnosis
| System | **Acute dyspnoea at REST** | **Chronic EXERTIONAL dyspnoea** |
|---|---|---|
| **CVS** | Acute pulmonary oedema / LVF; **MI** | **Chronic heart failure** |
| **Respiratory** | Acute asthma, acute COPD, **pneumothorax, pneumonia, PE**, ARDS, inhaled foreign body, laryngeal oedema | **COPD, asthma**, bronchial carcinoma, interstitial lung disease, allergic alveolitis, large pleural effusion, lymphatic carcinoma |
| **Others** | Metabolic acidosis, **psychogenic hyperventilation** | **Severe anaemia, obesity** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Dyspnoea is subjective: no receptors, no cortical map.
- Mechanisms: sensory nerves, mechanical load, chemoreceptors (hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis).
- Acute at rest: LVF, MI, asthma, COPD, pneumothorax, pneumonia, PE.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define dyspnoea and state what is known about its receptors.</summary>

**Answer:** The subjective sensation of breathlessness excessive for any given level of activity (an uncomfortable need to breathe); it has no defined receptors and no localised representation in the brain.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How can respiratory disease stimulate dyspnoea?</summary>

**Answer:** By stimulating intrapulmonary sensory nerves (pneumothorax, interstitial inflammation, PE), by increasing the mechanical load on respiratory muscles (airflow obstruction, fibrosis) and by hypoxia, hypercapnia or acidosis stimulating peripheral and central chemoreceptors.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does cardiac failure cause dyspnoea?</summary>

**Answer:** Pulmonary congestion reduces lung compliance and obstructs small airways; reduced cardiac output limits oxygen supply to skeletal muscles on exercise, producing lactic acidaemia that stimulates breathing via central chemoreceptors.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

## 2. Causes of dyspnoea and the MRC scale

- ⭐ **ACS** = symptoms of acute **myocardial ischaemia**: **UA, NSTEMI, STEMI** (MI = ACS with myocardial injury). Retrosternal discomfort + breathlessness, or **breathlessness alone** (atypical). Risk: **old age, smoking, HTN, hyperlipidaemia, DM**. **ECG + cardiac enzymes**; treat by ↓ O₂ demand and ↑ flow. *ST↑ + troponin/CK-MB↑ = STEMI; ST↓/T inversion + enzymes↑ = NSTEMI; non-specific ECG + normal enzymes = UA.*
- **CCF** = **LVF + RVF together**; **25–50% die within 5 years**; exertional dyspnoea, **orthopnoea, PND**. Decompensation: **salt, non-compliance, ischaemia, dysrhythmia, renal failure, PE, HTN, infection**; treat to **↓ lung congestion.**
- **COPD:** progressive obstruction with **little or no reversibility** (**emphysema / chronic bronchitis**); chronic dyspnoea + productive cough; exacerbation: ↑ dyspnoea and sputum; **rule out pneumothorax**; treat with **anticholinergics, β₂ agonists, steroids ± positive pressure ventilation.**
- **Asthma:** **commonest ER cause** of dyspnoea; **5–8%** of population; dyspnoea, wheeze, chest tightness, **dry cough**, **reversible** obstruction; **short-acting bronchodilators + steroids.**
- **Pneumothorax:** air in the pleural space; **acute pleuritic pain + dyspnoea not improved by O₂**; **absent breath sounds, JVD, tracheal deviation**; treatment depends on size and underlying disease.
- **Pneumonia:** acute lower RTI **with fever**; productive cough, dyspnoea, pleuritic pain; **inspiratory crackles**; **CXR** separates it from CCF; **antibiotics.**
- **PE:** clot in a pulmonary vessel; **acute dyspnoea**, pleuritic pain, cough, haemoptysis, fever; risk: **DVT, surgery, cancer, previous VTE**; always consider (high mortality); **anticoagulants.**
- **Anaemia:** low Hb causes dyspnoea; excessive menstruation contributes. Note: the slide also lists headache, blurred vision and "permanent brain damage"; this is slide wording, not standard teaching, and is not examined here.
- **Others:** **interstitial lung disease** (gradual, environmental exposure), **panic attack** (hyperventilation, sweating, numbness), **anaphylaxis** (minutes, urticaria, throat swelling, GI upset).

### MRC dyspnoea scale
| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **1** | Breathless only on **strenuous exercise** |
| **2** | Short of breath when **hurrying or walking up a slight hill** |
| **3** | **Walks slower** than peers on the level or **stops for breath** at own pace |
| **4** | **Stops after ~100 m** or a few minutes on the level |
| **5** | **Too breathless to leave the house** or when dressing/undressing |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ACS = UA, NSTEMI, STEMI; CCF = LVF + RVF (25–50% 5-year mortality); COPD = little reversibility; asthma = reversible.
- Pneumothorax: pleuritic pain, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation, not improved by O₂.
- PE: acute dyspnoea; DVT/surgery/cancer; anticoagulants. MRC 1–5.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define ACS and list its components and risk factors.</summary>

**Answer:** ACS describes symptoms from acute myocardial ischaemia: unstable angina, NSTEMI and STEMI. Risk factors: old age, smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and diabetes; ECG and cardiac enzymes guide diagnosis and treatment.

**Why:** Slides 8–10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare COPD and asthma.</summary>

**Answer:** COPD is progressive airway obstruction with little or no reversibility (emphysema or chronic bronchitis, chronic productive cough, pneumothorax risk); asthma is episodic dyspnoea, wheeze and dry cough from reversible obstruction (5–8% of the population), treated with bronchodilators and steroids.

**Why:** Slides 13–15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the presentation and risk factors of PE.</summary>

**Answer:** Acute onset shortness of breath, pleuritic chest pain, cough, haemoptysis and fever; risk factors are DVT, recent surgery, cancer and previous thromboembolism; treated with anticoagulants and always considered because of high mortality.

**Why:** Slide 18.
</details>

## 3. Cough

- ⭐ **Cough** = sudden, often repetitive reflex clearing the **large airways** of secretions, irritants, foreign particles and microbes. **Three phases:** **inhalation → forced exhalation against a closed glottis → violent release when the glottis opens.** Voluntary or involuntary; a **protective reflex** in healthy people. **Afferents:** **myelinated rapidly adapting receptors** and **non-myelinated C-fibres.**
- **Classification:** **acute < 3 weeks; subacute 3–8 weeks; chronic > 8 weeks**; **dry or productive**; **nocturnal / day / both.** Note: the Irwin figure labels chronic as "more than 3 weeks"; the text (> 8 weeks) is used for the exam.
- **Duration clues:** sudden → foreign body?; days → infection; weeks → bronchitis/pneumonia/TB/cancer; months-years → chronic bronchitis/reflux; variable → asthma.
- **Causes:** **respiratory infection** (commonest acute/subacute); **postinfectious cough** (dry, weeks); **GERD** (> half have no other symptoms; **oesophageal pH monitor**); **cough-variant asthma**; **atopic cough** (atopy history, sputum eosinophils, normal airway function); **COPD**; **chronic bronchitis** (productive cough ≥ **3 months in 2 consecutive years** = smoker's cough); **ACE inhibitors** (**10–25%**; only stopping the drug cures it; e.g. ramipril, quinapril).
- **Chronic cough, immunocompetent:** **95%** from **postnasal drip, asthma, GERD, smoking bronchitis, bronchiectasis, ACE inhibitors**; flowchart order: **PNDS → asthma → GERD.**
- **Work-up:** specific (with other symptoms) vs **non-specific**; labs, X-ray, spirometry, CT. **Treatment** by cause (cough suppressants/expectorants; antibiotics or inhaled steroids for chronic cough from protracted bacterial bronchitis or asthma).

### Cough associations
| Origin | Cause | Clinical features |
|---|---|---|
| **Pharynx** | Post-nasal drip | History of chronic rhinitis |
| **Larynx** | Laryngitis, tumour, whooping cough, croup | Voice change, odynophagia, painful harsh cough; paroxysms; **stridor** |
| **Trachea** | Tracheitis | **Retrosternal pain** |
| **Bronchi** | Acute bronchitis / COPD / asthma / carcinoma | Fever, short / productive, worse in the **morning** / dry, worse at **night** / persistent with **haemoptysis** |
| **Parenchyma** | TB / pneumonia / bronchiectasis, abscess / pulmonary oedema / interstitial fibrosis | Productive with haemoptysis / dry then productive / **postural** / **nocturnal, pink frothy sputum** / dry, irritant |
| **Pleura** | Parietal pleura | **Pleurisy on inspiration** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cough phases: inhale, forced exhale against closed glottis, explosive release.
- Acute < 3 w; subacute 3–8 w; chronic > 8 w.
- Chronic cough: PNDS, asthma, GERD, smoking bronchitis, bronchiectasis, ACE inhibitors.
- ACE-inhibitor cough 10–25%: stop the drug.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the cough reflex and what initiates it.</summary>

**Answer:** Inhalation, forced exhalation against a closed glottis, then violent release of air when the glottis opens; initiated by myelinated rapidly adapting receptors and non-myelinated C-fibres in the lungs.

**Why:** Slides 29–30.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Classify cough and list causes of chronic cough.</summary>

**Answer:** By duration (acute < 3 weeks, subacute 3–8, chronic > 8), sputum (dry/productive) and timing. In immunocompetent patients 95% of chronic cough is from postnasal drip, asthma, GERD, smoking-related chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis and ACE inhibitors.

**Why:** Slides 25 and 31.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain GERD and ACE-inhibitor cough.</summary>

**Answer:** GERD (acid reflux) should be considered in unexplained cough; more than half have no other symptoms; confirmed with an oesophageal pH monitor. ACE inhibitors cause cough in 10–25%; stopping them is the only cure.

**Why:** Slides 33–34 and 37.
</details>

## 4. Haemoptysis

- ⭐ **Haemoptysis** = coughing up **blood or blood-stained sputum** from the bronchi, larynx, trachea or lungs (e.g. TB, infections, cardiovascular disease).

### Haemoptysis vs haematemesis
| | **Haemoptysis** | **Haematemesis** |
|---|---|---|
| **History** | **No** nausea/vomiting; **lung disease**; asphyxia possible | Nausea/vomiting **present**; **gastric or hepatic disease**; asphyxia unusual |
| **Blood** | **Frothy**, liquid or clotted, **bright red or pink** | Rarely frothy, **coffee-ground**, **brown to black** |
| **Lab** | **Alkaline pH**, macrophages and neutrophils | **Acidic pH**, food particles |

### Causes
| Site | Common | Rare |
|---|---|---|
| **Tracheobronchial** | Carcinoma, bronchitis, bronchiectasis (also foreign body, acute bronchitis) | Adenoma, foreign body |
| **Parenchyma** | TB and chronic pneumonia, acute / suppurative pneumonia, lung abscess | Parasites, trauma, actinomycetes, mycetoma |
| **Lung vasculature** | Infarction, **PAN, Goodpasture's** | Idiopathic pulmonary haemosiderosis |
| **CVS** | **Acute LVF, mitral stenosis** | Aortic aneurysm |
| **Blood** | **Leukaemia, haemophilia** | Anticoagulants |

- **History:** TB, bronchiectasis, chronic bronchitis, mitral stenosis; smoking, silica dust; duration, frequency, **amount** (large or streaked), **source** (coughed or vomited), **character** (streaked, fresh, frothy pink, gelatinous); accompanying **fever, chest pain, purulent sputum, mucocutaneous bleeding, jaundice.**
- **Investigations:** **CXR, CT** (3D / virtual bronchoscopy), **bronchial angiography**, WBC, sputum cells and culture, **bronchial fibre endoscopy.**
- **Treatment:** by cause: **iced saline, topical vasoconstrictors (adrenaline, vasopressin)**, **selective bronchial intubation** (collapse the bleeding lung), **laser photocoagulation** at bronchoscopy, **bronchial artery angiography + embolisation**; **surgery (lobectomy / pneumonectomy) last resort.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Haemoptysis: frothy bright red, alkaline, lung disease; haematemesis: coffee-ground, acidic, food, GI disease.
- Causes by site: bronchial (carcinoma, bronchiectasis), parenchymal (TB, abscess), vascular (PAN, Goodpasture), CVS (LVF, MS), blood (leukaemia, haemophilia).
- Bronchial artery embolisation; surgery last.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Distinguish haemoptysis from haematemesis.</summary>

**Answer:** Haemoptysis: no nausea, lung disease, frothy bright red or pink blood, alkaline pH with macrophages and neutrophils. Haematemesis: nausea and vomiting, gastric or hepatic disease, coffee-ground or brown-black blood, acidic pH with food particles.

**Why:** Slide 42.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List causes of haemoptysis by site.</summary>

**Answer:** Bronchial (carcinoma, bronchiectasis, acute bronchitis, foreign body); parenchymal (TB, suppurative pneumonia, lung abscess, trauma, actinomycosis); lung vascular (infarction, PAN, Goodpasture); CVS (acute LVF, mitral stenosis, aortic aneurysm); blood (leukaemia, haemophilia, anticoagulants).

**Why:** Slides 43–46.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is haemoptysis managed?</summary>

**Answer:** According to the cause: iced saline or topical vasoconstrictors, selective bronchial intubation, laser photocoagulation, bronchial artery angiography with embolisation; surgery (lobectomy or pneumonectomy) is the last resort.

**Why:** Slide 49.
</details>

## 5. Chest pain

- ⭐ **Chest pain** may signal serious disease and is generally treated as a **medical emergency**; non-cardiac origin is often a **diagnosis of exclusion.**
- **Sources:** oesophagus, musculoskeletal, skin (**herpes zoster**), aorta, heart, lung, trachea.
- **Life-threatening causes:** **aortic dissection, oesophageal rupture, ACS, PE, tension pneumothorax.**

### Differential diagnosis
| Group | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | **ACS** (unstable angina, MI), aortic dissection, pericarditis and tamponade, arrhythmia (AF), stable angina, myocarditis, mitral valve prolapse, aortic aneurysm |
| **Respiratory** | Bronchitis, **PE**, pneumonia, haemothorax, pneumothorax and **tension pneumothorax**, **pleurisy**, TB, tracheitis |
| **GI** | **GERD**, hiatus hernia, achalasia, functional dyspepsia |
| **Chest wall** | **Costochondritis (Tietze's)**, rib fracture, disc prolapse and radiculopathy, breast conditions, **herpes zoster**, TB, osteoarthritis, **Bornholm disease** |

- **Unstable angina** needs emergency treatment **but not primary intervention** as in MI. **Tietze's syndrome** = benign osteochondritis often mistaken for heart disease.
- **Diagnosis:** history guides tests; **CXR/abdominal X-ray** (routine not needed), **CT** if unexplained, **ECG**, **V/Q scan or CT pulmonary angiogram** (suspected PE). **Bloods:** CBC, electrolytes/creatinine, liver enzymes, **CK/CK-MB, troponin I or T**, **D-dimer** (low suspicion of PE), **serum lipase** (exclude pancreatitis). **Management depends on the cause.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five life-threatening causes: aortic dissection, oesophageal rupture, ACS, PE, tension pneumothorax.
- Tietze's syndrome = benign costochondritis; Bornholm disease = epidemic myalgia.
- Troponin = myocardial damage; D-dimer for low suspicion of PE; lipase for pancreatitis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the life-threatening causes of chest pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Aortic dissection, oesophageal rupture, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism and tension pneumothorax.

**Why:** Slide 52.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Classify the causes of chest pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Cardiovascular (ACS, dissection, pericarditis, arrhythmia, angina, myocarditis, MVP, aneurysm), respiratory (bronchitis, PE, pneumonia, haemothorax, pneumothorax, pleurisy, TB, tracheitis), GI (GERD, hiatus hernia, achalasia, dyspepsia) and chest wall (costochondritis, rib injury, disc prolapse, breast, shingles, TB, OA, Bornholm disease).

**Why:** Slides 54–57.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which investigations are used in chest pain?</summary>

**Answer:** ECG, CXR, CT if unexplained, V/Q scan or CT pulmonary angiogram for suspected PE, and bloods: CBC, electrolytes and creatinine, liver enzymes, CK/CK-MB, troponin, D-dimer and serum lipase.

**Why:** Slides 58–59.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Chronic cough = > 8 weeks** (text); acute < 3, subacute 3–8.
- **ACE-inhibitor cough: 10–25%, only cured by stopping the drug.**
- **Haemoptysis is alkaline and frothy; haematemesis is acidic and coffee-ground.**
- **Pneumothorax: dyspnoea NOT improved with oxygen; absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation.**
- **CCF = LVF + RVF together; 25–50% die within 5 years.**
- **Asthma = reversible obstruction; COPD = little or no reversibility.**
- **Chronic bronchitis = productive cough ≥ 3 months in 2 consecutive years.**
- **Unstable angina needs emergency treatment but not primary intervention.**
- **PE: always consider it in acute dyspnoea; treat with anticoagulants.**
