## 1. The normal immune response

- ⭐ **Innate immunity:** **always present**, reacts **immediately**, **first line of defense**; main reactions: **inflammation** (cytokines and complement), **anti-viral defense (type I interferons)**, and **signals that stimulate adaptive immunity.** Acts in **hours.**
- **Adaptive immunity:** **normally silent**, develops over **days**: **humoral** (B cells, antibodies vs **extracellular** microbes) and **cell-mediated** (T cells vs **intracellular** microbes; **CTLs kill infected cells**, **helper T cells make cytokines to activate phagocytes**).
- **Steps:** **antigen recognition → activation, proliferation, differentiation into effector and memory cells → elimination of antigen → decline (memory cells survive).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Innate = hours, inflammation and interferons; adaptive = days, antibodies and T cells.
- Memory cells are the long-lived survivors.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare innate and adaptive immunity.</summary>

**Answer:** Innate immunity is always present and acts immediately (inflammation, interferons); adaptive immunity is normally silent, acts later and is specific (antibodies from B cells and T-cell responses).

**Why:** Slide 3-5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What do antibodies and T cells protect against?</summary>

**Answer:** Antibodies: extracellular pathogens; T cells: intracellular microbes, by killing infected cells (CTLs) or activating phagocytes (helper T cell cytokines).

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the steps of an adaptive response.</summary>

**Answer:** Antigen recognition; activation and differentiation to effector and memory cells; elimination of antigen; decline with memory cells surviving.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

## 2. Hypersensitivity: overview

- **Hypersensitivity** = **injurious immune reactions** (uncontrolled, against harmless antigens, or against host tissues). **Causes:** **autoimmunity**, **reactions against microbes**, **environmental antigens** (~**20%** allergic).
- **Four types:** **I (IgE, TH2, mast cells)**, **II (IgG/IgM against cell/tissue antigens)**, **III (immune complexes)**, **IV (T-cell mediated).**

| Type | Mechanism | Lesion | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | **IgE → mast cell mediators** | Vascular dilation, edema, smooth muscle contraction, mucus | **Anaphylaxis, allergies, atopic asthma** |
| **II** | **IgG/IgM on cell or tissue antigens**; phagocytosis, complement, dysfunction | Phagocytosis or lysis, inflammation, functional change | **Autoimmune hemolytic anemia, Goodpasture** |
| **III** | **Antigen-antibody complex deposition**; complement; leukocytes | Inflammation, **necrotizing vasculitis (fibrinoid necrosis)** | **SLE, serum sickness, Arthus** |
| **IV** | **Activated T cells**: cytokines and CTLs | Perivascular infiltrates, edema, **granuloma** | **Contact dermatitis, MS, type 1 diabetes, TB** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- I, II, III antibody-mediated; IV T cell-mediated.
- Atopy = tendency to type I reactions.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define hypersensitivity and give its causes.</summary>

**Answer:** Injurious immune reactions; due to autoimmunity, excessive or persistent reactions to microbes, or reactions to environmental antigens.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Classify the hypersensitivity reactions.</summary>

**Answer:** Types I (IgE), II (antibody against cell antigens), III (immune complex) and IV (T cell-mediated).

**Why:** Slides 9-11.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is atopy?</summary>

**Answer:** An increased propensity to develop immediate (type I) hypersensitivity reactions.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

## 3. Type I hypersensitivity (immediate)

- **Rapid reaction (minutes)** after **allergen + IgE on mast cells** in a sensitized host; forms: **hay fever (local), asthma, anaphylaxis (fatal systemic).**
- **Sequence:** **(1) TH2 activation and IgE production** (**IL-4: switch to IgE; IL-5: eosinophils; IL-13: mucus**); **(2) sensitization of mast cells** (**FcεRI**, high affinity); **(3) re-exposure → mast cell activation and mediators.**
- **Mediators:** **vasoactive amines (histamine, seconds to minutes), chemotactic factors, proteases (tryptase)**; **lipid mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes)**; **cytokines.**
- **Strong TH2 response depends on:** route, dose, chronicity, genetics. **20-30%** of reactions are **non-antigenic** (cold, exercise).
- **Phases:** **immediate** (vasodilation, congestion, edema) and **late-phase** (**eosinophils, neutrophils, T cells**).
- **Clinical:** **anaphylaxis** (shock from vasodilation; **laryngeal edema**), **bronchial asthma** (smooth muscle hyperactivity; late-phase injury), **allergic rhinitis**, **food allergy** (increased peristalsis: vomiting, diarrhea).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sequence: TH2/IgE → sensitized mast cells → re-exposure and degranulation.
- Late-phase = eosinophils, neutrophils, T cells.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the sequence of type I hypersensitivity.</summary>

**Answer:** TH2 activation with IgE production; mast cell sensitization through FcεRI; on re-exposure the allergen cross-links IgE, releasing mediators.

**Why:** Slides 13-15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the mast cell mediators.</summary>

**Answer:** Vasoactive amines such as histamine, chemotactic factors and proteases; lipid mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes); cytokines.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the two phases of the reaction.</summary>

**Answer:** Immediate phase with vasodilation, congestion and edema; late phase with eosinophil, neutrophil and T-cell infiltrate.

**Why:** Slide 17.
</details>

## 4. Types II, III and IV

- **Type II:** antibodies target **membrane molecules, matrix or adsorbed drug antigens**; mechanisms: **opsonization and phagocytosis** (IgG and C3 receptors), **complement-mediated inflammation** (classical pathway: glomerulonephritis, graft vascular rejection), **cellular dysfunction** (**myasthenia gravis:** anti-ACh receptor, weakness; **Graves:** anti-TSH receptor, hyperthyroidism).
- **Type II examples:** **autoimmune hemolytic anemia (RBC proteins), ITP (GpIIb:IIIa), pemphigus vulgaris (desmogleins, bullae), ANCA vasculitis, Goodpasture (basement membrane: nephritis and lung hemorrhage), acute rheumatic fever (cross-reaction with myocardium), pernicious anemia (intrinsic factor).**
- **Type III:** complexes (**exogenous or endogenous antigens**) deposit in **kidney, joints, small vessels**; neutrophils injure via **lysosomal enzymes and free radicals**; **systemic** (**three phases:** formation, deposition, inflammation; **SLE, serum sickness**) or **local (Arthus reaction:** peak **4-10 h**, edema, hemorrhage, ulceration). **Morphology:** **vasculitis, fibrinoid necrosis, neutrophils;** kidney: **granular IF deposits, electron-dense deposits along GBM.**
- **Type IV:** **DTH (CD4+)** and **CTL cytotoxicity (CD8+)**. **TH1 → IFN-γ → macrophages**; **TH17 → neutrophils, monocytes.** **Tuberculin reaction:** appears **8-12 h**, peaks **24-72 h**, **perivascular CD4+ cuffing.** **Granulomatous inflammation** = prolonged DTH (epithelioid cells, giant cells; TH1 cytokines). **Diseases:** **contact dermatitis, RA, MS, IBD, type 1 diabetes, psoriasis.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Type II = antibody to cell/tissue antigens; type III = deposited complexes; type IV = T cells.
- Arthus = local type III; tuberculin = DTH.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanisms of type II hypersensitivity.</summary>

**Answer:** Opsonization and phagocytosis, complement-mediated inflammation, and antibody-mediated cellular dysfunction (myasthenia gravis, Graves).

**Why:** Slides 19-22.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the pathogenesis and morphology of type III disease.</summary>

**Answer:** Circulating complexes form, deposit in tissues and cause complement-mediated inflammation; morphology shows acute vasculitis with fibrinoid necrosis and neutrophils; in kidney granular immunofluorescence and electron-dense GBM deposits.

**Why:** Slides 24-28.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the tuberculin reaction.</summary>

**Answer:** A DTH reaction in a sensitized person: erythema and induration at 8-12 hours peaking at 24-72 hours, with perivascular CD4+ T cell and macrophage cuffing.

**Why:** Slide 33.
</details>

## 5. Immunodeficiency syndromes

- **Primary (inherited):** impair **innate (phagocytes, NK, complement)** or **adaptive (B and T)** immunity; detected at **6 months-2 years** with **recurrent infections.**

| Defective component | Example |
|---|---|
| **Antibodies** | **X-linked hypogammaglobulinemia (Bruton's)**, **isolated IgA deficiency (most common)**, **hyper IgM** |
| **T cells** | **Thymic aplasia (DiGeorge's)** |
| **B and T cells** | **Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)** |
| **Phagocytes** | **Chronic granulomatous disease** (decreased oxidative burst), leukocyte adhesion deficiency, Chédiak-Higashi |
| **Complement** | **C2, C4 deficiency**, C3 deficiency, regulatory protein deficiency (PNH) |

- **Secondary (acquired)** are **more common:** **old age, chronic malnutrition, malignancy, metabolic disease (diabetes, liver/renal failure), drugs (cytotoxic, steroids), splenectomy (pneumococcal septicemia), AIDS.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Most common primary: isolated IgA deficiency.
- Secondary immunodeficiencies are commoner than primary.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify immunodeficiencies and give examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Primary (Bruton's, IgA deficiency, DiGeorge's, SCID, chronic granulomatous disease, C2/C4 deficiency) and secondary (malnutrition, malignancy, drugs, splenectomy, AIDS).

**Why:** Slides 37-42.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. When and how do primary immunodeficiencies present?</summary>

**Answer:** In infancy (6 months to 2 years) with recurrent infections.

**Why:** Slide 38.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is chronic granulomatous disease?</summary>

**Answer:** A phagocyte defect with decreased oxidative burst (X-linked or autosomal recessive phagocyte oxidase).

**Why:** Slide 39 and table 5.13.
</details>

## 6. AIDS

- **HIV** = retrovirus → **profound immunosuppression (opportunistic infections), secondary neoplasms, neurologic disease.** **Transmission:** **sexual, parenteral, mother-to-newborn.** **HIV-1** (USA, Europe, central Africa) vs **HIV-2** (West Africa).
- **Virion:** **p24 capsid** (best antigen for screening), **p7/p9**, **2 RNA copies**, **protease, reverse transcriptase, integrase**; **p17 matrix**; envelope **gp120 and gp41.**
- **Pathogenesis:** **gp120 binds CD4 then CCR5; gp41 fuses**; **reverse transcription → cDNA → integration (provirus)**; **latent** or **productive** infection; **CD4 depletion;** **CD4/CD8 normally ~2, ≤ 0.5 in AIDS.**

| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| **Acute** | **Mucosal infection of memory CD4+ T cells**, DCs carry virus to nodes, **viremia**; **3-6 weeks flulike syndrome** (40-90%), resolves in **2-4 weeks**; **seroconversion 3-7 weeks**; **CD8+ CTLs** reduce viremia by **~12 weeks** |
| **Chronic (clinical latency)** | **Continuous replication in nodes and spleen**; few symptoms; **CD4 slowly falls** |
| **AIDS** | **Breakdown of defense**, **high plasma virus**, **fever > 1 month, fatigue, weight loss, diarrhea**, then **opportunistic infections, neoplasms, neurologic disease** |

- **Viral set point** predicts CD4 decline; **HIV-1 RNA** marks progression.
- **Neoplasms:** **Kaposi sarcoma (most common; HHV-8; multicentric, aggressive)**; **non-Hodgkin lymphoma (second; brain primary lymphoma AIDS-defining; 30-40% EBV)**; **cervical carcinoma (HPV; routine gynecologic exam).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Three phases: acute, chronic latency, AIDS.
- Kaposi sarcoma (HHV-8), NHL (EBV), cervical cancer (HPV).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of HIV.</summary>

**Answer:** Core with p24 capsid, p7/p9, two RNA copies and three enzymes; p17 matrix; envelope glycoproteins gp120 and gp41.

**Why:** Slide 44.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the pathogenesis of HIV in a T cell.</summary>

**Answer:** gp120 binds CD4 and CCR5, gp41 fuses; reverse transcription gives cDNA that integrates as a provirus, remaining latent or producing virus that kills the cell.

**Why:** Slides 45-48.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Outline the natural history of HIV infection.</summary>

**Answer:** Acute infection with viremia and flulike illness, control by CD8+ CTLs, a long chronic latent phase with falling CD4 counts, then AIDS with opportunistic infections, neoplasms and neurologic disease.

**Why:** Slides 50-56.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Types I, II, III are antibody-mediated; type IV is T cell-mediated.**
- **Myasthenia gravis and Graves disease are type II (antibody-mediated dysfunction).**
- **Arthus = local type III; tuberculin = type IV.**
- **Most common primary immunodeficiency: isolated IgA deficiency.**
- **Kaposi sarcoma (most common) vs non-Hodgkin lymphoma (second).**
- **CD4/CD8 ratio: ~2 normally, ≤ 0.5 in AIDS.**
