## 1. Clinical presentation and examination

- **Tumours, tumour-like lesions and cysts** are considered together, partly because their **clinical presentation and management are similar**.
- **History:** often **asymptomatic (discovered accidentally)**.
- **Age:** **childhood and adolescence** = primary malignant tumours, notably **Ewing's tumour and osteosarcoma**; **chondrosarcoma and fibrosarcoma** typically occur in **older people**. ⭐ In patients **over 70**, **metastatic bone lesions are more common than all primary tumours together**.
- **Pain:** even a **tiny lesion** may be very painful if **encapsulated in dense bone** (e.g. **osteoid osteoma**). Other presentations: **swelling or a lump** (may be alarming) and **neurological symptoms** (**paraesthesiae or numbness**).
- **Examination:** focus on the **symptomatic part**, but include the **area of lymphatic drainage** and often the **pelvis, abdomen, chest and spine**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Often asymptomatic and found accidentally; pain, lump or neurological symptoms otherwise.
- Ewing and osteosarcoma = young; chondrosarcoma and fibrosarcoma = old.
- Over 70: metastases outnumber all primary bone tumours.

</div>

## 2. Imaging, laboratory investigations and biopsy

### Questions to ask when studying an X-ray
1. **Solitary** or multiple lesions?
2. What **type of bone** is involved?
3. **Where** is the lesion in the bone?
4. **Margins** well- or ill-defined?
5. **Flecks of calcification** in the lesion?
6. Is the **cortex eroded or destroyed**?
7. Any **periosteal new-bone formation**?
8. Does the tumour **extend into the soft tissues**?

- **Other imaging:** **radionuclide scanning, CT, MRI**.
- **Laboratory:** blood tests **exclude other conditions** (e.g. **infection**, **metabolic bone disorders**, or a **'brown tumour' in hyperparathyroidism**); **increased ESR** and **elevated serum alkaline phosphatase**; **serum protein electrophoresis** and **urine** tests are also listed.
- **Biopsy:** **needle biopsy** or **open biopsy**. ⭐ **Delay the biopsy until all imaging studies have been completed.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Eight X-ray questions: number, bone type, site, margins, calcification, cortex, periosteum, soft tissue.
- Imaging: X-ray, radionuclide scan, CT, MRI.
- Biopsy (needle or open) only after imaging is complete.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the questions to ask when studying a bone X-ray.</summary>

**Answer:** Solitary or multiple? Which type of bone? Where in the bone? Margins well- or ill-defined? Flecks of calcification? Cortex eroded or destroyed? Periosteal new-bone formation? Extension into the soft tissues?

**Why:** Slide "Imaging – X-rays".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the role of blood tests in a suspected bone tumour?</summary>

**Answer:** They exclude other conditions such as infection, metabolic bone disorders or a brown tumour of hyperparathyroidism; findings may include a raised ESR and raised serum alkaline phosphatase.

**Why:** Slide "Laboratory investigations".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When should a bone biopsy be performed and by which methods?</summary>

**Answer:** By needle or open biopsy, delayed until all imaging studies have been completed.

## 3. Differential diagnosis and staging

- **Differential diagnosis:** **soft-tissue haematoma, myositis ossificans, stress fracture, bone infection, gout.**
- **Figure 9.1:** (a) a huge swelling that was simply a **clotted haematoma**; (b) **bone infection with pathological fracture**; (c) **florid callus in an un-united fracture**; (d) **large erosion in the calcaneum by a gouty tophus**; (e) **bone infarcts**.

### Staging
| Group | Stages |
|---|---|
| **Benign tumours (Enneking)** | **Latent, active, aggressive** |
| **Bone sarcomas** | **Stage I** = all low-grade sarcomas; **Stage II** = histologically high-grade lesions; **Stage III** = sarcomas which have **metastasized** |

## 4. Methods of treatment

- **Three methods:** **tumour excision, multi-agent chemotherapy, radiotherapy.**
- **Types of excision** (from least to most extensive in the figure): **intracapsular (intralesional)** → **marginal** → **wide (local)** → **radical resection**.
- **Multi-agent chemotherapy (neoadjuvant and adjuvant):** **reduces the size of the primary lesion, prevents metastatic seeding and improves survival.**
- **Radiotherapy:** indications are **more restricted** nowadays; main complications are **post-irradiation spindle-cell sarcoma** and **pathological fracture in weight-bearing bones**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Excision, chemotherapy, radiotherapy.
- Intracapsular, marginal, wide, radical = increasing extent.
- Radiotherapy risks: post-irradiation sarcoma and pathological fracture.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the types of tumour excision.</summary>

**Answer:** Intracapsular (intralesional) excision, marginal excision, wide excision and radical resection.

**Why:** Slide "Tumour excision" and its figure.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the benefits of multi-agent chemotherapy?</summary>

**Answer:** Neoadjuvant and adjuvant regimens reduce the size of the primary lesion, prevent metastatic seeding and improve the chances of survival.

**Why:** Slide "Multi-agent chemotherapy".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the complications of radiotherapy?</summary>

**Answer:** Post-irradiation spindle-cell sarcoma and pathological fracture in weight-bearing bones; hence its indications are now more restricted.

**Why:** Slide "Radiotherapy".
</details>

## 5. Non-ossifying fibroma, fibrous dysplasia and osteoid osteoma

| Lesion | Key features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-ossifying fibroma (fibrous cortical defect)** | ⭐ **Commonest benign lesion of bone**; developmental defect, a **nest of fibrous tissue** that persists for some years before ossifying; **asymptomatic**, **children**, **incidental X-ray finding**; commonest sites **metaphyses of long bones** | — (asymptomatic) |
| **Fibrous dysplasia** | Developmental disorder: **trabecular bone replaced by cellular fibrous tissue**; large lesions → **weak bone, pathological fracture, progressive deformity** | Small: none. Large/painful/threatening fracture (or fractured): **curettage and grafting** |
| **Osteoid osteoma** | **< 1 cm**, symptoms **out of proportion to size**; **under 30 years**; any bone **except the skull**, over half in **femur or tibia**; **night pain relieved by salicylates**; X-ray: small radiolucent **nidus** | **Complete removal or destruction of the nidus** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Non-ossifying fibroma = commonest benign bone lesion, incidental in children.
- Fibrous dysplasia = bone replaced by fibrous tissue; fracture and deformity risk.
- Osteoid osteoma = nidus, night pain relieved by salicylates; remove the nidus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the non-ossifying fibroma.</summary>

**Answer:** The commonest benign bone lesion: a developmental defect with a nest of fibrous tissue that persists for years before ossifying; asymptomatic, found incidentally on X-ray in children, usually in the metaphyses of long bones.

**Why:** Slide "Benign bone lesions – non-ossifying fibroma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is fibrous dysplasia and how is it managed?</summary>

**Answer:** A developmental disorder in which trabecular bone is replaced by cellular fibrous tissue; large lesions weaken the bone (fracture, deformity). Small lesions need no treatment; large, painful or fracture-threatening lesions are curetted and grafted.

**Why:** Slide "Fibrous dysplasia".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the clinical and X-ray features and treatment of osteoid osteoma.</summary>

**Answer:** A tiny (< 1 cm) tumour in patients under 30, mostly femur or tibia (not skull), with night pain relieved by salicylates; X-ray shows a small radiolucent nidus; the only effective treatment is complete removal or destruction of the nidus.

**Why:** Slide "Osteoid osteoma".
</details>

## 6. Chondroma, osteochondroma and giant-cell tumour

| Lesion | Key features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Chondroma (enchondroma)** | Usually **asymptomatic**, found **incidentally** or after **pathological fracture**; **any age, any bone (hands and feet)**; **solitary or multiple** | Not always needed; remove if **enlarging** or **pathological fracture** |
| **Osteochondroma (cartilage-capped exostosis)** | One of the **commonest 'tumours' of bone**; **fast-growing ends of long bones** and **crest of the ilium**; **teenager**; stops growing at **end of normal growth**; pain from overlying **bursa** or impingement; X-ray **pathognomonic** exostosis from the **metaphysis**; **hereditary multiple exostosis**; ⭐ malignant change **1% solitary, 6% multiple**; **growth after growth period = malignancy** | Excise if symptomatic; **urgent operation** in an adult if recently bigger or painful |
| **Giant-cell tumour** | **Young adult**, **pain at the end of a long bone**, sometimes slight swelling; X-ray, CT, MRI; can **transform into osteosarcoma** | **Thorough curettage and bone grafting** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Chondroma: hands and feet, often incidental; remove if growing or fractured.
- Osteochondroma: growth after skeletal maturity or new pain = malignancy (1% solitary, 6% multiple).
- Giant-cell tumour: young adult, end of long bone, curettage and grafting.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. When should a chondroma be removed?</summary>

**Answer:** Treatment is not always necessary, but remove it if the tumour is enlarging or has presented with a pathological fracture.

**Why:** Slide "Chondroma (enchondroma)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What suggests malignant transformation of an osteochondroma?</summary>

**Answer:** Any enlargement after the end of the normal growth period, or in an adult a recent increase in size or new pain; malignant transformation occurs in 1% of solitary and 6% of multiple (hereditary multiple exostosis) lesions, so urgent operation is needed.

**Why:** Slides "Osteochondroma" (two slides).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the giant-cell tumour and its treatment.</summary>

**Answer:** A tumour of young adults causing pain (sometimes slight swelling) at the end of a long bone, assessed by X-ray, CT and MRI, with the potential to transform into an osteosarcoma; treated by thorough curettage and bone grafting.

**Why:** Slide "Giant-cell tumour".
</details>

## 7. Simple and aneurysmal bone cysts

| Cyst | Key features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple bone cyst (solitary / unicameral)** | **Childhood**, **metaphysis of a long bone**, most commonly **proximal humerus or femur**; **not a tumour**; **tends to heal spontaneously**; found after **pathological fracture** or **incidentally**; X-ray **well-demarcated radiolucent area in the metaphysis** | **Asymptomatic in older children: leave alone.** 'Active' cysts: **aspiration + injection of methylprednisolone or autogenous bone marrow**, or **curettage and packing with bone chips** |
| **Aneurysmal bone cyst** | **Any age, almost any bone**, long-bone **metaphyses**; X-ray **well-defined radiolucent cyst**; ⭐ **MRI diagnostic (fluid-fluid level)** | **Curettage then packing with bone grafts** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Simple cyst: child, proximal humerus or femur, heals spontaneously, may present with fracture.
- Aneurysmal cyst: any age/bone, fluid-fluid level on MRI.
- Both: curettage and grafting for active lesions (simple: steroid or marrow injection option).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the simple bone cyst.</summary>

**Answer:** A solitary (unicameral) cyst of childhood in the metaphysis of a long bone, most commonly the proximal humerus or femur; not a tumour and tends to heal spontaneously; found after pathological fracture or incidentally; X-ray shows a well-demarcated radiolucent area in the metaphysis.

**Why:** Slide "Simple bone cyst".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is a simple bone cyst treated?</summary>

**Answer:** Asymptomatic lesions in older children are left alone; active cysts are treated by aspiration and injection of methylprednisolone or autogenous bone marrow, or by thorough curettage and packing with bone chips.

**Why:** Slide "Simple bone cyst".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the diagnostic feature of an aneurysmal bone cyst and how is it treated?</summary>

**Answer:** A well-defined radiolucent cyst in any bone at any age (long-bone metaphyses) with a fluid-fluid level on MRI; treated by curettage and packing with bone grafts.

**Why:** Slide "Aneurysmal bone cyst".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Biopsy only after ALL imaging is complete.**
- **Osteochondroma growing after skeletal maturity = suspect malignancy** (1% solitary, 6% multiple).
- **Osteoid osteoma: tiny (< 1 cm) but very painful; night pain relieved by salicylates; treat by removing the nidus.**
- **Simple bone cyst is NOT a tumour and tends to heal spontaneously; MRI fluid-fluid level = aneurysmal bone cyst.**
- **Over 70: metastases are commoner than all primary bone tumours together.**
- **Non-ossifying fibroma = commonest benign bone lesion (incidental, children).**
- **Radiotherapy complications: post-irradiation spindle-cell sarcoma and pathological fracture in weight-bearing bones.**
