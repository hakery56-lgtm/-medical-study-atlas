## 1. Types of synapses

- ⭐ **Synapse** = the **junction between two neurons**: the **presynaptic neuron** (sends the signal) and the **postsynaptic neuron** (receives it), separated by the **synaptic cleft**. Synapses are **chemical or electrical**.

| | **Electrical synapse** | **Chemical synapse** |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | **Very rare** in the adult mammalian nervous system | **The predominant type** |
| **Mechanism** | **Gap junction**: current flows **directly through a specialized protein molecule** | **Neurotransmitters (NT)** synthesized and **stored in synaptic vesicles** |
| **Membrane gap** | **Very small (about 5 nm)** | **Synaptic cleft** |

- **Beyond the lecture:** gap junctions are built from **connexons** (ions pass directly cell to cell) → **very fast, almost no synaptic delay**. Chemical synapses are **slower** but **allow the signal to be modified**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Synapse = pre- and postsynaptic neuron with a cleft between them.
- Electrical (gap junction, ~5 nm, rare in adults) vs chemical (vesicles + NT, predominant).
- Electrical = fast, no delay; chemical = slower, modifiable.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define a synapse and name its parts.</summary>

**Answer:** A synapse is the junction between two neurons: the presynaptic neuron sends the signal, the postsynaptic neuron receives it, and the synaptic cleft lies between them.

**Why:** Slide 1.1 "What is a synapse?".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare electrical and chemical synapses.</summary>

**Answer:** Electrical synapses are very rare in the adult mammalian nervous system and work through gap junctions (current flows directly through a specialized protein, membrane gap about 5 nm). Chemical synapses predominate and work through neurotransmitters synthesized and stored in synaptic vesicles.

**Why:** Slide 1.2 "Electrical vs. chemical synapses".
</details>

**Answer:** Gap junction connexons let ions pass directly between cells, so there is almost no synaptic delay. Chemical synapses are slower but allow the signal to be modified.

**Why:** "Beyond the lecture" box of Part 1.
</details>

## 2. Neurotransmitter release and properties of synapses

| Step | Event | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Action potential arrives** | Depolarizes the **presynaptic membrane** |
| **2** | **Ca²⁺ influx** | **Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels** open, Ca²⁺ enters the terminal |
| **3** | **Vesicles attracted** | Raised intracellular Ca²⁺ attracts **NT-filled vesicles** to the presynaptic membrane |
| **4** | **Fusion and exocytosis** | Vesicles fuse with the **active zones** → NT released into the cleft |
| **5** | **NT diffuses** | Across the synaptic cleft |
| **6** | **Binds receptors** | **Specific receptors** on the postsynaptic membrane |
| **7** | **Postsynaptic response** | **Ion channels open** or **intracellular signaling** is triggered |

- ⭐ Vesicle fusion is carried out by **SNARE proteins**; **botulinum toxin cuts SNAREs** → **blocks NT release** (beyond the lecture).

### Properties of synapses
1. **One-way conduction** (pre → postsynaptic).
2. The synapse is a site of **neurotransduction** (**electrical signal → chemical signal**).
3. The intercellular chemical message is **converted into an intracellular signal**.

### Result of NT–receptor reaction (one or more)
| | Result | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **a** | **Direct** opening/closure of an ion channel | Receptor itself is, or is directly coupled to, the channel |
| **b** | **Indirect** opening/closure of an ion channel | An **intracellular messenger** links receptor and channel |
| **c** | Initiation of **intracellular enzymatic/metabolic activities** | Metabolic changes in the postsynaptic cell |
| **d** | Induction of **gene transcription** | **New protein** production |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Order: AP → Ca²⁺ entry → vesicles attracted → fusion → diffusion → binding → response.
- Ca²⁺ entry through voltage-gated channels triggers vesicle movement.
- Synapse conduction is one-way and converts electrical to chemical signal.
- Receptor effects: direct channel, indirect channel, enzymatic activity, gene transcription.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the steps of neurotransmitter release and synaptic transmission.</summary>

**Answer:** The AP depolarizes the presynaptic terminal; voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open and Ca²⁺ enters; the Ca²⁺ attracts vesicles to the membrane; vesicles fuse with active zones and release NT by exocytosis; NT diffuses across the cleft, binds specific postsynaptic receptors and produces a response (ion channels open or intracellular signaling).

**Why:** Table 2.1 "Steps of neurotransmitter release and synaptic transmission".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the properties of synapses.</summary>

**Answer:** One-way conduction from the pre- to the postsynaptic neuron; the synapse is a site of neurotransduction (electrical to chemical signal); the intercellular chemical message is converted into an intracellular signal.

**Why:** Section 2.2 "Properties of synapses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What can the reaction between a neurotransmitter and its receptor cause?</summary>

**Answer:** Direct or indirect opening/closure of an ion channel, initiation of intracellular enzymatic or metabolic activities, and induction of gene transcription (new protein production).

**Why:** Table in section 2.2 (results a to d).
</details>

## 3. Synaptic potentials and factors affecting transmission

| | **EPSP (excitatory)** | **IPSP (inhibitory)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Effect** | Brings the membrane **toward threshold (depolarization)** | Causes **hyperpolarization** |
| **Ion movement** | **Cations enter** or **anions exit** | **Anions enter** or **cations exit** |
| **Beyond the lecture** | Mainly **Na⁺ entry** at many excitatory synapses | Many are **Cl⁻ entry or K⁺ exit** |

- EPSP and IPSP can be present **together**: **EPSP larger → stimulation**; **IPSP larger → inhibition**.
- Synaptic potentials are **graded**, **not all-or-none**.
- **Synaptic delay ≈ 0.5 ms** (time to travel from pre- to postsynaptic neuron).

### Factors affecting synaptic transmission
| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Alkalosis** | **Enhances** transmission; **pH 7.4 → 7.8** → ↑ cerebral excitability and **convulsions** |
| **Acidosis** | **Depresses** transmission |
| **Hypoxia** | **Depresses** transmission and **prolongs reflex time** (accumulation of acidic metabolites) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- EPSP = depolarizing (cation in / anion out); IPSP = hyperpolarizing (anion in / cation out).
- The larger of EPSP/IPSP decides stimulation vs inhibition.
- Graded potentials, synaptic delay about 0.5 ms.
- Alkalosis enhances; acidosis and hypoxia depress transmission.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare EPSP and IPSP.</summary>

**Answer:** EPSP brings the membrane toward threshold (depolarization) as cations enter or anions exit; IPSP causes hyperpolarization as anions enter or cations exit. When both are present the larger one decides stimulation or inhibition.

**Why:** Table 3.1 "EPSP and IPSP".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the other properties of synaptic potentials?</summary>

**Answer:** They are graded, not all-or-none; and about 0.5 ms (the synaptic delay) is needed for the signal to travel from the pre- to the postsynaptic neuron.

**Why:** Section 3.2 "Other properties".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do alkalosis, acidosis and hypoxia affect synaptic transmission?</summary>

**Answer:** Alkalosis enhances it (pH 7.4 to 7.8 gives increased cerebral excitability and convulsions); acidosis depresses it; hypoxia depresses it and prolongs reflex time because acidic metabolites accumulate.

**Why:** Table 3.3 "Factors affecting synaptic transmission".
</details>

## 4. Neurotransmitters and acetylcholine

### Classification
| **A. Small-molecule, rapidly acting** | Transmitters |
|---|---|
| **Class I** | **Acetylcholine** |
| **Class II** | **The amines:** adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, histamine |
| **Class III** | **Amino acids:** GABA, glycine, glutamate, aspartate |
| **Class IV** | **Nitric oxide (NO)** |

- **B. Neuropeptide, slowly acting:** hypothalamic-releasing hormones, pituitary peptides, peptides that act on gut and brain, peptides from other tissues.
- **Neurons are named after their transmitter:** **cholinergic** (ACh), **catecholaminergic** (catecholamines), **serotonergic** (serotonin), **amino acidergic** (amino acids).

### Acetylcholine (ACh)
- ⭐ **ACh is the NT at:** the **neuromuscular junction**; **preganglionic** neurons of the **sympathetic and parasympathetic** PNS; **postganglionic** neurons of the **parasympathetic** PNS; **basal forebrain and brain stem** complexes.

| Process | Details |
|---|---|
| **Synthesis** | From **acetyl-CoA + choline**, by **choline acetyltransferase (CAT)** |
| **Degradation** | In the synaptic cleft by **acetylcholinesterase (AChE)** |
| **Receptors** | **Nicotinic** (NMJ, sympathetic ganglia, many CNS parts) and **muscarinic** (parasympathetic target organs and parts of the brain) |

- **Brain storming:** if AChE is blocked, **ACh persists** and keeps stimulating **nicotinic receptors** at the NMJ → disturbed transmission (twitching and weakness in AChE-inhibitor poisoning); AChE inhibitors treat **myasthenia gravis**.
- Note: the slides abbreviate acetylcholinesterase as "ACE"; the standard abbreviation is **AChE** (ACE = angiotensin-converting enzyme).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four small-molecule classes: ACh, amines, amino acids, NO; neuropeptides act slowly.
- ACh: NMJ, all preganglionic autonomic neurons, parasympathetic postganglionic neurons, basal forebrain/brain stem.
- CAT makes ACh; AChE destroys it in the cleft.
- Receptors: nicotinic and muscarinic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify the neurotransmitters.</summary>

**Answer:** Small-molecule rapidly acting: Class I acetylcholine; Class II amines (adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, histamine); Class III amino acids (GABA, glycine, glutamate, aspartate); Class IV nitric oxide. Neuropeptide slowly acting: hypothalamic-releasing hormones, pituitary peptides, gut and brain peptides, peptides from other tissues.

**Why:** Section 4.1 "Classification".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Where is ACh the neurotransmitter, and how is it made and removed?</summary>

**Answer:** At the neuromuscular junction, preganglionic sympathetic and parasympathetic neurons, postganglionic parasympathetic neurons, and basal forebrain/brain stem complexes. It is synthesized from acetyl-CoA and choline by choline acetyltransferase and degraded in the cleft by acetylcholinesterase.

**Why:** Section 4.2 "Acetylcholine (ACh)".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What happens at the neuromuscular junction if acetylcholinesterase is blocked?</summary>

**Answer:** ACh persists in the cleft and keeps stimulating nicotinic receptors, disturbing normal transmission (twitching and weakness in poisoning with AChE inhibitors); the same inhibitors are used to treat myasthenia gravis.

**Why:** "Brain storming" box and suggested answer.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Electrical synapse = gap junction, RARE in adults; chemical = vesicles, PREDOMINANT.**
- **Ca²⁺ ENTRY comes BEFORE vesicle attraction and fusion.**
- **EPSP = depolarization (cation in / anion out); IPSP = hyperpolarization (anion in / cation out).**
- **Synaptic potentials are GRADED, not all-or-none.**
- **Alkalosis ENHANCES; acidosis and hypoxia DEPRESS synaptic transmission.**
- **CAT synthesizes ACh; AChE degrades it (not "ACE").**
- **Nicotinic receptors at the NMJ; muscarinic at parasympathetic target organs.**
