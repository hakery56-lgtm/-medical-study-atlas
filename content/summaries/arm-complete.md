# Anatomy of the Arm (Complete Lecture) — Exam Night Review

Dr. Ahmed Al-Baghdadi's full arm lecture in one place: **bones and fascia, the two compartments and their muscles, veins and arteries, the anastomosis around the elbow, the elbow and proximal radio-ulnar joints, the cubital fossa, and the applied anatomy (humeral fractures, supracondylar fracture, Volkmann's contracture, pulled elbow).** After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

## 1. Bones, fascia & compartments

### Bone notes (humerus, radius, ulna)
- **Medial epicondyle is more prominent** than the lateral (golfer's elbow = medial epicondyle).
- The **medial part of the trochlea** is more prominent than the lateral part.
- The **olecranon fossa** (posterior) is **deeper** than the **radial and coronoid fossae** (anterior).
- **Ulna:** the **trochlear notch faces forwards**, with the **radial notch on the lateral side**.

### Deep fascia & compartments
- The arm is enclosed in a **sheath of deep fascia**.
- Two **intermuscular septa** (medial and lateral) extend from it to the **medial and lateral supracondylar ridges** of the humerus.
- They divide the arm into ⭐ **anterior** and **posterior fascial compartments**.

| Compartment | Muscles | Nerve | Artery |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior** | biceps brachii, coracobrachialis, brachialis | **musculocutaneous** | **brachial** |
| **Posterior** | triceps brachii | **radial** | **profunda brachii** + **ulnar collaterals** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Medial epicondyle and medial trochlea are more prominent; olecranon fossa is the deepest fossa.
- Deep fascia → medial and lateral intermuscular septa → anterior and posterior compartments.
- Anterior: 3 muscles, musculocutaneous nerve, brachial artery. Posterior: triceps, radial nerve, profunda brachii + ulnar collaterals.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is the arm divided into compartments?</summary>

**Answer:** The arm is enclosed in a sheath of deep fascia from which medial and lateral intermuscular septa extend to the medial and lateral supracondylar ridges of the humerus. These septa divide the arm into anterior and posterior fascial compartments.

**Why:** Fascia → septa → compartments.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the contents (muscles, nerve, artery) of each compartment of the arm.</summary>

**Answer:** Anterior compartment: biceps brachii, coracobrachialis and brachialis; nerve — musculocutaneous; artery — brachial. Posterior compartment: triceps brachii; nerve — radial; arteries — profunda brachii and ulnar collateral arteries (branches of the brachial artery).

**Why:** A "table" answer the examiner loves.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Mention three bony notes about the lower end of the humerus and the ulna.</summary>

**Answer:** The medial epicondyle is more prominent than the lateral; the medial part of the trochlea is more prominent than the lateral; the olecranon fossa is deeper than the radial and coronoid fossae; the trochlear notch of the ulna faces forwards with the radial notch on its lateral side.

**Why:** These are the only written notes on the bone slides.
</details>

## 2. Anterior compartment — muscles of the front of the arm

| Muscle | Origin | Insertion | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Biceps brachii** | **long head:** supraglenoid tubercle (**intracapsular, extrasynovial**); **short head:** coracoid process | **posterior part of radial tuberosity** + deep fascia of the forearm (bicipital aponeurosis) | flexes the supinated forearm; **supinates** the flexed pronated forearm; weak flexor of shoulder |
| **Coracobrachialis** | tip of coracoid (with short head of biceps) | **middle of medial border** of humerus | flexion + adduction of shoulder (weak) |
| **Brachialis** | front of **lower half** of humerus + intermuscular septa | **coronoid process & ulnar tuberosity** | ⭐ **main flexor of the elbow** |

⭐ **Nerve supply:** all by the **musculocutaneous nerve**, **except the lateral ½ of brachialis** → **radial nerve** (brachialis has **double nerve supply**).

### Biceps brachii — details
- **Long head is lateral** ("L for L"); **short head is medial**.
- The long head emerges at the top of the **intertubercular sulcus**, surrounded by a **tube of synovial membrane**.
- The short head arises from the **tip of the coracoid** in a **common tendon with coracobrachialis**.
- Lies **in front of brachialis**, with the ⭐ **musculocutaneous nerve sandwiched between them**.
- **Insertion:** radial tuberosity, with a **bursa** between tendon and tuberosity.
- **Bicipital aponeurosis:** extension of the tendon passing **medially** to blend with the **deep fascia of the forearm**.
- **Actions:** (1) ⭐ **powerful supinator** (because it inserts on the **posterior** aspect of the radial tuberosity); (2) **flexor of the forearm**; (3) long head aids **shoulder flexion** and **stabilizes the abducted shoulder**.

### Coracobrachialis
Inserts at the **middle of the medial border** of the humerus — **opposite the deltoid insertion** at the middle of the lateral border.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Biceps: long head supraglenoid (intracapsular, extrasynovial), short head coracoid; inserts on the posterior radial tuberosity → powerful supinator + elbow flexor.
- Brachialis = main flexor of the elbow; double supply (musculocutaneous + radial for lateral half).
- Coracobrachialis: coracoid → middle of medial humerus; weak flexor/adductor of shoulder.
- Musculocutaneous nerve lies between biceps and brachialis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the origin, insertion and actions of biceps brachii.</summary>

**Answer:** Long head from the supraglenoid tubercle (intracapsular, extrasynovial — surrounded by a synovial sheath in the intertubercular sulcus); short head from the tip of the coracoid process with coracobrachialis. It inserts into the posterior part of the radial tuberosity (with a bursa) and, via the bicipital aponeurosis, into the deep fascia of the forearm. Actions: powerful supinator of the forearm, flexor of the elbow, and its long head aids shoulder flexion and stabilizes the abducted shoulder.

**Why:** Origin + insertion + three actions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is biceps a powerful supinator?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it inserts onto the posterior aspect of the radial tuberosity; when it contracts it unwinds the radius, rotating it laterally into supination — most powerfully when the elbow is flexed.

**Why:** Insertion explains action.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is brachialis said to have a double nerve supply?</summary>

**Answer:** Most of it is supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve like the rest of the anterior compartment, but its lateral half is also supplied by the radial nerve.

**Why:** The only exception in the anterior compartment.
</details>

## 3. Posterior compartment — triceps brachii

| Head | Origin |
|---|---|
| **Long** | **infraglenoid tubercle** of scapula (**extracapsular**) |
| **Lateral** | back of humerus **above the spiral (radial) groove** |
| **Medial** | back of humerus **below the spiral groove** (deeper plane; also from intermuscular septum — "comparable to brachialis") |

- **Insertion:** superior surface of the **olecranon** of the ulna.
- **Nerve:** **radial nerve** — ⭐ **long head supplied in the axilla**; **lateral and medial heads in the spiral groove**.
- **Actions:** (1) ⭐ **main extensor of the elbow**; (2) long head aids **shoulder extension** and **stabilizes the abducted shoulder**.

### Bursae related to the triceps tendon
1. **Subcutaneous olecranon bursa** — in the subcutaneous tissue → inflamed by repeated friction = ⭐ **"student's elbow"**.
2. **Subtendinous olecranon bursa** — between the tendon and the olecranon.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Triceps heads: long (infraglenoid), lateral (above spiral groove), medial (below spiral groove, deepest).
- Inserts on the olecranon; radial nerve (long head in axilla, other heads in spiral groove).
- Main extensor of elbow; long head stabilizes the abducted shoulder.
- Two bursae: subcutaneous (student's elbow) and subtendinous.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the origins, insertion, nerve supply and action of triceps.</summary>

**Answer:** Long head from the infraglenoid tubercle (extracapsular); lateral head from the back of the humerus above the spiral groove; medial head from the back of the humerus below the spiral groove. Inserts into the superior surface of the olecranon. Supplied by the radial nerve (long head in the axilla; lateral and medial heads in the spiral groove). Main extensor of the elbow; the long head also aids shoulder extension and stabilizes the abducted shoulder.

**Why:** A complete muscle answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is "student's elbow"?</summary>

**Answer:** Inflammation (bursitis) of the subcutaneous olecranon bursa caused by repeated friction, e.g. leaning on the elbows on a desk.

**Why:** Classic applied-anatomy definition.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the bursae related to the triceps tendon.</summary>

**Answer:** The subcutaneous olecranon bursa (in the subcutaneous tissue) and the subtendinous olecranon bursa (between the tendon and the olecranon).

**Why:** Two bursae — easy marks.
</details>

## 4. Veins & arteries of the arm

### Superficial veins
- Both start from the **dorsal venous arch of the hand**: **basilic** (medial side), **cephalic** (lateral side).
- **Cephalic vein:** runs in the **deltopectoral groove** and **pierces the clavipectoral fascia** to enter the **axillary vein**.
- **Basilic vein:** pierces the deep fascia in the **medial mid-arm**, joins the **venae comitantes** → at the **lower border of teres major** becomes the **axillary vein**.

### Arterial supply of the upper limb
**Subclavian → axillary → brachial** (from the lower border of teres major) → **radial + ulnar**; ulnar → **common interosseous** → **anterior & posterior interosseous**. All arteries have **venae comitantes**.

### Brachial artery
| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Beginning** | continuation of the axillary artery at the **lower border of teres major** |
| **End** | divides into **radial and ulnar** arteries in the **cubital fossa**, **opposite the neck of the radius** |
| **Course** | on the **medial border of brachialis**; **superficial throughout**, just deep to the deep fascia; accompanied by venae comitantes |

⭐ **Relation to the median nerve:** **lateral** to the artery in the **axilla** → **crosses in front** of it in the **middle of the arm** → **medial** to it at the **elbow**.

### Branches
1. ⭐ **Profunda brachii** — **first and largest**; runs in the **radial groove with the radial nerve**; gives **anterior and posterior descending branches**.
2. **Superior ulnar collateral** — middle of the arm; accompanies the **ulnar nerve behind the medial epicondyle**.
3. **Inferior ulnar collateral** — just above the elbow; passes **in front of the medial epicondyle**.

### Why the brachial artery is used for blood pressure
1. It is **superficial** in front of the elbow; pulsations felt just **medial to the biceps tendon**.
2. The **cubital fossa** gives a **flat area** for the stethoscope.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cephalic → deltopectoral groove → clavipectoral fascia → axillary vein; basilic + venae comitantes → axillary vein at teres major.
- Brachial artery: lower border of teres major → neck of radius (cubital fossa); on brachialis; superficial.
- Median nerve: lateral → crosses anterior (mid-arm) → medial at elbow.
- Branches: profunda brachii (largest, with radial nerve), superior and inferior ulnar collaterals.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the brachial artery: beginning, course, end and branches.</summary>

**Answer:** It begins as the continuation of the axillary artery at the lower border of teres major, runs on the medial border of brachialis (superficial, just deep to the deep fascia, with venae comitantes), and ends in the cubital fossa opposite the neck of the radius by dividing into radial and ulnar arteries. Branches: profunda brachii (first and largest, with the radial nerve in the radial groove), superior ulnar collateral (with the ulnar nerve behind the medial epicondyle) and inferior ulnar collateral (in front of the medial epicondyle), plus muscular and nutrient branches.

**Why:** The full artery template.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the relation of the median nerve to the brachial artery.</summary>

**Answer:** In the axilla the median nerve is formed lateral to the axillary artery; in the middle of the arm it crosses anterior to the brachial artery; at the elbow it lies medial to the artery.

**Why:** Lateral → front → medial.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is auscultation of the brachial artery used to record blood pressure?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it is superficial in front of the elbow and its pulsations are easily felt just medial to the biceps tendon, and the cubital fossa provides a flat area to place the stethoscope over the artery.

**Why:** Two reasons from the lecture.
</details>

## 5. Anastomosis around the elbow

Formed by **8 branches**: **descending** (profunda brachii branches, superior and inferior ulnar collaterals) and **recurrent** (from radial, ulnar and posterior interosseous arteries).

| Side | Recurrent (from below) | Anastomoses with (from above) |
|---|---|---|
| **Medial, anterior** | **anterior ulnar recurrent** (ulnar a.) — in front of medial epicondyle | **inferior ulnar collateral** |
| **Medial, posterior** | **posterior ulnar recurrent** (ulnar a.) — behind medial epicondyle | **superior ulnar collateral** |
| **Lateral, anterior** | **radial recurrent** (radial a.) — in front of lateral epicondyle | **anterior descending branch of profunda brachii** |
| **Lateral, posterior** | **posterior interosseous recurrent** — behind lateral epicondyle | **posterior descending branch of profunda brachii** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 8 arteries: 4 descending (from brachial/profunda) + 4 recurrent (radial, ulnar ×2, posterior interosseous).
- Medial side = ulnar recurrents + ulnar collaterals (anterior ↔ inferior, posterior ↔ superior).
- Lateral side = radial recurrent ↔ anterior descending; posterior interosseous recurrent ↔ posterior descending.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the anastomosis around the elbow joint.</summary>

**Answer:** It is formed by 8 arteries. Medially: the anterior ulnar recurrent artery (in front of the medial epicondyle) anastomoses with the inferior ulnar collateral; the posterior ulnar recurrent (behind the medial epicondyle) with the superior ulnar collateral. Laterally: the radial recurrent (in front of the lateral epicondyle) with the anterior descending branch of the profunda brachii; the posterior interosseous recurrent (behind the lateral epicondyle) with the posterior descending branch of the profunda brachii.

**Why:** Four pairs = eight arteries.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the importance of the anastomosis around the elbow?</summary>

**Answer:** It provides a collateral circulation to the forearm and hand if the brachial artery is blocked or ligated below the origin of its collateral branches — e.g. hand perfusion may be preserved even when the radial pulse is lost in a supracondylar fracture.

**Why:** Links anatomy to the applied part.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which artery anastomoses with the posterior ulnar recurrent artery?</summary>

**Answer:** The superior ulnar collateral artery (branch of the brachial artery), behind the medial epicondyle.

**Why:** Posterior with superior; anterior with inferior.
</details>

## 6. The elbow joint & carrying angle

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Type** | synovial, **hinge** (uniaxial); **compound** (3 bones) |
| **Articular surfaces** | **trochlea & capitulum** of humerus; **trochlear notch of ulna & head of radius** |
| **Capsule** | **thin in front and behind**, **thick at the sides** |
| **Radial collateral ligament** | triangular; apex → lateral epicondyle; base → **annular ligament** |
| **Ulnar collateral ligament** | triangular; apex → medial epicondyle; base → coronoid to olecranon; ⭐ **crossed by the ulnar nerve** |
| **Movements** | **flexion & extension** |

### Carrying angle
- The angle between the long axes of the **arm and forearm** when the elbow is **fully extended** — about ⭐ **15°**, **wider in females**.
- **Importance:** lets the forearm **clear the hip** when swinging during walking; important in **carrying objects**.
- **Decreased = cubitus varus**; **increased = cubitus valgus**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Elbow: synovial hinge, compound; trochlea + capitulum with trochlear notch + radial head.
- Capsule thin front/back, thick at sides; two collateral ligaments; ulnar nerve crosses the UCL.
- Carrying angle ≈ 15°, wider in females; ↓ varus, ↑ valgus.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the elbow joint (type, articular surfaces, capsule, ligaments, movements).</summary>

**Answer:** A compound synovial hinge (uniaxial) joint between the trochlea and capitulum of the humerus and the trochlear notch of the ulna and head of the radius. The capsule is thin in front and behind and thick at the sides. It has a radial collateral ligament (apex at the lateral epicondyle, base to the annular ligament) and an ulnar collateral ligament (apex at the medial epicondyle, base from coronoid to olecranon; crossed by the ulnar nerve). Movements: flexion and extension.

**Why:** The standard joint template.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define the carrying angle and state its importance.</summary>

**Answer:** The angle between the long axes of the arm and forearm when the elbow is fully extended, about 15° and wider in females. It permits the forearm to clear the hip during walking and is important in carrying objects. A decrease is cubitus varus; an increase is cubitus valgus.

**Why:** Definition + value + importance + abnormalities.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the capsule of the elbow thick at the sides?</summary>

**Answer:** As in every hinge joint, it is strengthened at the sides by the strong radial and ulnar collateral ligaments, which prevent side-to-side movement, while it is thin in front and behind to allow flexion and extension.

**Why:** Structure fits function.
</details>

## 7. Proximal radio-ulnar joint, pronation & supination

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Type** | synovial, **pivot** (uniaxial) |
| **Surfaces** | **head of radius** with the **radial notch of ulna + annular ligament** |
| **Capsule** | above: continuous with the elbow capsule (⭐ **joint cavities continuous**); below: loosely attached to the **neck of radius** |
| **Annular ligament** | strong, curved; forms **¾ of the circle** with the radial notch |
| **Quadrate ligament** | thin band from the lower border of the radial notch to the **neck of radius**; closes the joint inferomedially |
| **Movements** | **supination & pronation** |

| | **Pronation** | **Supination** |
|---|---|---|
| Initiated by | **brachioradialis** (full supination → midprone) | **brachioradialis** (full pronation → midprone) |
| Completed by | **pronator teres + pronator quadratus** | **supinator** (elbow extended) + **biceps** (elbow flexed) |
| Gravity | helps (unscrewing) | antigravity (screwing) → more powerful muscles |
| Nerve | **median** | **radial** (+ musculocutaneous for biceps) |

⭐ The **axis of movement is the ulna** (the radius rotates around it).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Proximal radio-ulnar = pivot joint; annular ligament forms ¾ of the ring; cavity continuous with the elbow.
- Brachioradialis brings the forearm to midprone from either extreme.
- Pronation: pronator teres + quadratus (median n.). Supination: supinator (extended) + biceps (flexed).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the proximal radio-ulnar joint.</summary>

**Answer:** A synovial pivot (uniaxial) joint between the head of the radius and the ring formed by the radial notch of the ulna and the annular ligament (which forms ¾ of the circle). The capsule is continuous above with that of the elbow (their cavities communicate) and loosely attached below to the neck of the radius; the quadrate ligament closes it inferomedially. Movements: supination and pronation.

**Why:** Type, surfaces, capsule, ligaments, movement.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the muscles producing pronation and supination.</summary>

**Answer:** Both are initiated by brachioradialis, which brings the forearm to the midprone position. Pronation is completed by pronator teres and pronator quadratus (median nerve) and is helped by gravity. Supination is completed by supinator when the elbow is extended and by biceps when the elbow is flexed; it is an antigravity movement done by more powerful muscles.

**Why:** Initiated by / completed by.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the axis of pronation and supination?</summary>

**Answer:** The ulna — the radius rotates around it.

**Why:** A one-line viva question.
</details>

## 8. The cubital fossa

- **Definition:** a **triangular intermuscular space in front of the elbow**; base above, apex below.

| Boundary | Structure |
|---|---|
| **Base** | line between the **two humeral epicondyles** |
| **Medial** | **pronator teres** |
| **Lateral** | **brachioradialis** |
| **Floor** | **brachialis** (above, medial) + **supinator** (below, lateral) |
| **Roof** | skin; superficial fascia with **basilic vein + medial cutaneous nerve of forearm**, **cephalic vein + lateral cutaneous nerve of forearm**, **median cubital vein**; deep fascia reinforced by the **bicipital aponeurosis** |

### Contents (medial → lateral)
1. **Median nerve** — leaves between the **two heads of pronator teres**.
2. **Brachial artery** end + start of **radial** (leaves **superficial** to pronator teres) and **ulnar** (leaves **deep** to pronator teres) arteries.
3. **Biceps tendon** — to the radial tuberosity.
4. **Radial nerve** (leaves deep to brachioradialis) + **posterior interosseous** branch (pierces **supinator**).

Mnemonic from lateral to medial: ⭐ **T-A-N** = **T**endon, **A**rtery, **N**erve. Also contains the **epitrochlear lymph node** in front of the medial epicondyle.

### Rules of the elbow region
- **Rule 1:** **nerves never pass between bones** — they pass **between two heads of muscles**: radial (supinator), median (pronator teres), ulnar (flexor carpi ulnaris).
- **Rule 2:** **arteries may pass between two bones** — e.g. the **posterior interosseous artery** between ulna and radius.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Borders: pronator teres (medial), brachioradialis (lateral); floor brachialis + supinator; roof has the median cubital vein and bicipital aponeurosis.
- Contents medial → lateral: median nerve, brachial artery (→ radial, ulnar), biceps tendon, radial nerve.
- Pronator teres: median nerve between its heads, radial artery superficial, ulnar artery deep.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the boundaries of the cubital fossa.</summary>

**Answer:** Base: a line between the two epicondyles of the humerus; medial border: pronator teres; lateral border: brachioradialis; apex: meeting of the two borders; floor: brachialis (above and medially) and supinator (below and laterally); roof: skin, superficial fascia (basilic vein with medial cutaneous nerve of forearm, cephalic vein with lateral cutaneous nerve of forearm, median cubital vein) and deep fascia reinforced by the bicipital aponeurosis.

**Why:** The complete boundary answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the contents of the cubital fossa from medial to lateral and how each leaves.</summary>

**Answer:** (1) Median nerve — leaves between the two heads of pronator teres; (2) end of the brachial artery with the beginning of the radial artery (leaves superficial to pronator teres) and ulnar artery (leaves deep to pronator teres); (3) biceps tendon — to the radial tuberosity; (4) radial nerve — leaves deep to brachioradialis, its posterior interosseous branch pierces supinator. It also contains the epitrochlear lymph node.

**Why:** Order + exit route of each.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. State the two rules of nerves and arteries around the elbow.</summary>

**Answer:** Rule 1: nerves never pass between bones but pass around them, between two heads of muscles — radial between the heads of supinator, median between the heads of pronator teres, ulnar between the heads of flexor carpi ulnaris. Rule 2: arteries may pass between two bones, e.g. the posterior interosseous artery between ulna and radius.

**Why:** Two rules with examples.
</details>

## 9. Applied anatomy — fractures of the humerus

### Surgical neck fracture
- **Case:** 70-year-old woman, **fall on outstretched hand**, can't lift the arm; ecchymosis, crepitus, intact radial pulse.
- **High risk:** elderly (**osteoporosis**), seizures, electrocution.
- **At risk:** ⭐ **axillary nerve** (runs **5 cm below the acromion**) + **posterior circumflex humeral artery**.
- **Deformity:** proximal fragment **abducted / externally rotated** (as the lecture puts it, "**pectoralis major pull**").
- **Why "surgical":** high risk of **neurovascular injury** (vs the anatomical neck).

### Midshaft fracture
- **Case:** 35-year-old falls from scaffolding: deformity mid-arm, **weak wrist extension**, **numb dorsum of hand**.
- **"Hanging arm"** deformity (distal fragment hangs by gravity).
- ⭐ **Radial nerve** in the **spiral groove** (**10–14 cm below the acromion**) → test wrist/finger extension.
- **Triceps usually spared** (its branches arise proximal to the groove).
- Proximal fragment pulled **anteriorly by pectoralis major**; distal fragment pulled **medially by brachialis/biceps**.

### Displacement depends on the site
| Site | Proximal segment | Distal segment |
|---|---|---|
| **Above the bicipital groove** | **abducted** by greater tuberosity muscles (mainly **supraspinatus**) | **adducted** by muscles of the bicipital groove |
| **Above the deltoid tuberosity** | **adducted** by muscles of the bicipital groove | **abducted & pulled up** by **deltoid** |
| **Below the deltoid tuberosity** | **abducted** by **deltoid** | **adducted & pulled up** by **biceps & triceps** |

### Structures in contact with the humerus (4 sites)
| Site | Nerve | Artery | Deformity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surgical neck** | **axillary** | posterior circumflex humeral | **flat shoulder** |
| **Midshaft (spiral groove)** | **radial** | profunda brachii | **wrist drop** |
| **Supracondylar** | **median** | **brachial** | (Volkmann's) |
| **Medial epicondyle** | **ulnar** | ulnar collateral | **claw hand** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Surgical neck → axillary nerve + posterior circumflex humeral artery (flat shoulder).
- Midshaft → radial nerve + profunda brachii (wrist drop; triceps spared).
- Supracondylar → brachial artery + median nerve; medial epicondyle → ulnar nerve (claw hand).
- Displacement depends on the site relative to the bicipital groove and deltoid tuberosity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain how displacement of the fragments in a humeral shaft fracture depends on the site.</summary>

**Answer:** Above the bicipital groove: the proximal segment is abducted by the muscles on the greater tuberosity (mainly supraspinatus) and the distal segment adducted by the muscles attached to the bicipital groove. Above the deltoid tuberosity (below the groove): the proximal segment is adducted by the bicipital groove muscles and the distal segment abducted and pulled up by the deltoid. Below the deltoid tuberosity: the proximal segment is abducted by the deltoid and the distal segment adducted and pulled up by biceps and triceps.

**Why:** The three-row table is a guaranteed exam question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What structures are in direct contact with the humerus and liable to injury in fractures?</summary>

**Answer:** Surgical neck — axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery (flat shoulder); midshaft/spiral groove — radial nerve and profunda brachii artery (wrist drop); supracondylar region — brachial artery and median nerve; medial epicondyle — ulnar nerve and ulnar collateral artery (claw hand).

**Why:** 4 sites × nerve + artery.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is triceps often spared in a midshaft humeral fracture with radial nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the radial nerve gives its branches to triceps (the long head in the axilla, and branches to the other heads) proximal to the fracture site in the spiral groove, so elbow extension is preserved while wrist and finger extension are lost.

**Why:** Proximal branches escape the injury.
</details>

## 10. Paediatric supracondylar fracture & Volkmann's contracture

- **Case:** 6-year-old falls from monkey bars on an outstretched hand; swollen elbow held in extension; **no radial pulse but warm, pink fingers**.
- **High risk:** ages **5–7** (weak supracondylar area).
- **At risk:** **brachial artery** (pulse lost in **5–15% of type III**); median/radial/ulnar nerves — ⭐ **anterior interosseous nerve most commonly injured** (test the **"OK sign"**).
- **"Snapping pop"** during injury → severe displacement.
- **Anatomy:** **thin cortex** between the coronoid and olecranon fossae; distal humerus physis gives **80% of elbow growth**.
- **Brachial artery** is **anteromedial** to the fracture; ⭐ **pulse loss ≠ ischaemia** (collaterals may keep the hand perfused).
- Nerve tests: **AIN — OK sign**; **radial — thumb/wrist extension**; **ulnar — finger abduction**.
- **Extension type (95%):** distal fragment **posterior** (triceps pull). **Flexion type (5%):** anterior, rare, higher neurovascular risk.

### Volkmann's ischaemic contracture
1. The proximal fragment is displaced **forward and downward**, **compressing the brachial artery and median nerve**.
2. If the collaterals can't compensate → **ischaemia and necrosis of the forearm flexor muscles**.
3. Muscles replaced by **fibrous tissue** → thinning and shortening.
4. Result: ⭐ **flexion deformity of the wrist and fingers**.

### Three-point relationship at the elbow
- **Supracondylar fracture:** lies **above** the olecranon and epicondyles → their **triangle is not disturbed**.
- **Elbow dislocation:** the olecranon comes **in line with the epicondyles** → triangle **disturbed**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Supracondylar fracture: age 5–7; brachial artery and AIN at risk; 95% extension type.
- Pulse loss does not always mean ischaemia (collaterals).
- Volkmann's: brachial artery compression → flexor ischaemia → fibrosis → flexion deformity of wrist and fingers.
- Supracondylar fracture keeps the bony triangle; dislocation disturbs it.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is Volkmann's ischaemic contracture?</summary>

**Answer:** In a supracondylar fracture the proximal fragment is displaced forward and downward, compressing the brachial artery and median nerve. When the collateral circulation cannot compensate, the forearm flexor muscles become ischaemic and necrotic and are replaced by fibrous tissue, which thins and shortens them, producing a fixed flexion deformity of the wrist and fingers.

**Why:** Cause → ischaemia → fibrosis → deformity.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the bony triangle of the elbow preserved in supracondylar fracture but disturbed in dislocation?</summary>

**Answer:** The supracondylar fracture lies above the olecranon and the two epicondyles, so they stay in their normal triangular relationship. In elbow dislocation the olecranon moves and comes to lie more or less in line with the epicondyles, disturbing the triangle.

**Why:** A classic "explain why" question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why may the hand be warm and pink despite an absent radial pulse in a supracondylar fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the anastomosis around the elbow provides collateral circulation that can preserve hand perfusion even when the brachial artery is compressed — pulse loss is not the same as ischaemia.

**Why:** Anastomosis in action.
</details>

## 11. Radial head subluxation (nursemaid's / pulled elbow)

- **Case:** 2-year-old girl refuses to use her arm after being **pulled up by the wrist**; arm held in **slight flexion and pronation**; no swelling.
- **Mechanism:** ⭐ **axial traction on a pronated forearm**.
- **Pathognomonic:** **no voluntary supination** (won't reach for toys).
- **Age:** peak **1–4 years**.
- **Why children:** the radial head is an **immature narrow ovoid** (under 5 years) and the **annular ligament is thin, elastic, loose (tubular)** → it slips over the head into the **radiocapitellar joint**.
- **Reduction:** ⭐ **supination + flexion** (the lab also describes pressure on the radial head while supinating and extending).
- **Adult equivalent:** elbow dislocation (ligaments tear rather than stretch). **No tenderness over the radial head** distinguishes it from fracture.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pulled elbow: traction on a pronated forearm in a 1–4-year-old.
- Immature radial head + loose annular ligament → ligament slips into the joint.
- No supination; reduce by supination + flexion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why are young children vulnerable to radial head subluxation?</summary>

**Answer:** Under 5 years the radial head is an immature narrow ovoid, not fully developed, and the annular ligament is thin, elastic, loose and tubular; so when the child is lifted by the hand with the forearm pronated, the head slips partially out of the annular ligament, which becomes trapped in the radiocapitellar joint.

**Why:** Anatomy of the immature elbow.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the mechanism, presentation and reduction of pulled elbow.</summary>

**Answer:** Mechanism: axial traction on a pronated forearm (e.g. lifting a child by the hands). Presentation: a 1–4-year-old refuses to use the arm, held in slight flexion and pronation, with no swelling or deformity and no voluntary supination. Reduction: supination with flexion lifts the annular ligament out of the joint.

**Why:** Case → mechanism → sign → treatment.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the pathognomonic finding of pulled elbow?</summary>

**Answer:** Absence of voluntary supination — the child will not reach for toys with the affected hand.

**Why:** One-line viva answer.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Long head of biceps** = supraglenoid (**intra**capsular); **long head of triceps** = infraglenoid (**extra**capsular).
- **Main elbow flexor = brachialis**, not biceps; biceps is the **powerful supinator**.
- **Lateral head** of triceps is **above** the spiral groove; **medial head below**.
- Median nerve: lateral → **crosses in front** → medial to the brachial artery.
- Brachial artery ends **opposite the neck of the radius**.
- Radial artery leaves **superficial** to pronator teres; ulnar artery **deep**.
- Carrying angle ↓ = **varus**, ↑ = **valgus**.
- Axillary nerve **5 cm** below the acromion; radial nerve in the spiral groove **10–14 cm**.
- Supracondylar: **AIN** most commonly injured; **95% extension type**.

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. Septa → **anterior** (biceps, coracobrachialis, brachialis; musculocutaneous; brachial a.) and **posterior** (triceps; radial n.; profunda brachii).
2. **Biceps** = supinator + flexor; **brachialis** = main flexor (double supply); **coracobrachialis** = weak flexor/adductor.
3. **Triceps**: long (infraglenoid), lateral (above groove), medial (below groove) → olecranon; **student's elbow** = subcutaneous bursitis.
4. Cephalic → deltopectoral groove → axillary vein; basilic → axillary vein at teres major.
5. **Brachial a.**: teres major → neck of radius; profunda brachii (largest, with radial nerve), superior and inferior ulnar collaterals.
6. Elbow anastomosis: **8 arteries** (4 descending + 4 recurrent).
7. Elbow = compound synovial **hinge**; ulnar nerve crosses the UCL; **carrying angle 15°**.
8. Proximal radio-ulnar = **pivot**; annular ligament ¾ ring; axis = **ulna**.
9. Cubital fossa: pronator teres / brachioradialis; **T-A-N** from lateral; median nerve between pronator teres heads.
10. Fractures: surgical neck → **axillary**; midshaft → **radial**; supracondylar → **brachial a. + median**; medial epicondyle → **ulnar**.
11. **Volkmann's** = flexor ischaemia → fibrosis → flexion deformity.
12. **Pulled elbow**: traction on pronated forearm, 1–4 y; reduce by **supination + flexion**.
