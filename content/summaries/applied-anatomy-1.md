# Applied Anatomy of the Upper Limb I — Exam Night Review

A case-based lecture: every topic opens with a clinical scenario, then the anatomy that explains it. It covers shoulder dislocations, AC and SC joint injuries, clavicle fractures, the degenerative shoulder (frozen shoulder, rotator cuff tears, impingement), brachial plexus and nerve injuries, humeral fractures, and nursemaid's elbow. After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

## 1. Shoulder dislocation — anterior and posterior

### Anterior dislocation
**Case:** a 22-year-old man falls in football with his arm in **abduction + external rotation**; he has a **"squared-off" shoulder**, pain on movement, and the **humeral head palpable anteriorly**.

- **Mechanism**: abduction + external rotation.
- **Primary restraint failure**: **anteroinferior labral tear**.
- **Secondary damage**: **humeral head impaction**.
- **Nerve at risk**: ⭐ **axillary nerve (15–20% of cases)**.
- **Static stabilizers**: **glenoid labrum** (deepens the socket by **50%**) and the **inferior glenohumeral ligament (IGHL)**.
- **Dynamic stabilizers**: **rotator cuff** and **long head of biceps**.

### Posterior dislocation
**Case:** a 45-year-old man after **electrocution**, with severe pain, inability to lift the arm, arm **fixed in internal rotation** with limited external rotation.

- **Mechanism**: **seizure or electric shock** (violent muscle contractions).
- ⭐ **Often missed** — **up to 50%** are overlooked at first presentation.
- **Affected structures**: **posterior labrum** (tear); **teres minor and infraspinatus** (injured by the forceful internal rotation).
- The **glenoid is naturally retroverted**, so posterior dislocation is **less common** but carries a **high risk of chronic instability if missed**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Anterior = abduction + external rotation, squared-off shoulder, axillary nerve. Posterior = seizure/electric shock, arm locked in **internal rotation**, **missed in up to 50%**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Anterior: ABD + ER, squared-off shoulder, anteroinferior labral tear + head impaction, axillary nerve 15–20%.
- Static stabilizers: labrum (+50% depth), IGHL. Dynamic: rotator cuff, long head of biceps.
- Posterior: seizure/electrocution, arm fixed in IR, up to 50% missed; posterior labrum, teres minor & infraspinatus; retroverted glenoid.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. A footballer falls with his arm abducted and externally rotated and now has a squared-off shoulder. Explain the anatomy of the injury.</summary>

**Answer:** This is an anterior dislocation. The primary restraint to fail is the anteroinferior labrum (labral tear), and the humeral head may be impacted as secondary damage. The axillary nerve is at risk in 15–20% of cases. The main static stabilizers are the glenoid labrum (which deepens the socket by 50%) and the inferior glenohumeral ligament; the dynamic stabilizers are the rotator cuff and the long head of biceps.

**Why:** Mechanism → failed structures → nerve at risk → stabilizers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is posterior shoulder dislocation dangerous even though it is uncommon?</summary>

**Answer:** It follows seizures or electric shock and is often missed — up to 50% are overlooked initially. Because the glenoid is naturally retroverted, posterior dislocation is less common, but a missed one carries a high risk of chronic instability. It injures the posterior labrum and may injure teres minor and infraspinatus.

**Why:** "Often missed" + "chronic instability" are the key ideas.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the static and dynamic stabilizers of the shoulder.</summary>

**Answer:** Static: the glenoid labrum (deepens the socket by 50%) and the inferior glenohumeral ligament. Dynamic: the rotator cuff and the long head of biceps.

**Why:** A short, direct list question.
</details>

## 2. AC and SC joints, and movements of the shoulder girdle

### Comparison
| | **SC joint** | **AC joint** |
|---|---|---|
| Type | synovial, **modified saddle** | synovial, **plane** |
| Stability | **costoclavicular ligament** | **coracoclavicular ligament** |
| Intra-articular disc | **complete** | **incomplete** |
| Function | ⭐ **main joint for shoulder-girdle movements** | shares a little |

### Movements of the shoulder girdle
| Movement | Muscles |
|---|---|
| **Elevation** | upper trapezius, levator scapulae |
| **Depression** | pectoralis minor, pectoralis major |
| **Upward rotation** (glenoid faces up) | upper & lower trapezius, serratus anterior |
| **Downward rotation** | levator scapulae, rhomboid major & minor |
| **Protraction** | serratus anterior, pectoralis minor |
| **Retraction** | middle trapezius, rhomboid major & minor |

### AC joint injury
**Case:** a cyclist falls **directly onto the shoulder**: pain over the AC joint, **visible step-off**, pain on **cross-body adduction**.
- **Mechanism**: direct trauma (fall onto the shoulder) or indirect (fall on outstretched hand).
- **Classic sign**: ⭐ **"piano key" sign** — the clavicle end is **reducible with downward pressure**.
- **High-risk**: contact athletes (hockey), cyclists, military.
- **Coracoclavicular ligaments (conoid + trapezoid)** = **primary vertical stabilizers** — they **prevent superior displacement** of the clavicle; injury → **step deformity**.
- **AC ligaments** = **primary horizontal stabilizers**.

### SC joint injury
**Case:** direct impact to the front of the chest; pain and swelling over the medial clavicle.
- ⭐ The **only synovial joint connecting the axial to the appendicular skeleton**.
- **Articular surfaces**: sternal end of clavicle + manubrium + **1st costal cartilage**.
- **Stabilizers**: anterior/posterior SC ligaments (prevent anterior/posterior displacement); **costoclavicular** (resists superior displacement); **interclavicular** (joins the two clavicles).
- **Vital posterior relations**: **brachiocephalic vein, subclavian artery, trachea/oesophagus, pleura**.
- **Anterior dislocations are more common but less dangerous**; **posterior dislocations risk life-threatening mediastinal injury**.
- 🚩 **Red flags**: **dyspnoea / dysphagia** (mediastinal compression).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- SC: modified saddle, costoclavicular ligament, complete disc, main girdle joint. AC: plane, coracoclavicular ligament, incomplete disc.
- AC injury: piano key sign; CC ligaments (conoid + trapezoid) = vertical stabilizers, AC ligaments = horizontal.
- SC joint: only synovial link of axial to appendicular skeleton; posterior dislocation → mediastinal danger (dyspnoea, dysphagia).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the sternoclavicular and acromioclavicular joints.</summary>

**Answer:** SC joint: synovial modified saddle, stabilized by the costoclavicular ligament, complete intra-articular disc, the main joint for shoulder-girdle movements. AC joint: synovial plane, stabilized by the coracoclavicular ligament, incomplete disc, shares only a little in girdle movements.

**Why:** Four-point comparison table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the step deformity and piano key sign of AC joint injury.</summary>

**Answer:** The coracoclavicular ligaments (conoid and trapezoid) are the primary vertical stabilizers that prevent superior displacement of the clavicle; when they are torn the lateral clavicle rides up, giving a step deformity. Pressing it down reduces it (piano key sign). The AC ligaments are the horizontal stabilizers.

**Why:** Links the ligament to the sign.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is posterior SC dislocation more dangerous than anterior?</summary>

**Answer:** Posterior to the SC joint lie the brachiocephalic vein, subclavian artery, trachea, oesophagus and pleura, so a posterior dislocation can cause life-threatening mediastinal (vascular or airway) injury — red flags are dyspnoea and dysphagia. Anterior dislocations are more common but less dangerous.

**Why:** Relations explain the danger.
</details>

## 3. Clavicle fractures

**Case:** a 15-year-old falls off his bike onto the shoulder and holds the arm **adducted and supported at the elbow**, with deformity over the clavicle.

- **Mechanism**: **fall onto the shoulder (85%)** or direct trauma.
- **Classic sign**: **"sternal droop"** — the shoulder slumps down after loss of the clavicular strut.
- **High-risk**: adolescents (midshaft), newborns (birth trauma), contact athletes.

| Site | Frequency | Danger |
|---|---|---|
| **Medial 1/3** | least common (**5%**) | **mediastinal injury** |
| **Middle 1/3** | most common (**80%**) | subcutaneous, vulnerable |
| **Lateral 1/3** | — | **high nonunion risk** (coracoclavicular ligament disruption) |

- **Critical adjacent structures**: **subclavian vessels and brachial plexus** (posterolateral to the medial clavicle), **pleura/lung** (pneumothorax).

### Lecturer's questions
- **Functions**: transmits forces from the upper limb to the axial skeleton; a **strut** holding the arm away from the trunk (the arm hangs supported mainly by **trapezius**); gives muscle attachments.
- **Why is it commonly fractured?** It is **almost subcutaneous** and **relatively fixed**.
- **Why is the junction of the medial 2/3 and lateral 1/3 the commonest site?** It is the **thinnest point**:
  1. Between **two different curvatures**.
  2. Between **two different cross-sectional shapes**.
  3. Weakened by the **subclavius groove** on its inferior surface.
  4. Weakened by the **nutrient foramen**.
  5. **Unprotected** — no muscle attachment.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Fall onto the shoulder (85%); sternal droop; patient supports the adducted arm at the elbow.
- Medial 5% (mediastinum), middle 80%, lateral → nonunion (CC ligaments).
- At risk: subclavian vessels, brachial plexus, pleura/lung.
- Commonest site = thinnest point: 2 curvatures, 2 cross-sections, subclavius groove, nutrient foramen, no muscle attachment.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the commonest site of clavicle fracture at the junction of the medial 2/3 and lateral 1/3?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it is the thinnest point of the clavicle: it lies between two different curvatures and two different cross-sectional shapes, it is weakened by the subclavius groove on its inferior surface and by the nutrient foramen, and it is unprotected, having no muscle attachment.

**Why:** Five reasons from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the functions of the clavicle and why is it commonly fractured?</summary>

**Answer:** It transmits forces from the upper limb to the axial skeleton, acts as a strut holding the arm free from the trunk (supported principally by trapezius), and provides muscle attachments. It is commonly fractured because it is almost subcutaneous and relatively fixed.

**Why:** Two lecturer questions in one.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Classify clavicle fractures by site and give the risk of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Medial third — least common (5%), high risk of mediastinal injury. Middle third — most common (80%), subcutaneous and vulnerable. Lateral third — high risk of nonunion because of coracoclavicular ligament disruption.

**Why:** Site + frequency + risk.
</details>

## 4. The degenerative shoulder

### Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis)
**Case:** a 55-year-old diabetic with 6 months of gradual pain and stiffness, night pain, difficulty dressing and reaching behind the back; **active AND passive ROM globally restricted**, especially external rotation and abduction.
- **Classic triad**: **pain + stiffness + loss of ROM (active and passive)**.
- **High-risk**: ⭐ **diabetics (4× risk)**, **women aged 40–60**, thyroid disorders.
- **Capsule thickened and contracted** (especially the **coracohumeral ligament**); **synovium inflamed** early; **axillary recess obliterated** → limits abduction.
- ⭐ **Loss of external rotation is pathognomonic** (coracohumeral ligament contracture).
- **No primary joint damage** — articular cartilage intact (unlike osteoarthritis).

### Rotator cuff tears
**Case:** a 60-year-old woman with 6 months of pain, night pain, weakness lifting and combing hair; **positive drop-arm sign**, weak abduction/external rotation.
- **Cause**: **degeneration (age > 40)** or trauma (fall on outstretched arm).
- **Classic triad**: **pain + weakness + limited active ROM (passive ROM preserved)**.
- **High-risk**: manual labourers, overhead athletes (baseball pitchers).
- **Supraspinatus** is the most commonly torn; infraspinatus/teres minor → ER weakness; subscapularis (rare isolated) → IR weakness; coracoacromial arch contributes via impingement.
- **"Rotator cable"** = thickening of cuff fibres; **critical zone** = **hypovascular area near the supraspinatus insertion** → prone to degeneration.

### Impingement syndrome
**Case:** a 45-year-old construction worker with overhead pain, night pain on that side, **painful arc 60–120°**, weak supraspinatus testing, positive Hawkins.
- **Classic triad**: **painful arc + night pain + weakness with overhead activity**.
- **Mechanism**: compression of cuff tendons and the **subacromial bursa** under the **coracoacromial arch**.
- **High-risk**: overhead athletes (swimmers), labourers, diabetics.
- Supraspinatus most commonly impinged; bursitis; **Bigliani acromion types**: flat (I), curved (II), **hooked (III) → ↑ risk**.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap — the passive ROM rule:** frozen shoulder = **active AND passive** lost; cuff tear = **active** lost, **passive preserved**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Frozen shoulder: pain + stiffness + loss of active AND passive ROM; diabetics 4×; ER loss pathognomonic (coracohumeral ligament); axillary recess obliterated; cartilage intact.
- Cuff tear: pain + weakness + limited active ROM, passive preserved; supraspinatus; critical hypovascular zone; drop-arm sign.
- Impingement: painful arc 60–120° + night pain + overhead weakness; coracoacromial arch; Bigliani III hooked.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the affected structures in frozen shoulder.</summary>

**Answer:** The glenohumeral capsule is thickened and contracted, especially the coracohumeral ligament; the synovium is inflamed in early stages; the axillary recess is obliterated, limiting abduction. Loss of external rotation is pathognomonic because of coracohumeral ligament contracture. There is no primary joint damage — the articular cartilage stays intact, unlike osteoarthritis.

**Why:** Capsule, synovium, recess, ER, cartilage.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do you distinguish a rotator cuff tear from frozen shoulder?</summary>

**Answer:** In a cuff tear, active ROM is limited with weakness (positive drop-arm sign) but passive ROM is preserved; in frozen shoulder, both active and passive ROM are lost, especially external rotation.

**Why:** The passive ROM rule.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the critical zone of the rotator cuff?</summary>

**Answer:** A hypovascular area near the insertion of the supraspinatus tendon that is prone to degeneration — which is why supraspinatus is the most commonly torn cuff tendon.

**Why:** Anatomy (blood supply) explains the pathology.
</details>

## 5. Brachial plexus injuries

**Case:** after a difficult forceps delivery, a baby's arm is **medially rotated and adducted**, with the **forearm pronated and extended**.

### Branches (with roots)
- **Roots**: dorsal scapular (C5), **long thoracic (C5–7)**; **upper trunk**: **suprascapular (C5,6)**, nerve to subclavius (C5,6).
- **Lateral cord**: lateral pectoral (C5–7), musculocutaneous (C5–7).
- **Medial cord**: medial pectoral (C8,T1), ulnar (C8,T1).
- **Posterior cord (5)**: upper and lower subscapular (C5,6), **axillary (C5,6)**, **radial (C5–T1)**, thoracodorsal.

### Erb's point
Meeting point of **six nerves**: the **ventral rami of C5 and C6**, the **two branches of the upper trunk** (suprascapular and nerve to subclavius), and the **two divisions of the upper trunk**.

### Upper trunk injury — Erb–Duchenne paralysis (C5, C6)
- **Causes**: stretching of the upper plexus when the **head and shoulder are forced apart** (fall on the shoulder) or **obstructed labour**.
- **Nerves**: **musculocutaneous, axillary, suprascapular**, nerve to subclavius.
- **Motor**: paralysis of shoulder **abductors**, elbow **flexors** and **supinators** → **flat shoulder + "policeman's (waiter's) tip" deformity** (**arm adducted and medially rotated, forearm extended and pronated**); wasting of scapular muscles and front of arm.
- **Sensory**: lower ½ of deltoid, lateral arm, lateral forearm, lateral hand and lateral 2 fingers.

### Lower trunk injury — Klumpke's palsy (C8, T1)
- **Causes**: the limb **suddenly pulled upward and away** from the body; **shoulder dystocia**.
- **Nerves/muscles**: intrinsic hand muscles of the **ulnar** nerve (interossei, medial 2 lumbricals) + some **median** C8/T1 muscles (thenar).
- **Motor**: **combined claw hand** with some **ape hand**; weak thenar muscles; all small hand muscles paralysed → **hyperextended MCP + flexed IP joints**.
- **Sensory**: **medial border of the forearm** and **medial 1½ digits**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Erb's point = 6 nerves meet (C5, C6 rami + 2 branches + 2 divisions of the upper trunk).
- Erb–Duchenne (C5,6): head–shoulder separation / obstructed labour → waiter's tip (arm adducted & MR, forearm extended & pronated), flat shoulder; lateral arm/forearm/hand sensory loss.
- Klumpke (C8,T1): arm pulled up / shoulder dystocia → combined claw hand ± ape hand; medial forearm & medial 1½ digits sensory loss.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe Erb–Duchenne paralysis (cause, nerves, deformity, sensory loss).</summary>

**Answer:** Injury to the C5 and C6 fibres of the upper trunk from stretching when the head and shoulder are forced apart (fall on the shoulder) or in obstructed labour. The musculocutaneous, axillary, suprascapular nerves and nerve to subclavius are affected, paralysing the shoulder abductors, elbow flexors and supinators — producing a flat shoulder and the policeman's (waiter's) tip deformity: arm adducted and medially rotated, forearm extended and pronated. Sensation is lost over the lower half of the deltoid, lateral arm, lateral forearm, lateral hand and lateral two fingers.

**Why:** The full clinical picture of the case.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe Klumpke's palsy.</summary>

**Answer:** Injury to the C8 and T1 fibres of the lower trunk when the limb is suddenly pulled upward and away from the body, or in shoulder dystocia. It paralyses the intrinsic hand muscles (ulnar-supplied interossei and medial lumbricals, and some median C8/T1 thenar muscles), producing a combined claw hand with some ape hand (hyperextended MCP and flexed IP joints). Sensation is lost along the medial forearm and medial 1½ digits.

**Why:** Mirror image of Erb's palsy.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is Erb's point?</summary>

**Answer:** The meeting point of six nerves in the brachial plexus: the ventral rami of C5 and C6, the two branches of the upper trunk (suprascapular nerve and nerve to subclavius) and the two divisions of the upper trunk.

**Why:** "Six nerves" and what they are.
</details>

## 6. Injuries of individual nerves

| Nerve (root) | Common lesion site | Muscles | Motor deficit | Sensory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Axillary (C5–6)** | **surgical neck fracture**, **inferior dislocation** | deltoid, teres minor | loss of abduction (**15–110°**), **deltoid atrophy → flat shoulder**, weak lateral rotation | **cap of the shoulder** |
| **Suprascapular (C5–6)** | **entrapment in the scapular notch** | supraspinatus, infraspinatus | weak **first 15°** of abduction, some weak ER | **none** |
| **Long thoracic (C5–7)** | lateral chest wall trauma, **mastectomy** (runs on the surface of serratus anterior) | serratus anterior | difficult overhead abduction, weak protraction, ⭐ **winged scapula** | **none** |
| **Thoracodorsal (C6–8)** | trauma/surgery of lateral chest wall | latissimus dorsi | weak adduction, **extension** (most affected), medial rotation | **none** |

### Deformity → nerve (the lecturer's table)
| Deformity | Nerve |
|---|---|
| Flat shoulder contour | **axillary** |
| Wrist drop | **radial** |
| Partial claw hand | **ulnar** |
| Benediction hand / ape-like hand | **median** |
| Winged scapula | **long thoracic** |
| Waiter's tip | **upper trunk (Erb–Duchenne)** |
| Combined claw hand | **lower trunk (Klumpke)** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Axillary: surgical neck / inferior dislocation → flat shoulder, abduction lost, shoulder-cap numbness.
- Suprascapular: scapular notch → weak first 15° abduction; no sensory loss.
- Long thoracic: mastectomy → winged scapula; no sensory loss.
- Thoracodorsal: latissimus → extension most affected; no sensory loss.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the effect of axillary nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Root C5–C6; usually injured by fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus or inferior dislocation of the shoulder. It supplies deltoid and teres minor, so there is loss of abduction (15–110°), deltoid atrophy with loss of the rounded shoulder contour, and weak lateral rotation; sensation is lost over the skin of the cap of the shoulder.

**Why:** Root, site, muscles, motor, sensory — the lecturer's format.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the effect of long thoracic nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Root C5, C6, C7; it runs on the superficial surface of serratus anterior and is damaged by lateral chest wall trauma or cut during mastectomy at the lateral aspect of the breast. Serratus anterior paralysis causes difficulty with overhead abduction, weak protraction and a winged scapula; there is no sensory loss.

**Why:** Winged scapula is the sign.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare suprascapular and thoracodorsal nerve injuries.</summary>

**Answer:** Suprascapular (C5–6) is entrapped in the scapular notch; supraspinatus and infraspinatus weakness gives difficulty initiating the first 15° of abduction and some weak external rotation. Thoracodorsal (C6–8) is injured in lateral chest wall trauma or surgery; latissimus dorsi weakness gives weak adduction, extension and medial rotation, extension being most affected. Neither causes sensory loss.

**Why:** Two motor-only nerves side by side.
</details>

## 7. Humeral fractures

### Surgical neck fracture
**Case:** a 70-year-old woman falls on an outstretched hand; cannot lift the arm; bruising, crepitus, radial pulse intact.
- **High-risk**: elderly (osteoporosis), seizures, electrocution.
- **At risk**: **axillary nerve** (runs **5 cm below the acromion**) + **posterior circumflex humeral artery**.
- **Deformity**: proximal fragment **abducted/externally rotated** (pectoralis major pull).
- Called **"surgical"** because of its **high risk of neurovascular injury** (vs the anatomical neck).

### Midshaft fracture
**Case:** a construction worker falls on his arm: mid-arm deformity, **weak wrist extension**, **numb dorsum of the hand**.
- **"Hanging arm"**: distal fragment hangs by gravity.
- ⭐ **Radial nerve** in the **spiral groove, 10–14 cm below the acromion**:
  - Proximal to the fracture it supplies **triceps** → triceps is **often spared**.
  - At the groove it is lacerated/stretched → test **wrist and finger extension** (wrist drop).
  - Distally it emerges lateral to brachioradialis (sensory to the **dorsum of the hand**).
- Proximal fragment pulled **anteriorly by pectoralis major**; distal fragment **medially by brachialis/biceps**.

### Displacement depends on the fracture level
| Fracture site | Proximal fragment | Distal fragment |
|---|---|---|
| **Above the bicipital groove** | **abducted** (muscles on greater tuberosity — mainly supraspinatus) | **adducted** (muscles on the bicipital groove) |
| **Above the deltoid tuberosity** | **adducted** (bicipital groove muscles) | **abducted & pulled up** (deltoid) |
| **Below the deltoid tuberosity** | **abducted** (deltoid) | **adducted & pulled up** (biceps & triceps) |

### Events at or near the mid-shaft of the humerus
Nutrient artery enters the humerus; **origin of brachialis**; **insertion of deltoid**; **insertion of coracobrachialis**; **radial nerve leaves the spiral groove**; **basilic vein pierces the deep fascia**; **median nerve crosses in front of the brachial artery**; **ulnar nerve + superior ulnar collateral artery pierce the medial intermuscular septum** to pass backwards.

### Structures in contact with the humerus (4 fracture sites)
| Fracture | Nerve | Artery | Deformity if the nerve is cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surgical neck** | axillary | posterior circumflex humeral | flat shoulder |
| **Midshaft (spiral groove)** | radial | profunda brachii | wrist drop |
| **Supracondylar** | median | brachial | — (Volkmann's risk) |
| **Medial epicondyle** | ulnar | (superior) ulnar collateral | claw hand |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Surgical neck: axillary nerve (5 cm below acromion) + posterior circumflex humeral artery.
- Midshaft: radial nerve in spiral groove (10–14 cm), wrist drop, triceps spared; profunda brachii.
- Displacement depends on level relative to the bicipital groove and deltoid tuberosity.
- 4 contact sites: surgical neck–axillary, midshaft–radial, supracondylar–median + brachial a., medial epicondyle–ulnar.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain how displacement of humeral shaft fragments depends on the fracture site.</summary>

**Answer:** Above the bicipital groove, the proximal fragment is abducted by muscles on the greater tuberosity (mainly supraspinatus) and the distal fragment adducted by muscles on the bicipital groove. Between the groove and the deltoid tuberosity, the proximal fragment is adducted by the bicipital-groove muscles and the distal fragment abducted and pulled up by deltoid. Below the deltoid tuberosity, the proximal fragment is abducted by deltoid and the distal fragment adducted and pulled up by biceps and triceps.

**Why:** The lecturer's table in words.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is triceps often spared in a midshaft humeral fracture with radial nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the radial nerve gives its branches to triceps proximal to the spiral groove (the long head is even supplied in the axilla), so a lesion at the groove, 10–14 cm below the acromion, spares triceps but paralyses the wrist and finger extensors (wrist drop) and causes numbness over the dorsum of the hand.

**Why:** Level of branching explains the sparing.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the anatomical events at or near the mid-shaft of the humerus.</summary>

**Answer:** The nutrient artery enters the humerus; brachialis arises; deltoid and coracobrachialis insert; the radial nerve emerges from the spiral groove; the basilic vein pierces the deep fascia; the median nerve crosses in front of the brachial artery; and the ulnar nerve with the superior ulnar collateral artery pierce the medial intermuscular septum to pass backwards.

**Why:** The lecturer's own question.
</details>

## 8. Paediatric supracondylar fracture & Volkmann's contracture

**Case:** a 6-year-old falls from monkey bars onto an outstretched hand; swollen elbow held in extension; **no radial pulse but warm, pink fingers**.

- **Age 5–7** (weak supracondylar area); **thin cortex** between the coronoid and olecranon fossae; the distal humeral physis gives **80% of elbow growth**.
- **At risk**: **brachial artery** (pulse lost in **5–15%** of type III) — it lies **anteromedial**; ⭐ **pulse loss ≠ ischaemia** (collaterals may keep the hand perfused).
- **Nerves**: median/radial/ulnar — ⭐ **anterior interosseous nerve most commonly injured** (test the **"OK sign"**); radial → thumb/wrist extension; ulnar → finger abduction.
- **Extension type (95%)**: distal fragment **posterior** (triceps pull). **Flexion type (5%)**: distal fragment anterior — rare, higher neurovascular risk.
- **"Snapping pop"** during injury suggests severe displacement.

### Volkmann's ischaemic contracture
The **proximal fragment displaces forward and downward**, compressing the **brachial artery and median nerve** → if collaterals cannot compensate, the **forearm flexor muscles become ischaemic and necrotic** → replaced by **fibrous tissue** (thin, short muscles) → **flexion deformity of the wrist and fingers**.

### The three bony points
In a **supracondylar fracture** the olecranon and two epicondyles **keep their triangle** (the fracture is above them); in **elbow dislocation** the olecranon comes to lie **in line with the epicondyles**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Age 5–7, thin cortex between fossae; extension type 95% (distal fragment posterior).
- Brachial artery at risk (pulse loss ≠ ischaemia); AIN most commonly injured (OK sign).
- Volkmann's: brachial artery + median nerve compressed → flexor necrosis → fibrosis → flexion deformity of wrist and fingers.
- Supracondylar fracture keeps the 3-point triangle; elbow dislocation disturbs it.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is Volkmann's ischaemic contracture?</summary>

**Answer:** In a supracondylar fracture the proximal fragment displaces forward and downward, compressing the brachial artery and median nerve. When the collateral circulation cannot compensate, the forearm flexor muscles become ischaemic and necrotic and are replaced by fibrous tissue, which is thin and short, producing a flexion deformity of the wrist and fingers.

**Why:** The full sequence is the answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the triangle of the three bony points preserved in supracondylar fracture but not in elbow dislocation?</summary>

**Answer:** A supracondylar fracture lies above the olecranon and the two epicondyles, so they keep their triangular relationship; in dislocation of the elbow the olecranon comes to lie more or less in line with the epicondyles.

**Why:** A classic clinical-examination question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which nerve is most commonly injured in supracondylar fracture and how is it tested?</summary>

**Answer:** The anterior interosseous nerve (branch of the median), tested by weakness of the "OK sign". The radial nerve is tested by thumb/wrist extension and the ulnar nerve by finger abduction.

**Why:** AIN + OK sign.
</details>

## 9. Nursemaid's elbow (radial head subluxation)

**Case:** a 2-year-old refuses to use her arm after being **pulled up by the wrist**; arm held in slight flexion and pronation, no swelling.

- **Mechanism**: **axial traction on a pronated forearm** (lifting a child by the hands).
- **Pathognomonic**: **no voluntary supination** (won't reach for toys).
- **Age**: peak **1–4 years**.
- **Anatomy**: under 5 years the **radial head is an immature narrow ovoid**; the **annular ligament is thin, elastic and loose** → with pronation + traction it **slips over the radial head** into the radiocapitellar joint and is trapped.
- **Reduction**: **supination + flexion** (lifts the ligament out).
- Adult equivalent = elbow dislocation (ligaments tear rather than stretch). Differential vs fracture: **no tenderness over the radial head** in subluxation.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Traction on a pronated forearm, age 1–4; no voluntary supination.
- Immature ovoid radial head + loose annular ligament slips into the joint.
- Reduce with supination + flexion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the anatomical basis of nursemaid's elbow.</summary>

**Answer:** In children under 5 the radial head is an immature narrow ovoid and the annular ligament is thin, elastic and loose. Traction on a pronated forearm makes the annular ligament slip over the radial head into the radiocapitellar joint, where it becomes trapped.

**Why:** Radial head shape + ligament laxity + mechanism.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is nursemaid's elbow reduced and why does it work?</summary>

**Answer:** By supination plus flexion of the elbow, which lifts the trapped annular ligament out of the joint.

**Why:** Direct from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the pathognomonic finding and how do you distinguish it from a fracture?</summary>

**Answer:** The child will not supinate voluntarily (won't reach for toys with that hand); unlike a fracture, there is no swelling and no tenderness over the radial head.

**Why:** Sign + differential.
</details>

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. **Anterior dislocation**: ABD + ER, squared-off shoulder, anteroinferior labrum, **axillary nerve 15–20%**; labrum deepens socket **50%**; IGHL.
2. **Posterior dislocation**: **seizure/electrocution**, arm fixed in **IR**, **up to 50% missed**; retroverted glenoid.
3. **SC** = modified saddle, costoclavicular, complete disc, main girdle joint; **AC** = plane, coracoclavicular, incomplete disc.
4. AC injury: **piano key sign**; **CC ligaments (conoid + trapezoid)** vertical, AC ligaments horizontal.
5. SC posterior dislocation → **mediastinal danger** (dyspnoea, dysphagia).
6. Clavicle: fall onto shoulder **85%**; **sternal droop**; medial **5%**, middle **80%**, lateral → nonunion; commonest site = **thinnest point (5 reasons)**.
7. **Frozen shoulder**: active + passive ROM lost, **ER loss pathognomonic**, diabetics **4×**. **Cuff tear**: passive preserved, drop-arm, critical hypovascular zone.
8. **Impingement**: 60–120° arc + night pain + overhead weakness; **Bigliani III**.
9. **Erb's point** = 6 nerves. **Erb–Duchenne (C5,6)** = waiter's tip. **Klumpke (C8,T1)** = combined claw hand.
10. **Axillary** → flat shoulder; **suprascapular** → first 15°; **long thoracic** → winged scapula; **thoracodorsal** → extension.
11. **Surgical neck** → axillary + posterior circumflex; **midshaft** → radial (**10–14 cm**, wrist drop, triceps spared) + profunda; **supracondylar** → median + brachial; **medial epicondyle** → ulnar.
12. **Supracondylar**: age 5–7, extension type **95%**, **AIN** (OK sign), **Volkmann's** contracture; 3-point triangle kept.
13. **Nursemaid's elbow**: traction on pronated forearm, age 1–4, **supination + flexion** reduces it.
