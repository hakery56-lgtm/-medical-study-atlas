## 1. Cancer and aims of treatment

- **Cancer** occurs after **normal cells are transformed into neoplastic cells** through **alteration of their genetic material** and **abnormal expression of certain genes**.
- Neoplastic cells show **chromosomal abnormalities** and **loss of differentiated properties** → **uncontrolled cell division**; many **invade previously unaffected organs = metastasis**.
- ⭐ **Aims of treatment:**

| Aim | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Palliation** | Prompt relief of unpleasant symptoms (e.g. **superior vena cava obstruction** from a mediastinal tumor) |
| **Remission** | **All macroscopic and microscopic features disappear**, though disease is known to persist |
| **Cure** | **All the cells of the clone must be destroyed** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cancer = genetic alteration + abnormal gene expression → uncontrolled division and metastasis.
- Three aims: palliation, remission, cure.
- Cure needs destruction of every cell of the clone.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does cancer arise and what are the features of neoplastic cells?</summary>

**Answer:** Normal cells are transformed into neoplastic cells by alteration of their genetic material and abnormal expression of certain genes; they show chromosomal abnormalities and loss of differentiated properties, divide uncontrollably and may invade other organs (metastasis).

**Why:** Slide 1 (Anticancer Drugs).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define palliation, remission and cure.</summary>

**Answer:** Palliation is prompt relief of unpleasant symptoms (e.g. SVC obstruction from a mediastinal tumor); remission is disappearance of all macroscopic and microscopic features although disease persists; cure requires destruction of all cells of the clone.

**Why:** Slide 1.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is remission not the same as cure?</summary>

**Answer:** In remission the disease is known to persist, whereas cure needs all the cells of the clone to be destroyed.

**Why:** Slide 1.
</details>

## 2. Classification of anticancer drugs

### By chemical structure and source
- **Alkylating agents, antimetabolites, antibiotics, plant extracts, hormones, others.**

### By biochemical mechanism of action
| Mechanism | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Block nucleic acid biosynthesis (antimetabolites)** | **Folic acid antagonist:** methotrexate (inhibits **dihydrofolate reductase**); **pyrimidine antagonists:** fluorouracil (inhibits **thymidylate synthetase**), cytarabine (inhibits **DNA polymerase**); **purine antagonist:** mercaptopurine (inhibits interconversion of purine nucleotides); **ribonucleoside diphosphate reductase antagonist:** hydroxyurea |
| **Directly influence structure and function of DNA** | **Alkylating agent:** cyclophosphamide; **platinum:** cisplatin, carboplatin; **antibiotics:** bleomycin, mitomycin C; **topoisomerase inhibitors:** camptothecin, podophyllotoxin |
| **Interfere with transcription / block RNA synthesis** | **Doxorubicin** (binds DNA to block RNA production) |
| **Interfere with protein synthesis and function** | **Antitubulins:** vinca alkaloids, taxanes; **harringtonines** (ribosome function); **L-asparaginase** (amino acid supply) |
| **Influence hormone homeostasis** | Estrogens and antagonists, androgens and antagonists, progestogens, glucocorticoids, **GnRH inhibitors (leuprolide, goserelin)**, **aromatase inhibitor (anastrozole)** |

- Hormonal drugs **bind hormone receptors to block sex hormone actions** → inhibition of tumor growth.

### By cell-cycle (phase) specificity
- **CCNSA (cell cycle nonspecific agents)** = active **throughout the cell cycle**: **alkylating agents, platinum compounds, antibiotics**.
- **CCSA (cell cycle specific agents)** = act in a **specific phase**. Slide diagram:

| Phase | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **G1** | L-asparaginase |
| **S** | Methotrexate, 6-mercaptopurine, 5-fluorouracil, mitomycin C, hydroxyurea, doxorubicin |
| **G2** | Bleomycin, etoposide, topotecan, daunorubicin |
| **M** | Vincristine, vinblastine, paclitaxel, docetaxel |

- Note: the slide lists antibiotics as cell cycle nonspecific, yet the diagram places mitomycin C, doxorubicin, bleomycin and daunorubicin in specific phases; know both slide statements for this lecture.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Mechanism classes: nucleic acid synthesis, DNA structure, transcription, protein synthesis, hormones.
- CCNSA = alkylating agents, platinum compounds, antibiotics.
- Phase diagram: G1 asparaginase; S antimetabolites; G2 bleomycin/etoposide/topotecan; M vinca alkaloids and taxanes.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify anticancer drugs according to their biochemical mechanism of action.</summary>

**Answer:** Block nucleic acid biosynthesis (antimetabolites); directly influence DNA structure and function (alkylating agents, platinum, bleomycin, mitomycin C, topoisomerase inhibitors); interfere with transcription (doxorubicin); interfere with protein synthesis and function (vinca alkaloids, taxanes, harringtonines, L-asparaginase); influence hormone homeostasis.

**Why:** Slides 2-4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the difference between CCNSA and CCSA? Give examples.</summary>

**Answer:** CCNSA are active throughout the cell cycle (alkylating agents, platinum compounds, antibiotics); CCSA act in a specific phase (e.g. antimetabolites in S, vinca alkaloids and taxanes in M, L-asparaginase in G1).

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the targets of methotrexate, fluorouracil, cytarabine and hydroxyurea.</summary>

**Answer:** Methotrexate: dihydrofolate reductase; fluorouracil: thymidylate synthetase; cytarabine: DNA polymerase; hydroxyurea: ribonucleoside diphosphate reductase.

**Why:** Slide 2.
</details>

## 3. Alkylating agents

- **Drugs:** **cyclophosphamide, chlorambucil, carmustine, lomustine, busulfan.**
- ⭐ **Mechanism:** **bind irreversibly to DNA by alkylation** → DNA **cannot replicate** → no synthesis of proteins and essential metabolites → reproduction inhibited → **cell dies**.
- **Adverse effects:** **myelosuppression (dose-limiting)**, nausea and vomiting, **teratogenesis**, **gonadal atrophy**, major risk of **leukemogenesis and carcinogenesis**.

| Drug | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Cyclophosphamide** | Can be given **orally**; **CLL, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, breast and ovarian cancer**; **potent immunosuppressant** (rheumatoid disorders, autoimmune nephritis); AEs: **alopecia, nausea, vomiting, myelosuppression, hemorrhagic cystitis** |
| **Carmustine, lomustine** | **Nitrosoureas**; **highly lipophilic**, CSF levels ≈ **30% of plasma** → **brain tumors** |
| **Busulfan** | Oral; **chronic granulocytic leukemia** and other myeloproliferative disorders; AEs: myelosuppression, nausea/vomiting, **"busulfan lung" (rare fatal pulmonary fibrosis)** at high doses |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Alkylation of DNA is irreversible; myelosuppression is dose-limiting; leukemogenic.
- Cyclophosphamide → hemorrhagic cystitis; also immunosuppressant.
- Carmustine/lomustine (nitrosoureas) penetrate the CNS; busulfan → CGL and pulmonary fibrosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the mechanism and main adverse effects of alkylating agents.</summary>

**Answer:** They alkylate (bind irreversibly to) DNA so it cannot replicate, protein and metabolite synthesis stops and the cell dies. Adverse effects: dose-limiting myelosuppression, nausea and vomiting, teratogenesis, gonadal atrophy, leukemogenesis and carcinogenesis.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why are carmustine and lomustine used for brain tumors?</summary>

**Answer:** They are nitrosoureas that are highly lipophilic and reach CSF concentrations about 30% of plasma concentrations (excellent CNS penetration).

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the indications and adverse effects of cyclophosphamide and busulfan.</summary>

**Answer:** Cyclophosphamide: CLL, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, breast and ovarian cancer, rheumatoid disorders and autoimmune nephritis; AEs alopecia, nausea, vomiting, myelosuppression, hemorrhagic cystitis. Busulfan (oral): chronic granulocytic leukemia and myeloproliferative disorders; AEs myelosuppression, nausea/vomiting and "busulfan lung".

**Why:** Slides 6-7.
</details>

## 4. Antimetabolites

- **S phase-specific** structural analogues of essential metabolites that **interfere with DNA synthesis**; ⭐ **myelosuppression is the dose-limiting toxicity** for all.

| Drug | Mechanism | Indications | Adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Methotrexate (MTX)** (folic acid antagonist) | Similar to folic acid; actively transported into cells; **inhibits dihydrofolate reductase** → no **tetrahydrofolate** for **thymidine and purine** synthesis | **Choriocarcinoma, acute lymphocytic leukemia, meningeal metastases** | **Myelosuppression, GIT disturbances, renal toxicity** (crystalluria of the **7-OH metabolite**) |
| **6-Mercaptopurine (6-MP)** (purine antagonist) | Believed to **inhibit purine base synthesis** (exact mechanism uncertain) | **Maintenance of remission in ALL** | Well tolerated; mild myelosuppression; long-term **hepatotoxicity** |
| **5-Fluorouracil (5-FU)** (pyrimidine antagonist) | Thymine analogue (**fluorine replaces methyl**); metabolites **5-FdUMP** (inhibits **thymidylate synthetase** → no thymidine) and **5-FdUTP** (incorporated into **RNA** by RNA polymerase) | **Solid tumors only**: breast, colorectal, gastric, head and neck squamous cell | Nausea/vomiting; **oral and GIT ulceration (dose-limiting with continuous infusion)**; **myelosuppression (worse after bolus)** |

- Note: the 5-FU diagram on the slide lists "germ cell tumor, hepatic tumor" as uses, whereas the text lists breast, colorectal, gastric and head and neck tumors; know both as written.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Antimetabolites = S phase; dose-limiting toxicity is myelosuppression.
- MTX inhibits DHFR (renal crystalluria of 7-OH metabolite); 6-MP maintains remission in ALL; 5-FU inhibits thymidylate synthetase.
- 5-FU: infusion → GIT ulceration; bolus → myelosuppression.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mechanism of action, uses and toxicity of methotrexate.</summary>

**Answer:** MTX resembles folic acid, is actively transported into cells and inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, blocking formation of tetrahydrofolate needed for thymidine and purine synthesis. Used for choriocarcinoma, ALL and meningeal metastases. Toxicity: myelosuppression, GIT disturbances and renal toxicity from crystalluria of the 7-OH metabolite.

**Why:** Slides 8-9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain how 5-fluorouracil works.</summary>

**Answer:** 5-FU is a thymine analogue (methyl replaced by fluorine). Its metabolite 5-FdUMP inhibits thymidylate synthetase, preventing thymidine synthesis for DNA; 5-FdUTP is incorporated into RNA and interferes with RNA function.

**Why:** Slides 9-10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the toxicity of 5-FU after continuous infusion versus bolus injection.</summary>

**Answer:** With continuous infusion, oral and GIT ulceration is dose-limiting; with bolus injections myelosuppression is more problematic. Nausea and vomiting also occur.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

## 5. Antitumor antibiotics

| Drug | Mechanism | Uses | Adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Doxorubicin, daunorubicin** | **DNA intercalating agents** blocking **DNA and RNA synthesis**; toxic mainly in **S phase** | Doxorubicin: acute leukemias, lymphoma, solid tumors; daunorubicin: induces remission of **nonlymphocytic leukemia and ALL** | not listed on the slides |
| **Mitomycin C** | **Alkylates DNA** → strand breakage, inhibits DNA synthesis | Lip, oral cavity, pharynx, digestive organs, peritoneum, breast, **urinary bladder** | **Delayed, prolonged myelosuppression** (platelets and leukocytes) |
| **Actinomycin D** | **Intercalates DNA** → blocks transcription and mRNA synthesis; **IV** | **Trophoblastic (gestational) tumors, testicular cancer, Wilms' tumor, Ewing's sarcoma** | not listed on the slides |
| **Bleomycin** | Intercalates DNA but major cytotoxicity = **free radicals + DNA strand breakage** | **Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, testicular cancer**, other solid tumors | **Pulmonary and mucocutaneous reactions** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Doxorubicin/daunorubicin and actinomycin D intercalate DNA; mitomycin C alkylates; bleomycin makes free radicals.
- Mitomycin C → delayed prolonged myelosuppression; bleomycin → lung and mucocutaneous toxicity.
- Actinomycin D → trophoblastic tumors, testicular cancer, Wilms', Ewing's.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare the mechanisms of doxorubicin, mitomycin C and bleomycin.</summary>

**Answer:** Doxorubicin intercalates DNA and blocks DNA and RNA synthesis (S phase); mitomycin C alkylates DNA causing strand breakage; bleomycin intercalates but kills mainly by free radical formation and DNA strand breakage.

**Why:** Slides 11-13.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the uses of actinomycin D?</summary>

**Answer:** Given IV for trophoblastic (gestational) tumors, testicular cancer and pediatric tumors (Wilms' tumor, Ewing's sarcoma); it intercalates DNA and prevents transcription.

**Why:** Slide 12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the serious adverse effects of mitomycin C and bleomycin.</summary>

**Answer:** Mitomycin C: delayed and prolonged myelosuppression affecting platelets and leukocytes. Bleomycin: pulmonary and mucocutaneous reactions.

**Why:** Slides 12-13.
</details>

## 6. DNA-topoisomerase inhibitors

- **Epipodophyllotoxins:** **etoposide** (**topoisomerase II** inhibitor). **Camptothecin analogs:** **topotecan, irinotecan** (**topoisomerase I** inhibitors).
- **Mechanism:** target **topoisomerases**, enzymes that **unwind and relax DNA** for replication → hinder growth of rapidly dividing cancer cells.
- **Route:** **oral or IV**. **Uses:** **advanced colorectal cancer, testicular tumors, small-cell lung cancer**, certain leukemias and lymphomas. **Side effect:** mainly **myelosuppression**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Etoposide = topo II; topotecan and irinotecan = topo I.
- Topoisomerases unwind/relax DNA for replication.
- Main toxicity: myelosuppression.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name the topoisomerase inhibitors and their targets.</summary>

**Answer:** Etoposide (epipodophyllotoxin) inhibits topoisomerase II; topotecan and irinotecan (camptothecin analogs) inhibit topoisomerase I.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why do topoisomerase inhibitors affect rapidly dividing cells?</summary>

**Answer:** Topoisomerases unwind and relax DNA to allow replication for cell division, so inhibiting them hinders tumor growth, particularly in rapidly dividing cancer cells.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the uses and side effect of these drugs.</summary>

**Answer:** Advanced colorectal cancer, testicular tumors, small-cell lung cancer, certain leukemias and lymphomas; given orally or IV; side effect is primarily myelosuppression.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

## 7. Plant alkaloids

### Vinca alkaloids (vinblastine, vincristine, vinorelbine)
- **Mechanism:** **prevent microtubule assembly** → no mitotic filaments → cells **arrest in late G2 phase** (slide wording; the phase diagram places them in M).
- **Vinblastine** + bleomycin + cisplatin → **metastatic testicular tumors**; **vincristine** + prednisone → **remission in childhood leukemia**; **vinorelbine** → **non-small-cell lung cancer and breast cancer**.

### Taxanes (paclitaxel, docetaxel, cabazitaxel)
- **Mechanism:** **enhance all aspects of tubulin polymerization** — **the opposite of vinca alkaloids** — yet also cytotoxic.
- **Indications:** ovarian, breast, bladder, cervical, GIT, non-small cell lung, prostate and uterine cancers.
- **Adverse effects:** nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, **peripheral sensory neuropathy**, **neutropenia**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Vinca alkaloids prevent microtubule assembly; taxanes enhance tubulin polymerization.
- Vinblastine → testicular; vincristine + prednisone → childhood leukemia; vinorelbine → NSCLC and breast.
- Taxanes → wide range of solid tumors; neuropathy and neutropenia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Contrast the mechanisms of vinca alkaloids and taxanes.</summary>

**Answer:** Vinca alkaloids prevent microtubule assembly (no mitotic filaments); taxanes enhance all aspects of tubulin polymerization, the opposite action, but both are cytotoxic, showing tubulin dynamics is a target.

**Why:** Slides 14-15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the clinical uses of vinblastine, vincristine and vinorelbine.</summary>

**Answer:** Vinblastine with bleomycin and cisplatin for metastatic testicular tumors; vincristine with prednisone to induce remission in childhood leukemia; vinorelbine for non-small-cell lung and breast cancer.

**Why:** Slide 14.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the adverse effects of taxanes.</summary>

**Answer:** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, peripheral sensory neuropathy and neutropenia.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

## 8. Platinum compounds

- **Cisplatin, carboplatin.** **Mechanism:** **bind to guanine in DNA and RNA** (stabilized by hydrogen bonding) → **unwinding and shortening of the DNA helix**.

| Drug | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Cisplatin** | **IV**; **first-line for testicular, ovarian and bladder cancer**; also melanoma and other solid tumors; **little myelosuppression** but **severe nausea, vomiting, nephrotoxicity** |
| **Carboplatin** | Similar spectrum; approved only as **second-line drug for ovarian cancer** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Platinum drugs bind guanine → unwinding/shortening of the DNA helix.
- Cisplatin: first-line testicular, ovarian, bladder; nephrotoxic, emetogenic, little myelosuppression.
- Carboplatin: second-line for ovarian cancer.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the mechanism of platinum compounds?</summary>

**Answer:** They bind to guanine in DNA and RNA, stabilized by hydrogen bonding, causing unwinding and shortening of the DNA helix.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. State the indications and toxicity of cisplatin.</summary>

**Answer:** IV first-line drug for testicular, ovarian and bladder cancer, also melanoma and other solid tumors; relatively little myelosuppression but severe nausea, vomiting and nephrotoxicity.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does carboplatin differ from cisplatin in use?</summary>

**Answer:** It has a similar spectrum of activity but is approved only as a second-line drug for ovarian cancer.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

## 9. Hormones in cancer therapy

- **Hormone-dependent cancers** (especially **breast, prostate, endometrial**) respond to their hormone antagonists: **estrogen antagonists → breast cancer; androgen antagonists → prostate cancer; corticosteroids → lymphocytic leukemias and lymphomas.**

| Agent | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Estrogens** | Inhibit effects of endogenous androgens in **androgen-dependent metastatic prostatic carcinoma**; **diethylstilbestrol** usually the agent of choice; AEs: **cardiac and cerebrovascular complications**, **carcinoma of the male breast** |
| **Progestins** | **Endometrial carcinoma** and **metastatic hormone-dependent breast cancer** |
| ⭐ **Tamoxifen** (antiestrogen) | **Drug of choice in postmenopausal women** with or recovering from metastatic breast cancer; **most effective in ER-positive tumors**; adjunct to oophorectomy, leuprolide or goserelin in **premenopausal** ER-positive women |
| **Androgens** | In most ER⁺ breast cancers they are **inhibitory/antiproliferative** (counterbalance estrogen); **virilizing effects and hepatic toxicity** limit use; **fluoxymesterone** most widely used |
| **Glucocorticoids** | **Integral to curative therapy of ALL, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, Hodgkin's disease**; also prevent allergic reaction, **control emesis**, relieve **intracranial hypertension / spinal cord compression**, relieve **pain** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hormone antagonists: breast (estrogen), prostate (androgen), lymphoid malignancies (corticosteroids).
- Tamoxifen = drug of choice in postmenopausal metastatic ER⁺ breast cancer; DES for prostate cancer.
- Androgens limited by virilization and hepatotoxicity; glucocorticoids also give supportive benefits.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Which cancers are hormone-dependent and what hormonal treatment is used for each?</summary>

**Answer:** Breast cancer (estrogen antagonists), prostate cancer (androgen antagonists/estrogens such as diethylstilbestrol), endometrial cancer (progestins), and lymphocytic leukemias and lymphomas (corticosteroids).

**Why:** Slides 17-18.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the use of tamoxifen.</summary>

**Answer:** It is the drug of choice in postmenopausal women with or recovering from metastatic breast cancer, most effective in ER-positive tumors, and is also adjunctive to oophorectomy, leuprolide or goserelin in premenopausal women with ER-positive tumors.

**Why:** Slide 18.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the roles of glucocorticoids in cancer care.</summary>

**Answer:** Curative therapy of ALL, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma and Hodgkin's disease; prevention of allergic reaction, emesis control, relief of intracranial hypertension or spinal cord compression, and pain relief.

**Why:** Slides 18-19.
</details>

## 10. Immunomodulating drugs

- **Immunosuppressive agents:** suppress immune mechanisms; used for **autoimmune diseases** and to **prevent graft rejection** after transplantation. Examples: **ciclosporin, tacrolimus, adrenocortical hormones, antimetabolites, alkylating agents, antilymphocyte globulin, mycophenolate mofetil.**
- **Immunopotentiators:** **enhance antitumor immunity**, used for neoplastic disease: **recombinant interferons and cytokines.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Immunosuppressants: autoimmune disease and graft rejection.
- Immunopotentiators enhance antitumor immunity (interferons, cytokines).
- Several anticancer drugs (antimetabolites, alkylating agents, steroids) are also immunosuppressive.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are immunosuppressive agents used for? Name examples.</summary>

**Answer:** To suppress immune mechanisms in autoimmune diseases and to prevent graft rejection; examples are ciclosporin, tacrolimus, adrenocortical hormones, antimetabolites, alkylating agents, antilymphocyte globulin and mycophenolate mofetil.

**Why:** Slide 20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are immunopotentiators?</summary>

**Answer:** Agents that enhance antitumor immunity and treat neoplastic disease, e.g. recombinant interferons and cytokines.

**Why:** Slide 20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which classes of anticancer drugs also appear in the immunosuppressant list?</summary>

**Answer:** Adrenocortical hormones (glucocorticoids), antimetabolites and alkylating agents.

**Why:** Slide 20.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Nitrosoureas (carmustine, lomustine) = brain tumors** (lipophilic, CSF ≈ 30% of plasma).
- **Methotrexate inhibits DHFR; 5-FU inhibits thymidylate synthetase; cytarabine inhibits DNA polymerase.**
- **Vinca alkaloids PREVENT microtubule assembly; taxanes ENHANCE tubulin polymerization.**
- **Etoposide = topoisomerase II; topotecan/irinotecan = topoisomerase I.**
- **Cisplatin: little myelosuppression** but nephrotoxic and very emetogenic; **carboplatin = second-line for ovarian cancer only.**
- **5-FU: infusion → GIT ulceration; bolus → myelosuppression.**
- **Busulfan lung** = pulmonary fibrosis; **bleomycin** = pulmonary and mucocutaneous toxicity; **cyclophosphamide** = hemorrhagic cystitis.
- **Tamoxifen** = postmenopausal ER⁺ breast cancer; **diethylstilbestrol** = prostate cancer.
- **6-MP** maintains remission in ALL; **vincristine + prednisone** = childhood leukemia remission.
