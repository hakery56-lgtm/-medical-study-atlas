# Chronic Inflammation, Healing & Repair — Exam Night Review

This lecture follows acute inflammation. It covers what happens when inflammation doesn't resolve (chronic and granulomatous inflammation), how tissues repair themselves (regeneration and scar), skin wound healing by first and second intention, abnormal healing, and fracture healing. After each topic: **Key points** and 3 short-essay questions (tap a question to reveal the answer).

---

## 1. Chronic inflammation — definition, causes and features

**Definition:** inflammation of **prolonged duration** in which **active inflammation, tissue injury and healing proceed simultaneously**.

It occurs when the acute response **cannot be resolved**, because of:
- **persistence of the injurious agent**, or
- **interference with the normal process of healing**.

### Causes
1. **Viral infections** — intracellular infections need **lymphocytes and macrophages** to eradicate them.
2. **Persistent microbial infections** — e.g. **tubercle bacilli**: organisms of **low toxicity** that evoke **delayed hypersensitivity**.
3. **Prolonged exposure to toxic agents** — **exogenous** (e.g. **silicosis**) or **endogenous** (e.g. **atherosclerosis**).
4. **Autoimmunity** — e.g. **rheumatoid arthritis**.

### Morphological features (three hallmarks)
1. **Infiltration by mononuclear cells** — **macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells** (a persistent reaction to injury).
2. **Tissue destruction** — by the persistent agent or by the inflammatory cells themselves.
3. **Repair** by **angiogenesis and fibrosis** — an attempt to replace lost tissue.

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Acute = **neutrophils**; chronic = **mononuclear cells** (macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells). In chronic inflammation, **injury and healing happen at the same time**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Prolonged inflammation with simultaneous active inflammation, tissue injury and healing.
- Arises from persistent agent or interference with healing.
- Causes: viruses, persistent microbes (TB), toxic agents (silicosis, atherosclerosis), autoimmunity (RA).
- Hallmarks: mononuclear infiltrate, tissue destruction, repair by angiogenesis + fibrosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define chronic inflammation and state why it develops.</summary>

**Answer:** Inflammation of prolonged duration in which active inflammation, tissue injury and healing proceed simultaneously. It develops when the acute response cannot be resolved, either because the injurious agent persists or because the normal process of healing is interfered with.

**Why:** Definition + the two reasons.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the causes of chronic inflammation with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Viral infections (intracellular, needing lymphocytes and macrophages); persistent microbial infections such as tubercle bacilli (low toxicity, delayed hypersensitivity); prolonged exposure to toxic agents — exogenous (silicosis) or endogenous (atherosclerosis); and autoimmunity (rheumatoid arthritis).

</details>

**Why:** Three features — the lung example shows all three.
</details>

## 2. Cells of chronic inflammation

### The mononuclear phagocyte system
**Blood monocytes → tissue macrophages**, which have local names:
| Site | Macrophage |
|---|---|
| Liver | **Kupffer cells** |
| Spleen | **sinus histiocytes** |
| CNS | **microglia** |
| Lung | **alveolar macrophages** |

### The macrophage — ⭐ the dominant cell of chronic inflammation
- Monocytes emigrate early in acute inflammation and **enlarge into macrophages**.
- Their products **eliminate injurious agents**, **start repair**, and cause **much of the tissue injury** of chronic inflammation.
- In short-lived inflammation macrophages disappear once the irritant is gone; in chronic inflammation they **keep accumulating**.

### Mechanisms of macrophage accumulation
1. ⭐ **Continued recruitment of monocytes from the circulation** — **the most important source**.
2. **Local proliferation** of macrophages.
3. **Immobilization** within the site (by cytokines and oxidized lipids).

### Other cells
- **Lymphocytes**: in antibody- and cell-mediated reactions (and even non-immune inflammation); they interact **bidirectionally with macrophages**, each activating the other.
- **Plasma cells**.
- **Eosinophils**: abundant in **IgE-mediated** reactions and **parasitic infections**.
- **Mast cells**: widely distributed in connective tissue; take part in **both acute and chronic** inflammation.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Monocyte → macrophage: Kupffer (liver), sinus histiocytes (spleen), microglia (CNS), alveolar macrophages (lung).
- Macrophage = dominant cell; removes agents, starts repair, causes tissue injury.
- Accumulation: recruitment (most important), local proliferation, immobilization.
- Lymphocytes interact bidirectionally with macrophages; eosinophils in IgE/parasites; mast cells in acute and chronic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the mechanisms of macrophage accumulation in chronic inflammation?</summary>

**Answer:** Continued recruitment of monocytes from the circulation (the most important source), local proliferation of macrophages, and immobilization of macrophages at the site of inflammation by cytokines and oxidized lipids.

**Why:** Three mechanisms, with the most important one named.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the macrophage the dominant cell of chronic inflammation?</summary>

**Answer:** Monocytes enter tissues early and become macrophages, whose products eliminate injurious agents such as microbes, initiate repair, and are responsible for much of the tissue injury. In chronic inflammation, unlike short-lived inflammation, macrophages keep accumulating instead of disappearing.

**Why:** Role + persistence.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the tissue macrophages of the liver, spleen, CNS and lung.</summary>

**Answer:** Kupffer cells (liver), sinus histiocytes (spleen), microglia (CNS) and alveolar macrophages (lung).

**Why:** A quick matching question.
</details>

## 3. Granulomatous inflammation

- **Definition**: a **distinctive pattern of chronic inflammation** with **focal accumulations of activated macrophages** that take on an **epithelial-like (epithelioid)** appearance.
- **Granuloma**: a **nodular collection of epithelioid macrophages** surrounded by a **rim (collar) of lymphocytes and plasma cells**.
- ⭐ **Tuberculosis is the prototype** of granulomatous disease.

### Morphology (H&E)
- **Epithelioid cells**: pale/pink **granular cytoplasm** with **indistinct cell boundaries**.
- Surrounding **collar of lymphocytes** secreting cytokines that keep the macrophages activated.
- **Older granulomas** develop a rim of **fibroblasts and connective tissue**.
- **Multinucleated giant cells**: **40–50 µm**, a large mass of cytoplasm with **20 or more nuclei** — formed by **fusion of epithelioid macrophages**.
  - **Langhans giant cell**: nuclei at the **periphery** (horseshoe).
  - **Foreign-body giant cell**: nuclei arranged **haphazardly**.
- In **TB** granulomas, **hypoxia + free-radical injury** → a **central zone of necrosis** with a **cheesy** gross appearance = **caseous necrosis**.

### Two types
| | **Immune granuloma** | **Foreign-body granuloma** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | **insoluble particles** that induce a **cell-mediated immune response** | **inert** material, **too large** to be digested by one macrophage; **no** inflammatory or immune response |
| Examples | bacteria: **TB, leprosy**; parasites: **schistosomiasis**; fungi: **histoplasmosis, blastomycosis**; **sarcoidosis** | **metal/dust (silicosis)**, **splinter**, **suture** |
| Feature | epithelioid cells + giant cells | epithelioid & giant cells **around** the foreign body, which lies **in the centre** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Granuloma = epithelioid macrophages + collar of lymphocytes/plasma cells; TB is the prototype.
- Giant cells = fused macrophages (20+ nuclei, 40–50 µm): Langhans (peripheral) vs foreign-body (haphazard).
- Caseous necrosis in TB from hypoxia + free radicals.
- Immune granulomas (TB, leprosy, schistosomiasis, fungi, sarcoidosis) vs foreign-body granulomas (silica, splinter, suture).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define granulomatous inflammation and a granuloma.</summary>

**Answer:** Granulomatous inflammation is a distinctive pattern of chronic inflammation characterized by focal accumulations of activated macrophages that develop an epithelial-like (epithelioid) appearance. A granuloma is a nodular collection of epithelioid macrophages surrounded by a rim of lymphocytes and plasma cells.

**Why:** Two definitions often asked together.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the morphology of a tuberculous granuloma.</summary>

**Answer:** Aggregates of epithelioid macrophages with pink granular cytoplasm and indistinct borders, surrounded by a collar of lymphocytes; multinucleated Langhans giant cells (40–50 µm, 20 or more peripheral nuclei, formed by fusion of macrophages); a central zone of caseous necrosis (cheesy grossly) produced by hypoxia and free-radical injury; and in older granulomas a rim of fibroblasts and connective tissue.

**Why:** All components, from centre outwards.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare immune and foreign-body granulomas.</summary>

**Answer:** Immune granulomas are caused by insoluble particles that induce a cell-mediated immune response (e.g. TB, leprosy, schistosomiasis, histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, sarcoidosis). Foreign-body granulomas form around inert material too large for a single macrophage to digest that incites no inflammatory or immune response (e.g. silica, splinters, sutures); the foreign body lies in the centre, surrounded by epithelioid and foreign-body giant cells.

**Why:** Cause + examples + position of the agent.
</details>

## 4. Repair: regeneration vs scar, and tissue types

**Repair** = restoration of tissue architecture and function after injury, by:
1. **Regeneration** — restoration of **normal cells**.
2. **Scar formation** — deposition of **connective tissue**.

### Tissues by proliferative capacity
| Tissue | Behaviour | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Labile** | **continuously dividing**, cells constantly lost and replaced | **haematopoietic cells**, most **surface epithelia** (skin, oral cavity) |
| **Stable** | **quiescent (G0)**, minimal activity, but **divide in response to injury** | parenchyma of **liver, kidney, pancreas** |
| **Permanent** | **terminally differentiated**, non-proliferative after birth → repair by **scar** | **neurons**, **cardiac muscle** |

### Regeneration happens when
1. the tissue **can replace** the damaged components, and
2. the **residual tissue is structurally intact**.
- Controlled by **growth factors** and **cell–ECM interactions**.
- By **surviving differentiated cells** (e.g. **hepatocytes**) or **tissue stem cells** (skin and gut epithelia).
- ⭐ **Best example: the liver**.

### Scar formation happens when
1. the tissue **cannot regenerate** at all,
2. regeneration **alone is not enough**, or
3. the **supporting structures are too damaged** to support regeneration.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Repair = regeneration (normal cells) or scar (connective tissue).
- Labile (marrow, epithelia), stable (liver, kidney, pancreas — G0), permanent (neurons, cardiac muscle → scar).
- Regeneration needs replaceable cells + intact framework; liver is the best example.
- Scar when no regeneration, insufficient regeneration, or damaged supporting structure.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify tissues according to their proliferative capacity with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Labile tissues divide continuously (haematopoietic cells, most surface epithelia such as skin and oral cavity). Stable tissues are quiescent in G0 but divide after injury or loss of mass (parenchyma of liver, kidney, pancreas). Permanent tissues are terminally differentiated and non-proliferative after birth, so repair is dominated by scar formation (neurons, cardiac muscle).

**Why:** Three classes, each with examples.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. When does regeneration occur and how is it accomplished?</summary>

**Answer:** When the tissue is able to replace the damaged components and the residual tissue is structurally intact. It is controlled by growth factors and cell–ECM interactions and is accomplished by surviving differentiated cells (e.g. hepatocytes) or tissue stem cells (skin and intestinal epithelia). The liver is the best example.

**Why:** Conditions + cells + example.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When does scar formation replace regeneration?</summary>

**Answer:** When the injured tissue is incapable of regeneration, when repair cannot be accomplished by regeneration alone, or when the supporting structures are too severely damaged to support regeneration of the tissue cells.

**Why:** Three conditions.
</details>

## 5. Steps in scar formation, granulation tissue & angiogenesis

### Sequence
1. **Haemostatic plug** — within **minutes**: platelets stop bleeding, give a **scaffold for fibrin**, release chemokines and mediators.
2. **Inflammation** — **6–48 hours**: inflammatory cells remove microbes and debris; it resolves as they are cleared.
3. **Cell proliferation** — up to **10 days**:
   - **epithelial cells** migrate to cover the wound;
   - **endothelial cells and pericytes** form new vessels (**angiogenesis**);
   - **fibroblasts** proliferate and lay down the **collagen** of the scar.

### Granulation tissue — ⭐ hallmark of early repair
- **Highly vascular loose connective tissue**, **peaks at 5–7 days**.
- Components: **fibroblasts**, **loose connective tissue**, **new thin-walled capillaries**, **mononuclear leukocytes** (mainly macrophages).
- Name comes from its **gross look**: **pink, soft, granular** (under the scab of a skin wound).
- By **2 weeks** it is progressively **replaced by collagen** → a stable fibrous scar.

### Angiogenesis — new vessels from existing ones
1. **Vasodilation** (**nitric oxide**) + ↑ permeability (**VEGF**).
2. **Pericytes separate** and the **basement membrane breaks down** → a vessel sprout.
3. **Endothelial cells migrate** toward the injury.
4. **Remodelling into capillary tubes**.
5. **Recruitment of periendothelial cells** (pericytes for capillaries, smooth muscle for larger vessels).
- Clinical: **anti-VEGF drugs (Avastin)** treat **cancer** and **diabetic retinopathy**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Plug (minutes) → inflammation (6–48 h) → proliferation (to 10 days): epithelium, angiogenesis, fibroblasts/collagen.
- Granulation tissue: fibroblasts + loose CT + new capillaries + macrophages; peaks 5–7 days; pink, soft, granular; replaced by collagen from 2 weeks.
- Angiogenesis: NO vasodilation + VEGF permeability → pericyte separation → migration → tubes → pericyte recruitment; anti-VEGF (Avastin).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the steps of repair by scar formation.</summary>

**Answer:** Within minutes a platelet haemostatic plug stops bleeding and provides a scaffold for fibrin; within 6–48 hours inflammation removes microbes and debris and then resolves; over up to 10 days epithelial cells migrate to cover the wound, endothelial cells and pericytes form new vessels (angiogenesis), and fibroblasts proliferate and deposit collagen that forms the scar.

**Why:** Three steps with their timing.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is granulation tissue?</summary>

**Answer:** A highly vascularized loose connective tissue that is the hallmark of early repair, peaking at 5–7 days. It consists of proliferating fibroblasts, loose connective tissue, new thin-walled capillaries and mononuclear leukocytes (mainly macrophages). It is named for its pink, soft, granular gross appearance, and from about 2 weeks it is replaced by collagen to form a fibrous scar.

**Why:** Definition + components + name + fate.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the steps of angiogenesis.</summary>

**Answer:** Vasodilation in response to nitric oxide and increased permeability induced by VEGF; separation of pericytes and breakdown of the basement membrane to allow a vessel sprout; migration of endothelial cells toward the injury; remodelling into capillary tubes; and recruitment of periendothelial cells (pericytes or smooth muscle) to form the mature vessel.

**Why:** Five ordered steps.
</details>

## 6. Skin wound healing — first vs second intention

Phases of cutaneous healing: **inflammation** → **granulation tissue + re-epithelialization** → **wound contraction, ECM deposition and remodelling**.

### Primary (first) intention — e.g. a surgical incision
Edges **apposed**, **minimal tissue loss**, **no infection**.
| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| Immediately | gap filled with **blood and fibrin clot** |
| **First 24 h** | **neutrophils** invade |
| **24–48 h** | epidermis grows from the basal cells as a thin layer |
| **Day 3** | **granulation tissue** fills the wound; **macrophages replace neutrophils** |
| **Day 5** | **collagen bridges** the edges; new vessels |
| **Week 1 – day 10** | **sutures removed** — the wound has only **10%** of normal strength |
| **Week 2** | inflammation and oedema clear; **pale fibrous scar** |
| **Month 1** | **50%** strength |
| **Month 3** | **70–80%** of normal tensile strength (the maximum) |

### Secondary (second) intention — e.g. ulcers, large wounds
Edges **widely separated and irregular**, **large tissue loss**, **infection common**. Differences from primary:
- **Larger fibrin clot**, more debris and exudate → **more intense inflammation**.
- **Larger amount of granulation tissue**.
- ⭐ **Wound contraction** by **myofibroblasts** (smooth-muscle-like) — **the feature that most clearly differentiates** secondary from primary healing.
- **Substantial scar** and **thinning of the epidermis**.

### Factors influencing wound healing
**Nutrition** (protein, **vitamin C**), **metabolic status** (**diabetes delays healing**), **circulation**, **size** of wound, **location** (well-vascularized tissues heal faster), **infection** (clean heals faster), **mechanical forces** (motion).

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** A healed scar **never** returns to 100% — the maximum is **70–80% at 3 months**. The key difference between the two types of healing is **wound contraction (myofibroblasts)**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Primary: apposed edges; clot → neutrophils 24 h → epithelium 24–48 h → granulation day 3 → collagen day 5 → sutures out day 7–10 (10%) → 50% at 1 month → 70–80% at 3 months.
- Secondary: large defect, more inflammation and granulation tissue, wound contraction by myofibroblasts, big scar.
- Factors: nutrition (vitamin C), diabetes, circulation, size, location, infection, motion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the sequence of healing by primary intention.</summary>

**Answer:** The gap fills with blood and fibrin clot; neutrophils invade in the first 24 hours; the epidermis grows from basal cells at 24–48 hours; by day 3 granulation tissue fills the wound and macrophages replace neutrophils; by day 5 collagen bridges the edges with new vessels; sutures are removed at 7–10 days (10% strength); by 2 weeks a pale scar forms; the scar reaches 50% strength at 1 month and 70–80% at 3 months.

**Why:** A timeline question — examiners check the numbers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does healing by secondary intention differ from primary intention?</summary>

**Answer:** The large defect produces a larger fibrin clot with more necrotic debris and exudate, so inflammation is more intense; more granulation tissue forms; wound contraction occurs due to myofibroblasts (the most distinguishing feature); and there is a substantial scar with thinning of the epidermis.

**Why:** Four differences, with contraction highlighted.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the factors that influence wound healing.</summary>

**Answer:** Nutritional status (protein and vitamin C), metabolic status (diabetes delays healing), circulatory status, size of the wound, location (well-vascularized tissues heal faster), infection (clean wounds heal faster) and mechanical forces such as motion.

**Why:** Seven factors from the lecture.
</details>

## 7. Abnormal healing

| Abnormality | What it is | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypertrophic scar** | excess collagen → **raised scar** | grows fast, many **myofibroblasts**, **regresses** over months; after thermal/traumatic injury to the **deep dermis** |
| **Keloid** | scar **grows beyond the wound boundaries** | **does not regress**; more common in **African Americans**; **ear lobe** and **chest** |
| **Proud flesh** | **excess granulation tissue** protruding above the skin | **blocks re-epithelialization**; must be removed |
| **Desmoid** | **excess fibroblast** proliferation after incisions or trauma | rare; **recurs after excision** |
| **Contracture** | **exaggerated wound contraction** | deformity (e.g. **claw hand**), limited joint movement; **palms, soles, after burns** |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Hypertrophic scar stays **within** the wound and **regresses**; keloid goes **beyond** the wound and **does not regress**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Excess collagen: hypertrophic scar (within wound, regresses) vs keloid (beyond wound, no regression, ear lobe/chest).
- Proud flesh = excess granulation tissue blocking epithelialization.
- Desmoid = excess fibroblasts, recurs after excision.
- Contracture = exaggerated contraction (palms, soles, burns).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare hypertrophic scar and keloid.</summary>

**Answer:** Both result from excessive collagen. A hypertrophic scar is raised but stays within the wound, grows rapidly with abundant myofibroblasts and tends to regress over months; it follows thermal or traumatic injury to the deep dermis. A keloid grows beyond the boundaries of the original wound and does not regress; it is more common in African Americans, especially on the ear lobe and chest.

**Why:** The boundary + regression contrast.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is proud flesh?</summary>

**Answer:** Formation of excessive granulation tissue that protrudes above the surrounding skin and blocks re-epithelialization; it must be removed to allow the epithelium to restore itself.

**Why:** A short definition question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a contracture and where is it seen?</summary>

**Answer:** An exaggeration of normal wound contraction causing deformity (e.g. claw hand) or limited joint mobility, seen particularly on the palms, the soles and after serious burns.

**Why:** Definition + sites.
</details>

## 8. Fractures and fracture healing

**Fracture** = loss of bone integrity from mechanical injury and/or diminished bone strength; the **most common pathological condition of bone**.

### Types
| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Simple** | overlying **skin intact** |
| **Compound** | bone **communicates with the skin surface** |
| **Comminuted** | bone **fragmented** |
| **Displaced** | ends **not aligned** |
| **Stress** | slowly developing, after **repetitive loads** |
| **Greenstick** | only **partly through** the bone — **infants** (soft bones) |
| **Pathologic** | bone **weakened by disease** (e.g. tumour) |

⭐ Fracture healing is **unique** because it can be completed **without a scar**.

### Three phases
1. **Inflammatory**: torn vessels → **haematoma** fills the gap; the fibrin mesh seals it and is a **scaffold**; platelets and inflammatory cells release **PDGF, TGF-β, FGF**, activating **osteoprogenitor cells** (periosteum, medulla, soft tissue) and osteoclasts/osteoblasts.
2. **Repair**: haematoma → **granulation tissue**; by **end of week 1** a **soft callus (procallus)** anchors the ends; by **~2 weeks** → **bony callus** (osteoprogenitors lay **woven bone**; some cells form **fibrocartilage and hyaline cartilage** that undergo **endochondral ossification**); mineralization → **hard callus**.
3. **Remodelling**: the callus becomes fusiform and slowly disappears; **unstressed parts are resorbed** under weight bearing until the bone is restored as **lamellar bone** with the **medullary cavity** re-formed.

### Factors impairing fracture healing
- Type of injury (geometry, open injury, mechanism), treatment (fixation, gap size), gender, age.
- **Comorbidities**: diabetes, malnutrition, peripheral vascular disease, hypothyroidism, polytrauma.
- **Drugs**: **NSAIDs**, **corticosteroids**, antibiotics, anticoagulants; **smoking**; **alcohol**.
- **Infection** — especially in **open** fractures.
- ⭐ **Inadequate immobilization** → callus moves → **delayed union / nonunion**; persistent nonunion → cystic degeneration, lining by synovial-like cells → **false joint (pseudoarthrosis)**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Types: simple, compound, comminuted, displaced, stress, greenstick (infants), pathologic.
- Heals without a scar (unique).
- Inflammatory (haematoma, PDGF/TGF-β/FGF) → repair (soft callus week 1, bony callus ~2 weeks, endochondral ossification, hard callus) → remodelling (lamellar bone, medullary cavity).
- Poor immobilization → nonunion → pseudoarthrosis; NSAIDs, steroids, smoking, diabetes, infection impair healing.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the phases of fracture healing.</summary>

**Answer:** Inflammatory phase: a haematoma fills the fracture gap; its fibrin mesh seals the site and acts as a scaffold, and platelets and inflammatory cells release PDGF, TGF-β and FGF that activate osteoprogenitor cells. Repair phase: granulation tissue forms; by the end of the first week a soft callus (procallus) anchors the fragments; by about 2 weeks it becomes a bony callus of woven bone, with cartilage undergoing endochondral ossification, and mineralization produces a hard callus. Remodelling phase: under weight bearing, unstressed callus is resorbed until the bone is restored as lamellar bone with its medullary cavity.

**Why:** Three phases with their key events.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is pseudoarthrosis and how does it form?</summary>

**Answer:** Inadequate immobilization lets the callus move, preventing its maturation and causing delayed union or nonunion. If nonunion persists, the malformed callus undergoes cystic degeneration and its luminal surface becomes lined by synovial-like cells, creating a false joint (pseudoarthrosis).

**Why:** The chain from movement to false joint.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List factors that impair fracture healing.</summary>

**Answer:** Type of injury (fracture geometry, open injury, mechanism); fracture treatment (fixation, gap size); gender and age; comorbidities such as diabetes, malnutrition, peripheral vascular disease, hypothyroidism and polytrauma; medications (NSAIDs, corticosteroids, antibiotics, anticoagulants); smoking and alcohol; infection, especially in open fractures; and inadequate immobilization.

**Why:** Group them so nothing is missed.
</details>
