## 1. Cells of the nervous system & the neuron

- The nervous system has **two main classes of cells: neurons and glial cells.**
- ⭐ **Neuron = the functional unit** — the smallest unit of a tissue that can carry out the function of that tissue.
- Neurons are **"excitable cells"** that communicate by transmitting **electrical impulses**; **excitable cells** are cells **capable of producing large, rapid electrical signals called action potentials**.
- Most neurons have **three components**: a **cell body** and **two types of processes (neurites)** — the **dendrite(s)** and an **axon**.

- Two cell classes: neurons and glia.
- Neuron = functional unit; excitable cell producing action potentials.
- Three components: cell body (soma), dendrites, axon.

## 2. The parts of the neuron

### Cell body (soma / perikaryon)
- Contains the **nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and most of the free ribosomes**; **mitochondria** are in the cell body and throughout the neuron.
- Does most of what other cells do: **protein synthesis and cellular metabolism**.
- ⭐ **Mature neurons retain their nuclei but lose the ability to divide** → adults have all the neurons they will ever have (in most areas).

### Dendrites
- **Branch from the cell body** and **receive input from other neurons** at specialized junctions called **synapses** (cell bodies can also receive synapses).
- Branching pattern varies: **stellate** cells (star-shaped) vs **Purkinje** cells (highly branched).
- **More branching = more synapses** (most synapses occur on dendrites).

### Axon (nerve fiber)
- **Sends information** (a dendrite *receives* information).
- **Generally one axon per neuron**, but it can branch → **collaterals** (more branching = more communication with other cells).

### Myelin sheath
- **Protein–lipid complex** wrapped around the axon of many neurons.
- In the **PNS** it is formed by a **Schwann cell** wrapping its membrane around an axon **up to 100 times**.
- The sheath covers the axon **except at the nodes of Ranvier** — periodic **1 µm** gaps where the axon is **unmyelinated**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Soma: nucleus, ER, Golgi, ribosomes; protein synthesis and metabolism; neurons cannot divide.
- Dendrites receive (at synapses); axon sends (one axon, branches = collaterals).
- Myelin = protein–lipid complex; PNS: Schwann cell wraps up to 100 times; nodes of Ranvier = 1 µm unmyelinated gaps.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the cell body of a neuron.</summary>

**Answer:** The cell body (soma or perikaryon) contains the nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and most of the free ribosomes, with mitochondria in the soma and throughout the neuron. It carries out protein synthesis and cellular metabolism. Mature neurons keep their nuclei but cannot divide, so in most areas adults have all the neurons they will ever have.

**Why:** Organelles + functions + no division.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare dendrites and the axon.</summary>

**Answer:** Dendrites branch from the cell body and receive input from other neurons at synapses; the extent of branching indicates the number of synapses. The axon (nerve fiber) sends information; a neuron usually has one axon, which can branch into collaterals, the amount of branching reflecting the amount of communication with other cells.

**Why:** Receive vs send.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the myelin sheath and the nodes of Ranvier.</summary>

**Answer:** Myelin is a protein–lipid complex wrapped around the axon; in the peripheral nervous system a Schwann cell wraps its membrane around the axon up to 100 times. The sheath covers the axon except at the nodes of Ranvier, periodic gaps of about 1 µm where the axon is unmyelinated.

**Why:** Formation + gaps.
</details>

## 3. Structural classification of neurons

According to the **number of processes** leaving the cell body:

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Unipolar** | single process | **invertebrate** neuron |
| **Bipolar** | **two processes** (one dendrite side, one axon) | **bipolar cell of the retina** |
| ⭐ **Pseudounipolar** | single process that **splits into a peripheral and a central axon** | **dorsal root ganglion cell** (sensory neuron to skin and muscle) |
| **Multipolar** | **many dendrites + one axon** | **stellate, pyramidal and Purkinje cells** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Unipolar (invertebrate), bipolar (retina), pseudounipolar (dorsal root ganglion), multipolar (stellate, pyramidal, Purkinje).
- Pseudounipolar = one process that divides into peripheral and central branches.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify neurons structurally with an example of each.</summary>

**Answer:** According to the number of processes from the cell body: unipolar (invertebrate neuron), bipolar (bipolar cell of the retina), pseudounipolar (dorsal root ganglion cell) and multipolar (stellate, pyramidal and Purkinje cells).

**Why:** Four types + examples.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is a pseudounipolar neuron?</summary>

**Answer:** A neuron with a single process from the cell body that divides into a peripheral axon (to skin and muscle) and a central axon (to the CNS), as in dorsal root ganglion cells.

**Why:** The sensory neuron of the spinal nerves.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give three examples of multipolar neurons.</summary>

**Answer:** Stellate cells, pyramidal cells and Purkinje cells.

**Why:** Straight from the figure.
</details>

## 4. Functional classification & nerve fiber types

### Functional classes
| Class | Function |
|---|---|
| **Afferent (sensory)** | transmit information from **sensory or visceral receptors to the CNS** |
| **Efferent (motor)** | transmit information from the **CNS to effector organs** |
| ⭐ **Interneurons** | **99% of all neurons**; located **entirely in the CNS**; perform **all the functions of the CNS** |

### Nerve fibers by conduction velocity & diameter
- **Conduction velocity increases** with **larger diameter** and with **electrical insulation (myelin)**.

| Fiber | Examples |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Aα** | **largest diameter, fastest**: somatic motor and **proprioceptive** fibers |
| **Aβ** | sensory fibers of **fine touch and fine pressure** |
| **Aγ** | motor fibers to the **muscle spindle** |
| **Aδ** | sensory fibers of **acute (sharp) pain, crude touch and cold** |
| **B** | **preganglionic autonomic** fibers |
| ⭐ **C** | **smallest, unmyelinated**: **chronic pain, heat, gross pressure** and **postganglionic sympathetic** fibers |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Afferent → CNS; efferent → effectors; interneurons = 99% of neurons, entirely in the CNS.
- Velocity ↑ with diameter and myelin.
- Aα (motor, proprioception) fastest; Aβ (fine touch); Aγ (muscle spindle); Aδ (sharp pain, cold); B (preganglionic autonomic); C (chronic pain, heat, postganglionic sympathetic; unmyelinated).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify neurons functionally.</summary>

**Answer:** Afferent neurons carry information from sensory or visceral receptors to the CNS; efferent neurons carry information from the CNS to effector organs; interneurons make up 99% of all neurons, lie entirely within the CNS and perform all of its functions.

**Why:** Three classes.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What determines the conduction velocity of a nerve fiber?</summary>

**Answer:** Its diameter (velocity increases with a larger diameter) and the presence of electrical insulation by a myelin sheath.

**Why:** Two determinants.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Classify nerve fibers with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Aα — largest and fastest (somatic motor and proprioceptive); Aβ — fine touch and pressure; Aγ — motor to muscle spindles; Aδ — acute pain, crude touch and cold; B — preganglionic autonomic; C — smallest and unmyelinated (chronic pain, heat, gross pressure, postganglionic sympathetic).

**Why:** Six types in order of speed.
</details>

## 5. Glial cells

- **Glia** (Latin = **"glue"**) = the second class; **account for 90% of all cells** in the nervous system.
- Functions: **structural integrity** + **chemical and anatomical support** for neurons.

| Glial cell | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Microglia** | CNS | part of the **immune system**; **scavenger cells** like tissue macrophages — **remove debris** after injury or infection |
| ⭐ **Oligodendrocytes** | CNS | small, few processes; in **white matter → myelin**; in **gray matter → support neurons**; **one oligodendrocyte myelinates many axons** |
| ⭐ **Schwann cells** | PNS | **myelin of the PNS**; each cell forms a **segment of myelin ≈ 1 mm long**; **one Schwann cell → one axon**; gaps between segments = **nodes of Ranvier** |
| ⭐ **Astrocytes** | CNS | **most common glia in the CNS**, **star-shaped**; contact **capillaries and neurons**; **nutritive function**; form the **blood–brain barrier** |
| **Ependymal cells** | CNS | **line the central cavities of the brain and spinal cord** (ventricles, central canal) |
| **Satellite cells** | PNS | **surround cell bodies in the PNS**; functions like astrocytes |

- Many oligodendrocytes or Schwann cells are needed to myelinate a **single axon**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Glia = 90% of the cells of the nervous system; "glue".
- CNS: microglia (immune, scavengers), oligodendrocytes (myelin; one cell → many axons), astrocytes (most common, nutritive, blood–brain barrier), ependymal cells (line cavities).
- PNS: Schwann cells (myelin; one cell → one axon, ≈ 1 mm segment), satellite cells (surround cell bodies).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the types of glial cells and one function of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Microglia — immune scavengers removing debris; oligodendrocytes — CNS myelin and support of neurons in gray matter; Schwann cells — PNS myelin; astrocytes — nutritive function and formation of the blood–brain barrier; ependymal cells — lining the central cavities of the brain and spinal cord; satellite cells — surround cell bodies in the PNS with astrocyte-like functions.

**Why:** Six glial cells.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells.</summary>

**Answer:** Both form myelin. Oligodendrocytes are in the CNS and one cell sends projections to myelinate segments of many axons; Schwann cells are in the PNS and each Schwann cell forms a segment (~1 mm) of myelin around only one axon. Many cells of either type are needed to myelinate the whole length of one axon, with nodes of Ranvier between the segments.

**Why:** CNS vs PNS; many vs one axon.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the functions of astrocytes?</summary>

**Answer:** They are the most common glial cells of the CNS, star-shaped, contacting both capillaries and neurons; they are thought to have a nutritive function and take part in forming the blood–brain barrier.

**Why:** Link to capillaries.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Interneurons = 99% of neurons**, but **glia = 90% of all cells** of the nervous system.
- **Oligodendrocyte = CNS, one cell → many axons; Schwann cell = PNS, one cell → one axon.**
- **Pseudounipolar** neurons = dorsal root ganglion (sensory); **bipolar** = retina.
- **Aα = fastest/largest; C = slowest/unmyelinated**; **B = preganglionic autonomic**; **C includes postganglionic sympathetic**.
- **Aδ = acute (sharp) pain; C = chronic pain.**
- Neurons **cannot divide**; glia can.
- **Astrocytes** form the blood–brain barrier; **microglia** are the immune cells.
