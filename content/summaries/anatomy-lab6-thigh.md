## 1. Bones: femur and hip bone

- **Lower limb bones:** **pelvic girdle (2 hip bones + sacrum), femur, tibia and fibula, tarsus, metatarsus, phalanges.**
- **Femur** (**longest and strongest**): **upper end:** **head (fovea), neck, greater trochanter (trochanteric fossa), lesser trochanter, intertrochanteric line (anterior), intertrochanteric crest (posterior, quadrate tubercle)**; **neck-shaft angle 125°** (this lab). **Attachments:** **greater trochanter: abductors and lateral rotators; lesser trochanter: psoas major and iliacus; intertrochanteric line: iliofemoral ligament and capsule.**
- **Shaft:** smooth convex anterior surface; posterior: **gluteal tuberosity, pectineal and spiral lines, linea aspera (medial and lateral lips), supracondylar ridges, popliteal surface.** **Lower end:** **2 condyles** (lateral more prominent, popliteal groove), **patellar surface**, **intercondylar fossa**, **2 epicondyles**, **adductor tubercle** (medial epicondyle).
- **Hip bone = ilium + ischium + pubis:** **ilium:** crest, **ASIS, AIIS, PSIS, PIIS**, **3 gluteal lines**, **iliac fossa, iliac tuberosity, auricular surface**; **ischium:** body, **tuberosity, spine, ramus** (joins inferior pubic ramus = **ischiopubic ramus**); **pubis:** body, **crest, tubercle, superior and inferior rami.**
- **Inguinal ligament:** lower margin of the **external oblique aponeurosis**, **ASIS to pubic tubercle.**
- **Articulations:** **symphysis pubis (anterior), sacroiliac joint (posterior), hip joint (lateral).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Femur neck-shaft angle 125° in this lab.
- Hip bone = ilium + ischium + pubis, fused at the acetabulum.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the upper end of the femur.</summary>

**Answer:** Head with fovea, neck, greater trochanter with the trochanteric fossa, lesser trochanter, and the intertrochanteric line (front) and crest (back) between the trochanters.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the lower end of the femur.</summary>

**Why:** Slides 6-8.
</details>

## 2. Acetabulum, obturator foramen and sciatic foramina

- **Acetabulum:** **ilium + pubis + ischium**; separated by **triradiate cartilage before puberty**; **fuse at 15-17 years**, **complete at 20-25.** **Floor:** **acetabular fossa (rough, non-articular)** and **lunate surface (articular horseshoe)**; **acetabular notch** closed by the **transverse acetabular ligament**; **labrum** deepens it.
- **Obturator foramen:** below and in front of the acetabulum, **filled by the obturator membrane** except superiorly: **obturator canal** (**obturator vessels and nerve**).
- **Greater sciatic foramen:** **greater sciatic notch, sacrotuberous, sacrospinous and anterior sacroiliac ligaments.** **Lesser sciatic foramen:** **ischial tuberosity, ischial spine, sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Acetabulum complete at 20-25 years.
- Obturator canal: obturator nerve and vessels.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the acetabulum and its development.</summary>

**Answer:** A cup formed by the ilium, pubis and ischium separated by the triradiate cartilage until fusion at 15-17 years, complete by 20-25; floor with fossa and lunate surface, notch closed by the transverse ligament and deepened by the labrum.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the obturator foramen and canal.</summary>

**Answer:** A large opening below and in front of the acetabulum closed by the obturator membrane except superiorly, where the obturator canal transmits the obturator vessels and nerve.

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the borders of the sciatic foramina.</summary>

**Answer:** Greater: greater sciatic notch, sacrotuberous, sacrospinous and anterior sacroiliac ligaments; lesser: ischial tuberosity, ischial spine, sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments.

**Why:** Slide 12.
</details>

## 3. Relationship to other regions

- **Four gateways:** **gap between the inguinal ligament and pelvic bone; greater sciatic foramen; obturator canal; lesser sciatic foramen.**
- **Pelvic cavity** is divided by the **pelvic diaphragm (levator ani)** into the **true pelvis** (reproductive organs, bladder, rectum) and the **perineum** (external genitalia, anal region).
- **Abdomen ↔ thigh (gap below the inguinal ligament):** **3 muscles** (**psoas major, iliacus, pectineus**), **3 nerves** (**femoral, femoral branch of the genitofemoral, lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh**), **2 vessels** (**femoral artery and vein**), **lymphatics**; **a weak area → femoral hernia.**
- **Pelvis ↔ gluteal region (greater sciatic foramen):** **1 muscle (piriformis), 4 nerves (sciatic, superior and inferior gluteal, pudendal), 3 vessels (superior and inferior gluteal, internal pudendal)**; **obturator nerve and vessels** pass through the **obturator canal.**
- **Perineum ↔ gluteal region (lesser sciatic foramen):** **tendon of obturator internus**, **pudendal nerve and internal pudendal artery** (leave through the greater foramen, wrap around the **ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 3 muscles, 3 nerves, 2 vessels pass under the inguinal ligament.
- Greater foramen: 1 muscle, 4 nerves, 3 vessels.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What passes between the abdomen and the thigh?</summary>

**Answer:** Psoas major, iliacus and pectineus; the femoral, femoral branch of genitofemoral and lateral cutaneous nerves; the femoral artery and vein and lymphatics; the gap is a weak area for femoral hernia.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What passes through the greater sciatic foramen?</summary>

**Answer:** Piriformis; the sciatic, superior gluteal, inferior gluteal and pudendal nerves; the superior and inferior gluteal vessels and the internal pudendal artery.

**Why:** Slide 16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does the pudendal bundle reach the perineum?</summary>

**Answer:** It leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, curves around the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament and enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen with the obturator internus tendon.

**Why:** Slide 17.
</details>

## 4. Dissection images: gluteal and thigh muscles

- **Gluteal region figures:** **gluteus medius, obturator internus, quadratus femoris**; **sciatic nerve (black), nerve to quadratus femoris (green), pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels (blue), tendon of obturator externus (yellow), arteries of the trochanteric anastomosis (red).**
- **Medial compartment:** **obturator externus, pectineus, adductor brevis, adductor longus, adductor magnus (adductor and hamstring portions), gracilis.**
- **Femoral triangle dissection:** **sartorius, adductor longus, pectineus, iliopsoas, inguinal ligament**; contents: **femoral artery + branches + sympathetic plexus, femoral vein + tributaries, femoral nerve + branches, femoral sheath, lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh, femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve, deep inguinal nodes.** Anterior thigh: **tensor fasciae latae, sartorius, rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis.**

- Adductor magnus has adductor and hamstring parts.
- Femoral triangle floor: iliopsoas, pectineus, adductor longus.

</div>

## 5. Case study I: fracture of the neck of the femur

- **70-year-old woman tripped; pain, difficulty walking; limb shortened and laterally rotated; X-ray: fracture neck of femur.**
- **Why common in the elderly (women more):** **osteoporosis**, minor trip (indirect violence); **women:** **postmenopausal osteoporosis (estrogen deficiency)** and **smaller angle of inclination.**
- **Capsule:** **strong and thick**; **zona orbicularis** (inner circular fibers: collar, retains fragments) and **retinacula** (longitudinal anterior fibers supporting retinacular arteries).
- **Main support:** **extracapsular ligaments, especially the iliofemoral** (inverted Y, AIIS → intertrochanteric line; prevents falling backward and hyperextension). **Anterior relations:** **femoral artery, vein and nerve**; **posterior:** **sciatic nerve.**
- **Fractures:** **intracapsular (subcapital, cervical)** → **avascular necrosis**, **prosthesis**; **extracapsular (basal, intertrochanteric)** → no necrosis. **Complications:** **avascular necrosis**; **deformity: lateral rotation (short gluteal rotators + maximus), shortening (sartorius, rectus femoris, adductors, iliopsoas).** **True leg length: ASIS to medial malleolus.**
- **Dislocation:** **uncommon (strong stable weight-bearing joint)**; stabilized by **bony, ligamentous (spiral) and muscular factors** (reciprocal: anterior ligaments strongest, posterior medial rotators strongest). **Posterior dislocation** when **flexed, adducted, medially rotated**: **limb internally rotated, adducted, shorter**; with the hip **abducted** it comes with a **posterior acetabular lip fracture**; **complications:** deformity and **sciatic nerve injury.** **Anterior** (extension, abduction, lateral rotation) compresses the **femoral nerve and artery**; **central** (force along the shaft) pushes the head through the acetabular floor. **CDH:** loose capsule or ligaments; cannot abduct, short externally rotated limb, asymmetric folds; **Shenton's and Perkin's lines.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Intracapsular fractures: avascular necrosis; extracapsular: none.
- Posterior dislocation endangers the sciatic nerve.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why are femoral neck fractures common in elderly women?</summary>

**Answer:** Osteoporosis, especially postmenopausal estrogen deficiency, and a smaller angle of inclination make the bone weak after a minor trip.

**Why:** Slide 48.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare intracapsular and extracapsular fractures.</summary>

**Answer:** Intracapsular cut the nutrient and retinacular arteries and cause avascular necrosis needing a prosthesis; extracapsular interrupt only the nutrient artery.

**Why:** Slides 51-52.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare posterior, anterior and central dislocations.</summary>

**Answer:** Posterior (flexed, adducted, medially rotated; limb internally rotated and short; sciatic nerve risk), anterior (extension, abduction, lateral rotation; femoral vessels and nerve compressed), central (force along the shaft through the acetabular floor).

**Why:** Slides 55-57.
</details>

## 6. Case studies II and III

- **Case II (superior gluteal nerve injury):** **bullet injury to the left gluteal region; limp, sagging of the right hip on stepping on the left foot; Trendelenburg positive.** **Superior gluteal nerve:** motor branch of the **sacral plexus**, enters **above the piriformis through the greater sciatic foramen**, runs **between gluteus medius and minimus**, supplies them and **tensor fasciae latae.** **Trendelenburg sign:** tests **gluteus medius and minimus**; normal: the other pelvis **rises**; abnormal: the **good hip drops (waddling gait)**; seen in **superior gluteal nerve paralysis (L4, 5, S1)** and **poliomyelitis.**
- **Case III (sciatic nerve injury):** **gluteal injection → difficulty dorsiflexing and everting the foot; sensory loss on the dorsum of the foot and toes (except the lateral side of the little toe)** = **sciatic injury from a wrongly placed injection.** **Injection:** **upper outer quadrant**; **sciatic marking** by **3 points** (2.5 cm lateral to the midpoint of PSIS-ischial tuberosity; halfway between ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter; junction of upper ⅔ and lower ⅓ of the back of the thigh); **V-shaped triangle** over the ASIS and iliac crest; injection enters **gluteus medius.** **Sciatic supply:** **motor:** **hamstrings, ischial part of adductor magnus, all leg and foot muscles**; **sensory:** **all leg and foot except the medial side (saphenous).** **Effects:** weak knee flexion, foot drop, calf wasting, lost Achilles reflex, sensory loss, trophic ulcer.
- **Achilles tendon reflex:** **monosynaptic ipsilateral segmental stretch reflex; plantar flexion** from **gastrocnemius and soleus** contraction on sudden stretch; **tests S1.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Case II: Trendelenburg = superior gluteal nerve.
- Case III: foot drop and dorsal sensory loss = sciatic (common peroneal part).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Diagnose Case II and explain the sign.</summary>

**Answer:** Left superior gluteal nerve injury: paralysis of gluteus medius and minimus makes the opposite hip sag on the affected leg (Trendelenburg sign, waddling gait).

**Why:** Slides 63-64.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Diagnose Case III and name the effects.</summary>

**Answer:** Sciatic nerve injury from a gluteal injection: foot drop, loss of dorsiflexion and eversion, sensory loss on the dorsum of the foot and toes, with weak knee flexion, calf wasting and lost Achilles reflex.

**Why:** Slides 65-67.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the Achilles reflex?</summary>

**Answer:** A monosynaptic stretch reflex causing plantar flexion by gastrocnemius and soleus; it tests S1.

**Why:** Slide 68.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Superior gluteal nerve passes ABOVE the piriformis; the sciatic nerve passes below.**
- **Intracapsular fractures → avascular necrosis; extracapsular → none.**
- **Posterior dislocation: internally rotated, adducted, shorter limb.**
- **Greater foramen: 1 muscle, 4 nerves, 3 vessels; gap under the inguinal ligament: 3 muscles, 3 nerves, 2 vessels.**
- **Achilles reflex = S1; knee jerk = L3-L4.**
