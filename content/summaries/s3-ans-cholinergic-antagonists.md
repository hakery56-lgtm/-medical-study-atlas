## 1. Classification and general features

- **Cholinergic antagonists:** **nicotinic** (neuromuscular blockers; ganglionic blockers) and **muscarinic** (selective; nonselective).
- ⭐ **Muscarinic antagonists** (**antimuscarinic / parasympatholytic**): **competitive antagonists** blocking muscarinic receptors; effect **overcome by ↑ ACh or a muscarinic agonist**; **selective** (e.g. M1) or **nonselective**; natural or synthetic.
- **Natural alkaloids:** **deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna) → atropine**; **thorn apple (Datura stramonium) → scopolamine**; **tertiary amines**, lipid-soluble, absorbed and **cross the BBB**.

**Key points**
- Antimuscarinic = competitive, surmountable; belladonna → atropine; datura → scopolamine; tertiary amines cross the BBB.

## 2. Pharmacological effects of atropine (prototype)

| System | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Secretions** | **Salivary, lacrimal, bronchial, sweat inhibited at very low doses**; gastric slightly reduced; **mucociliary clearance inhibited** (ipratropium lacks this) |
| **Heart** | **Tachycardia (M2 block)**, modest (80–90/min), only parasympathetic tone removed; **paradoxical bradycardia at very low doses**; exercise response and **BP unaffected** |
| **Eye** | **Mydriasis, loss of light reflex, cycloplegia** (blurred near vision), **↑ IOP** (dangerous in **narrow-angle glaucoma**) |
| **GI tract** | **Motility inhibited** (larger doses, incomplete); **pirenzepine (M1)** ↓ gastric acid |
| **Other smooth muscle** | **Bronchodilatation**, prevents reflex bronchoconstriction; relaxes **biliary and urinary** tract → **urinary retention in elderly men with BPH** |
| **CNS** | **Excitation**: restlessness → agitation, disorientation |

- **Central muscarinic block** also affects the **extrapyramidal system**: ↓ tremor, involuntary movement and rigidity in **Parkinson's disease**; counteracts **antipsychotic extrapyramidal effects**. **Scopolamine**: **sedation** at low dose, excitation at high dose; **antiemetic** (motion sickness).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Atropine: ↓ secretions, tachycardia, mydriasis/cycloplegia, bronchodilatation, urinary retention, CNS excitation.
- BP and exercise response are unaffected.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the effects of atropine on the heart.</summary>

**Answer:** Tachycardia from M2 blockade, modest (up to 80-90 beats/min) because only existing parasympathetic tone is removed; paradoxical bradycardia at very low doses; exercise response and arterial pressure are unaffected.

**Why:** Slide "Effects on heart rate".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the effects of atropine on the eye.</summary>

**Answer:** Mydriasis with loss of the light reflex, cycloplegia (blurred near vision) and possible rise in intraocular pressure, which is dangerous in narrow-angle glaucoma.

**Why:** Slide "Effects on the eye".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the effects on smooth muscle and the CNS.</summary>

**Answer:** Bronchial, biliary and urinary smooth muscles relax (urinary retention in elderly men with BPH; reflex bronchoconstriction prevented); GI motility is partially inhibited; the CNS is mainly excited (restlessness, agitation, disorientation).

**Why:** Slide "Effects on other smooth muscle" and "Effects on the CNS".
</details>

## 3. Individual drugs

| Drug / group | Features | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Atropine** | Belladonna alkaloid, nonselective, well absorbed, **60% excreted unchanged**, crosses BBB, **t½ ~2 h** (eye effect **3 days–1 week**) | **Anesthesia adjunct, anticholinesterase poisoning, bradycardia, GI hypermotility** |
| **Scopolamine (hyoscine)** | Crosses BBB, **CNS depression**; absorbed from GIT and **skin (patch)** | **Drug of choice for motion sickness** |
| **Hyoscine-N-butyl bromide** | Synthetic derivative, CNS stimulant action | **GI, biliary, urinary antispasmodic** |
| **Dicycloverine (dicyclomine)** | Atropine-like | Antispasmodic |
| **Cyclopentolate, tropicamide** | Tertiary amines, **duration ~6 h** | **Ophthalmic mydriatics** |
| **Quaternary derivatives** (atropine methonitrate, homatropine, methscopolamine, propantheline) | **Poorly absorbed, no BBB, no CNS effects** | **GI hypermotility** |
| **Ipratropium (SAMA), tiotropium (LAMA)** | Quaternary; **no inhibition of mucociliary clearance**; inhaled; tiotropium **once daily** | **Asthma, COPD** |
| **Pirenzepine (M1)** | Selective | **Peptic ulcer** |
| **Oxybutynin, tolterodine, darifenacin (M3)** | Act on bladder; milder dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision | **Urinary incontinence** |
| **Benztropine, benzhexol, procyclidine, biperiden** | **Central** antimuscarinics (extrapyramidal) | **Parkinsonism, antipsychotic EPS** |

- **Atropine side effects:** **urinary retention, dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, tachycardia, CNS disturbance**; slows gastric emptying (↑ ulcer symptoms). **Contraindications:** **glaucoma (esp. closed-angle), BPH**; caution in **infants with fever**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Scopolamine = motion sickness; quaternary derivatives = GI only; tiotropium LAMA once daily.
- M1 pirenzepine = ulcer; M3 = bladder.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare atropine and scopolamine.</summary>

**Answer:** Both cross the BBB; atropine causes CNS stimulation and is used for anesthesia adjunct, anticholinesterase poisoning, bradycardia and GI hypermotility; scopolamine causes CNS depression and is the drug of choice for motion sickness, available as transdermal patches.

**Why:** Slides "Atropine" and "Scopolamine".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the quaternary derivatives and ipratropium/tiotropium used for?</summary>

**Answer:** Quaternary derivatives (atropine methonitrate, homatropine, methscopolamine, propantheline) are poorly absorbed, lack CNS effects and treat GI hypermotility; ipratropium (short-acting) and tiotropium (long-acting, once daily) are inhaled bronchodilators for asthma and COPD that do not inhibit mucociliary clearance.

**Why:** Slide "Quaternary derivatives of atropine".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name selective muscarinic antagonists and their uses.</summary>

**Answer:** Pirenzepine (M1) suppresses gastric acid in peptic ulcer; oxybutynin, tolterodine and darifenacin (M3) inhibit bladder micturition in urinary incontinence; centrally acting benztropine, benzhexol, procyclidine and biperiden treat Parkinsonism and antipsychotic extrapyramidal effects.

**Why:** Slides "Selective muscarinic antagonists".
</details>

## 4. Atropine poisoning

- **Mnemonic:** **dry as a bone, blind as a bat, red as a beet, mad as a hatter** → **dry mouth, mydriasis, blurred vision, tachycardia, hot flushed skin, agitation, delirium**; **more serious in children**.
- **Atropine fever** = **hyperthermia from blocked thermoregulatory sweating** (dangerous in infants); **atropine flush** = **cutaneous vasodilatation** of head, neck, arms, trunk (may be diagnostic).
- **Antidote: physostigmine** (crosses BBB) — **great caution, indicated patients only** (may cause **convulsions**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Dry, blind, red, mad; fever and flush; antidote physostigmine with caution.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the features of atropine poisoning.</summary>

**Answer:** Dry mouth, mydriasis, blurred vision, tachycardia, hot flushed skin, agitation and delirium (dry as a bone, blind as a bat, red as a beet, mad as a hatter), more serious in children, with hyperpyrexia.

**Why:** Slide "Atropine poisoning".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain atropine fever and flush.</summary>

**Answer:** Fever results from blockage of thermoregulatory sweating (especially in infants); the flush is cutaneous vasodilatation of the head, neck, arms and trunk ("red as a beet") and may be diagnostic.

**Why:** Slide "Atropine poisoning".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the antidote and its precaution?</summary>

**Answer:** Physostigmine, because it opposes central muscarinic block, but it should be given with great caution and only to indicated patients as it may precipitate convulsions.

**Why:** Slide "Atropine poisoning".
</details>

## 5. Summary of clinical uses

| System | Use (drug) |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | **Sinus bradycardia** (e.g. after MI): **atropine** |
| **Ophthalmic** | **Pupil dilation**: **tropicamide, cyclopentolate** |
| **Neurological** | **Motion sickness**: **scopolamine**; **Parkinsonism / drug-induced movement disorders**: **benzhexol, benztropine** |
| **Respiratory** | **Asthma, COPD**: **ipratropium, tiotropium** |
| **Anesthetic premedication** | **Dry secretions, bronchodilatation**: **atropine** (less important now) |
| **Gastrointestinal** | **Endoscopy** (scopolamine); **IBS/diverticular disease** (dicycloverine); **peptic ulcer** (pirenzepine, now less used) |
| **Urinary** | **Incontinence**: **tolterodine, oxybutynin** |
| **Poisoning** | **Organophosphorus / carbamate insecticides, anticholinesterase overdose**: **atropine** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Eight uses: cardiac, eye, neurological, respiratory, anesthesia, GI, urinary, poisoning.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the clinical uses of antimuscarinic drugs.</summary>

**Answer:** Sinus bradycardia (atropine), mydriasis (tropicamide, cyclopentolate), motion sickness (scopolamine), Parkinsonism (benzhexol, benztropine), asthma and COPD (ipratropium, tiotropium), anesthetic premedication (atropine), GI antispasmodic/endoscopy/peptic ulcer, urinary incontinence (tolterodine, oxybutynin) and poisoning with organophosphates or anticholinesterases (atropine).

**Why:** Slide "Summary of major clinical uses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which antimuscarinic is used in anticholinesterase poisoning?</summary>

**Answer:** Atropine, for poisoning with organophosphorus or carbamate insecticides or anticholinesterase overdose.

**Why:** Slide "Summary of major clinical uses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What gastrointestinal uses exist?</summary>

**Answer:** Scopolamine to facilitate endoscopy by relaxing GI smooth muscle, dicycloverine as antispasmodic in irritable bowel syndrome or diverticular disease, and pirenzepine for peptic ulcer (used less since H2 antagonists and PPIs).

**Why:** Slide "Summary of major clinical uses".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Atropine tachycardia is modest and BP is unaffected; very low doses cause bradycardia.**
- **Quaternary derivatives do not cross the BBB; ipratropium/tiotropium do not inhibit mucociliary clearance.**
- **Scopolamine = sedation and motion sickness; atropine = excitation.**
- **Atropine is contraindicated in closed-angle glaucoma and BPH.**
- **Physostigmine antidote only with caution (convulsions).**
- **Pirenzepine = M1 (ulcer); oxybutynin/tolterodine/darifenacin = M3 (bladder).**
