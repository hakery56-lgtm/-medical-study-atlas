## 1. Roots, dermatomes and the plexus

- **Spinal nerve:** **ventral root = motor** (anterior horn; + preganglionic sympathetic T1–L2); **dorsal root = sensory**, cell bodies in the **DRG** (in/near the foramen). **Root avulsion is proximal to the DRG → SNAP preserved** despite an anaesthetic dermatome.
- **Cervical roots:** **8 roots, 7 vertebrae**; **C1–C7 exit above** their vertebra, **C8 between C7 and T1**, T1 downward exit below.

| Root | Key movement | Sensory area |
|---|---|---|
| **C5** | Shoulder abduction | Lateral arm |
| **C6** | Elbow flexion, wrist extension | Thumb, lateral forearm |
| **C7** | Elbow and finger extension | Middle finger |
| **C8** | Finger flexion | Little finger, medial hand |
| **T1** | Finger abduction/adduction | Medial arm and forearm |

- ⭐ **Brachial plexus:** **ventral rami C5–T1** (± C4/T2); roots between **scalenus anterior and medius**; enters axilla with the **axillary artery**. **Roots → trunks (superior C5-6, middle C7, inferior C8-T1) → divisions (behind clavicle) → cords (lateral, medial, posterior) → branches.** **Anterior divisions → lateral + medial cords (flexors); posterior divisions → posterior cord (extensors).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Root avulsion = preganglionic, SNAP preserved.
- Roots, trunks, divisions, cords, branches; anterior divisions → lateral + medial cords.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why are SNAPs preserved in a root avulsion?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the avulsion lies proximal to the dorsal root ganglion, so the sensory cell bodies and peripheral sensory axons remain intact even though the dermatome is anaesthetic.

**Why:** Slide "Segmental organisation of the spinal nerve".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the formation of the brachial plexus.</summary>

**Answer:** Ventral rami of C5–T1 form roots, which form three trunks (superior C5-6, middle C7, inferior C8-T1); each splits into anterior and posterior divisions behind the clavicle; these form lateral, medial and posterior cords around the axillary artery, giving the terminal branches.

**Why:** Slides "The brachial plexus" and "Components of the plexus".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the myotomes and dermatomes of C5 to T1.</summary>

**Answer:** C5 shoulder abduction/lateral arm; C6 elbow flexion and wrist extension/thumb and lateral forearm; C7 elbow and finger extension/middle finger; C8 finger flexion/little finger and medial hand; T1 finger abduction and adduction/medial arm and forearm.

**Why:** Slide "Dermatomes and myotomes".
</details>

## 2. Branches of the plexus

| Level | Branch (roots) | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Roots** | **Dorsal scapular (C5)** | Rhomboids, levator scapulae |
| **Roots** | **Long thoracic (C5–C7)** | Serratus anterior |
| **Superior trunk** | **Suprascapular (C5, C6)**; nerve to subclavius | Supraspinatus, infraspinatus |
| **Lateral cord** | **Lateral pectoral**; **musculocutaneous (C5–C7)** | Pectoralis major; biceps, brachialis, coracobrachialis |
| **Medial cord** | **Medial pectoral (C8, T1)**; **ulnar (C8, T1, C7)** | Pectorals; hand intrinsics, FCU, medial FDP |
| **Lateral + medial** | **Median (C5–T1)** | Forearm flexors, thenar |
| **Posterior cord** | **Upper/lower subscapular; thoracodorsal (C6–C8); axillary (C5, C6); radial (C5–T1)** | Subscapularis, teres major; latissimus; deltoid, teres minor; triceps, extensors |

- **Long thoracic palsy → medial winging** (serratus anterior): scapular medial border lifts on **pushing a wall**; weak elevation/abduction > 90°. **Trapezius palsy (accessory nerve) → lateral winging.**
- **Course:** **interscalene** (roots/trunks above subclavian artery), **retroclavicular** (divisions cross the first rib in the cervico-axillary canal), **axillary** (cords around the **2nd part** of the axillary artery; terminal branches at lower border of pectoralis minor). **Supraclavicular approach** = roots/trunks; **infraclavicular** = cords/branches.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Long thoracic = serratus anterior (medial winging); axillary = deltoid/teres minor; musculocutaneous = biceps group.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Name the branches of the posterior cord.</summary>

**Answer:** Upper and lower subscapular nerves, thoracodorsal nerve (C6-C8), axillary nerve (C5, C6) and radial nerve (C5-T1).

**Why:** Slide "Branches by level".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the long thoracic nerve palsy?</summary>

**Answer:** Serratus anterior paralysis causing medial winging of the scapula (seen when pushing against a wall) with weak forward elevation and abduction above 90°; trapezius palsy instead causes lateral winging.

**Why:** Slide "Long thoracic nerve palsy: winged scapula".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the course and relations of the plexus.</summary>

**Answer:** Roots emerge between scalenus anterior and medius; trunks lie in the posterior triangle above the subclavian artery; divisions pass behind the clavicle and across the first rib; cords surround the second part of the axillary artery, with terminal branches at the lower border of pectoralis minor.

**Why:** Slide "Course and relations".
</details>

## 3. Nerve injury classification and plexopathies

| Seddon type | Pathology | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| **Neurapraxia** | **Conduction block**, axon continuity preserved | **Good**, spontaneous recovery |
| **Axonotmesis** | **Axonal disruption**, endoneurial tubes intact | **Variable**, regeneration possible |
| **Neurotmesis** | **Complete transection** | **Poor** without repair |

- **Wallerian degeneration** follows axonotmesis; **axons regrow ≈ 1 mm/day**.

| Feature | Preganglionic (avulsion) | Postganglionic |
|---|---|---|
| **Horner syndrome** | May be present (C8, T1) | Absent |
| **Winged scapula / rhomboids** | Present with C5–C7 avulsion | Absent |
| **Paraspinal EMG** | Denervation | Normal |
| **SNAP** | **Preserved** | **Absent** |
| **MRI / myelography** | Pseudomeningocele, root defect | Normal |
| **Surgery** | **Nerve transfer** | **Repair or graft** |

- **Plexopathies:** **traumatic** (traction, penetrating, iatrogenic), **thoracic outlet syndrome**, **neuralgic amyotrophy (Parsonage-Turner)** (acute severe shoulder pain then weakness; upper trunk; most recover), **radiation** (delayed, painless, **myokymia**), **neoplastic** (**Pancoast**: lower trunk + Horner, pain early, MRI key).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Preganglionic: SNAP preserved, Horner, nerve transfer; postganglionic: SNAP absent, repair or graft.
- Neurapraxia good, axonotmesis variable, neurotmesis poor.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the Seddon classification.</summary>

**Answer:** Neurapraxia: conduction block with axon continuity preserved and good spontaneous recovery; axonotmesis: axonal disruption with intact endoneurial tubes and variable prognosis; neurotmesis: complete transection with poor prognosis without repair.

**Why:** Slide "Seddon classification".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate pre- from postganglionic lesions.</summary>

**Answer:** Preganglionic: Horner may be present, winged scapula with C5-C7 avulsion, paraspinal EMG denervation, preserved SNAP, root defect on MRI, treated by nerve transfer. Postganglionic: none of these, SNAP absent, repair or graft.

**Why:** Slide "Preganglionic versus postganglionic lesions".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe neuralgic amyotrophy and radiation plexopathy.</summary>

**Answer:** Neuralgic amyotrophy: acute severe shoulder pain then weakness over days, patchy often upper trunk, most recover over months to years. Radiation plexopathy: delayed by months to decades, usually painless progressive weakness with myokymia on EMG.

**Why:** Slide "Other plexopathies".
</details>

## 4. Erb and Klumpke palsy; thoracic outlet syndrome

| | **Erb (upper, C5–C6)** | **Klumpke (lower, C8–T1)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Posture** | **Waiter's tip**: shoulder adducted/internally rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated, wrist flexed | **Claw hand**: MCP hyperextension, IP flexion, intrinsic paralysis |
| **Weakness** | Deltoid, biceps, brachialis, supraspinatus, infraspinatus | Hand intrinsics |
| **Sensory loss** | Lateral arm and forearm | Medial forearm and hand |
| **Extra** | — | **Horner** if T1 sympathetics involved |
| **Mechanism** | **↑ head-shoulder angle** | **Forceful upward abduction** |

- **Populations:** **adults** — high-energy traction, penetrating, iatrogenic, avulsion common; **neonates** — shoulder dystocia, **≈ 1/1000 births**, upper pattern most frequent.
- **Prognosis:** **stretch** recovers over weeks–months; **avulsion** no recovery → **nerve transfers**; **rupture** → repair/graft; **physiotherapy** for all.
- **Thoracic outlet syndrome:** compression between neck and shoulder: **neurogenic (lower trunk, most common)**, **venous (subclavian vein; effort thrombosis)**, **arterial (stenosis, aneurysm, embolism)**. **Causes:** **cervical rib/fibrous band from C7**, trauma, overhead work, pregnancy. **Features:** neck/shoulder pain, finger numbness, **thenar wasting** (neurogenic), swelling/ischaemia (vascular). **Tests:** Roos, Adson, Wright (limited specificity), NCS/EMG, X-ray for cervical rib, MRI; **treatment:** physiotherapy first; **first rib resection + scalenectomy** for refractory/vascular.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Erb = C5-C6 waiter's tip; Klumpke = C8-T1 claw hand ± Horner.
- Neurogenic TOS most common; cervical rib; physio first.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare Erb and Klumpke palsy.</summary>

**Answer:** Erb (C5, C6): waiter's tip posture, weak deltoid, biceps, brachialis, supraspinatus and infraspinatus, lateral arm/forearm sensory loss, from increased head-shoulder angle. Klumpke (C8, T1): claw hand, medial forearm/hand sensory loss, Horner syndrome if T1 sympathetics are involved, from forceful upward abduction.

**Why:** Slide "Erb and Klumpke palsy".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the types and causes of thoracic outlet syndrome?</summary>

**Answer:** Neurogenic (lower trunk, the most common), venous (subclavian vein compression or effort thrombosis) and arterial (subclavian artery stenosis, aneurysm, embolism); causes include a cervical rib or fibrous band from C7, trauma, repetitive overhead activity and pregnancy.

**Why:** Slides "Thoracic outlet syndrome".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is TOS managed?</summary>

**Answer:** Supervised physiotherapy first; first rib resection with scalenectomy for refractory or vascular cases; diagnosis uses provocative tests (limited specificity), NCS/EMG, imaging and laboratory exclusion of mimics.

**Why:** Slide "Thoracic outlet syndrome: diagnosis and treatment".
</details>

## 5. Median nerve

- **Origin:** **lateral + medial cords**, C5–T1; **no branches in axilla/arm**. **Forearm:** all flexors **except FCU and medial FDP**; **AIN** → **FPL, lateral FDP, pronator quadratus**; **palmar cutaneous** → thenar skin. **Hand:** **LOAF** (lateral 2 lumbricals, opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis); sensory **lateral 3.5 palmar digits**.
- **Sites:** **elbow** (supracondylar fracture); **wrist** (Colles, stab, **CTS**).
- **High lesion:** forearm **supinated**, weak wrist flexion with **ulnar deviation**; **benediction hand** (FDS + lateral FDP paralysed; index/middle straight on fist); **AIN palsy** = no thumb IP flexion, **OK sign negative**.
- **Median palsy:** **ape hand**, flat thenar, loss of opposition/abduction/pincer; sensory loss lateral 3.5 digits; skin warmer/drier.
- **CTS:** compression under the **flexor retinaculum**; nocturnal pain/tingling of lateral 3.5 digits; **thenar skin spared** (palmar cutaneous branch superficial); risks: **pregnancy, diabetes, hypothyroidism, RA, repetitive work**; **Phalen, Tinel, EMG, ultrasound**; **splint/steroid, open or endoscopic release**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- LOAF; AIN = FPL + lateral FDP + pronator quadratus; benediction hand = high lesion.
- CTS spares thenar skin.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is supplied by the median nerve in the hand?</summary>

**Answer:** Motor via the recurrent branch (LOAF: lateral two lumbricals, opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis); sensory to the palmar lateral 3.5 digits and the dorsal distal phalanges.

**Why:** Slide "Median nerve in the hand".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the benediction hand and AIN palsy.</summary>

**Answer:** Benediction hand: on making a fist the index and middle fingers stay straight while ring and little fingers flex weakly, from paralysis of FDS and lateral FDP; AIN palsy: loss of thumb IP flexion with a negative OK sign.

**Why:** Slide "High median lesion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is thenar skin spared in CTS?</summary>

**Answer:** The palmar cutaneous branch passes superficial to the flexor retinaculum.

**Why:** Slide "Carpal tunnel syndrome: pathophysiology and features".
</details>

## 6. Ulnar nerve

- **Origin:** **medial cord**, C8–T1 (C7); no branches in axilla/arm; **behind the medial epicondyle**; forearm between the two heads of **FCU**.
- **Supply:** forearm **FCU + medial FDP**, palmar/dorsal cutaneous; hand **hypothenar, medial 2 lumbricals, adductor pollicis, all interossei**; sensory **medial 1.5 digits**.
- **Interossei:** **PAD** (palmar adduct), **DAB** (dorsal abduct); with lumbricals flex MCP and extend IP; tests: abduction vs resistance, **card test**, **Froment** sign.
- **Sites:** **elbow** (fracture/dislocation, medial epicondyle, bad operating-table position); **wrist** (stab, **hook of hamate** fracture, **Guyon canal**). **Guyon canal:** between **pisiform and hook of hamate**, roof **pisohamate ligament**; like CTS in the ulnar distribution; causes ganglion, handlebar pressure, hamate fracture.
- **Claw hand and ulnar paradox:** **low lesion** → marked claw (FDP intact); **high lesion** → **less claw** (medial FDP paralysed) but **weaker grip**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PAD and DAB; Froment tests adductor pollicis.
- Ulnar paradox: high lesion = less claw, worse function.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the ulnar nerve branches in the forearm and hand.</summary>

**Answer:** Forearm: FCU and medial FDP, palmar and dorsal cutaneous branches. Hand: hypothenar muscles, medial two lumbricals, adductor pollicis and all interossei; sensory to the medial 1.5 digits.

**Why:** Slide "Ulnar nerve: branches".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe Guyon canal syndrome.</summary>

**Answer:** Compression in the tunnel between the pisiform and hook of the hamate roofed by the pisohamate ligament; resembles carpal tunnel syndrome in the ulnar distribution; caused by ganglion, repetitive handlebar pressure or hamate fracture.

**Why:** Slide "Guyon canal syndrome".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain the ulnar paradox.</summary>

**Answer:** A low lesion shows marked ring and little finger clawing because FDP is intact; a high lesion shows less clawing because medial FDP is paralysed but causes greater disability from weaker grip.

**Why:** Slide "Claw hand and the ulnar paradox".
</details>

## 7. Radial nerve and comparison

- **Radial:** larger terminal branch of the **posterior cord** (C5–T1); **spiral groove**; divides at the lateral epicondyle into **superficial radial (sensory)** and **posterior interosseous (motor)**.
- **Branches:** axilla (long head triceps); **spiral groove** (medial/lateral triceps, anconeus, cutaneous nerves); **brachioradialis, ECRL**; **PIN** (remaining extensors + supinator); **superficial radial** (lateral 2/3 dorsum of hand, lateral 3.5 digits).
- **Injury:** **Saturday night/crutch palsy, humeral shaft/supracondylar fracture**; **wrist drop + finger drop**, **triceps spared** unless proximal to the groove; sensory loss **small area over the first dorsal web space**.

| Nerve | Cord/roots | Deformity | Key motor loss | Sensory loss | Common sites |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Median** | Lateral + medial; C5–T1 | **Ape; benediction** | Thumb opposition; FDS, FPL | Lateral 3.5 palmar digits | Wrist (CTS), elbow |
| **Ulnar** | Medial; C8, T1 | **Claw hand** | Hand intrinsics, adductor pollicis | Medial 1.5 digits | Medial epicondyle, Guyon canal |
| **Radial** | Posterior; C5–T1 | **Wrist drop** | Wrist and finger extension | First dorsal web space | Spiral groove, humeral fracture |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Radial: wrist drop, triceps spared, small web-space sensory loss.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the course and termination of the radial nerve.</summary>

**Answer:** The larger terminal branch of the posterior cord winds around the humerus in the spiral groove and divides at the lateral epicondyle into the sensory superficial radial nerve and the motor posterior interosseous nerve.

**Why:** Slide "Radial nerve: origin and course".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the causes and signs of radial nerve injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Saturday night palsy, crutch palsy and humeral shaft or supracondylar fracture; wrist drop and finger drop with triceps spared unless proximal to the spiral groove; sensory loss over a small area of the first dorsal web space.

**Why:** Slide "Radial nerve injury".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the three nerve lesions.</summary>

**Answer:** Median: ape/benediction hand, loss of thumb opposition and FDS/FPL, lateral 3.5 palmar digits. Ulnar: claw hand, loss of hand intrinsics and adductor pollicis, medial 1.5 digits. Radial: wrist drop, loss of wrist and finger extension, first dorsal web space.

**Why:** Slide "Comparison of median, ulnar and radial nerve lesions".
</details>

## 8. Evaluation and surgical strategy

- **Clinical:** **MRC 0–5**, **advancing Tinel**, sensory mapping, check **Horner** and **vascular injury**. **Electrodiagnosis:** **EMG/NCS at 3–4 weeks** (after Wallerian degeneration), repeat serially. **Imaging:** **MRI/MR neurography**, **CT myelography** (avulsion), **ultrasound**.
- **Timing:** **early (days)** sharp transection/vascular injury → explore and repair; **delayed primary (2–3 weeks)** contused wounds; **observation 3–6 months** closed traction injury (serial exam and EMG, operate if no recovery); **late (> 12–18 months)** endplates degenerate → **tendon/muscle transfer**.
- **Repair options:** **neurolysis, tension-free repair, nerve grafting (sural)**; **nerve transfers** (**Oberlin**: ulnar fascicle → biceps branch; **spinal accessory → suprascapular**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- EMG at 3–4 weeks; observe closed injuries 3–6 months; sharp injuries early.
- Late (> 12–18 months): tendon/muscle transfers.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Outline the evaluation of a nerve injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Clinically MRC grading, advancing Tinel, sensory mapping and checking for Horner and vascular injury; baseline EMG/NCS at 3-4 weeks then serially; MRI or MR neurography, CT myelography for avulsion and high-resolution ultrasound.

**Why:** Slide "Evaluation of a nerve injury".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the timing of surgery.</summary>

**Answer:** Early within days for sharp transection or vascular injury; delayed primary at 2-3 weeks for contused wounds; observe closed traction injuries for 3-6 months and operate if no recovery; after 12-18 months favour tendon or muscle transfer.

**Why:** Slide "Timing and surgical strategy".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the repair options.</summary>

**Answer:** Neurolysis, direct tension-free repair, nerve grafting (e.g. sural) and nerve transfers such as the Oberlin transfer (ulnar fascicle to biceps branch) and spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve.

**Why:** Slide "Timing and surgical strategy".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Preganglionic: SNAP preserved; postganglionic: SNAP absent.**
- **Medial winging = long thoracic; lateral winging = trapezius.**
- **Erb = C5-C6 waiter's tip; Klumpke = C8-T1 claw ± Horner.**
- **Benediction hand = high median lesion; ulnar paradox = high ulnar lesion looks less clawed.**
- **CTS spares thenar skin; radial palsy spares triceps unless proximal to the spiral groove.**
- **Observe closed traction injuries 3-6 months; repair sharp injuries early.**
