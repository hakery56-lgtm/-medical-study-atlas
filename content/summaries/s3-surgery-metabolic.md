## 1. Homeostasis and the response to injury

- **Homeostasis** = the **foundation of normal physiology**.
  - **Elective surgery:** **minimal access surgery** and **"stress-free" perioperative care** preserve homeostasis.
  - **Emergency surgery:** **resuscitation** (to restore homeostatic responses) and **critical care** (organ support) return the injured patient to a state where homeostasis is possible.

### The response to injury is GRADED
- **The more severe the injury, the greater the metabolic and immunological response.**
- **After elective surgery of intermediate severity:** a **transient, modest rise in temperature, heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), energy expenditure and WBC count.**
- **After major trauma/sepsis** these changes are accentuated → **SIRS (systemic inflammatory response syndrome), hypermetabolism, marked catabolism, shock and multiple organ dysfunction (MODS).**

### Immunological sequelae of major injury
- A **proinflammatory state** driven by the **innate immune system (macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells)** evolves into the **compensatory anti-inflammatory response syndrome (CARS)** characterised by **suppressed immunity and diminished resistance to infection**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Elective surgery: minimal access, stress-free perioperative care. Emergency: resuscitation + critical care.
- Response is graded: mild after elective surgery (↑ temp, HR, RR, energy expenditure, WBC); severe after major trauma/sepsis → SIRS, hypermetabolism, catabolism, shock, MODS.
- Pro-inflammatory state (innate immunity) → CARS (immunosuppression, infection risk).

</div>

## 2. Mediators of the metabolic response

### Neuroendocrine pathways
- **Afferent nociceptive neurones → spinal cord → thalamus → hypothalamus → pituitary.**
- **Hypothalamus releases CRF → anterior pituitary releases ACTH → adrenals secrete cortisol.**
- **Hypothalamic activation of the sympathetic nervous system → adrenaline release and glucagon release.**
- **Other changes:** altered **insulin release and sensitivity**; **hypersecretion of prolactin and GH**; **inactivation of peripheral thyroid hormones and gonadal function**.
- **GH functions:** **lipolytic, insulin-antagonising, proinflammatory.**

### Phases of the neuroendocrine response
| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Acute phase** (short-term survival) | **actively secreting pituitary**; **↑ counter-regulatory hormones: cortisol, glucagon, adrenaline** |
| ⭐ **Chronic phase** (chronic wasting) | **hypothalamic suppression**; **low serum levels of target-organ hormones** |

### Immune mediators and cytokines
- The **innate system (macrophages)** interacts with the **adaptive system (T and B cells)** in generating the metabolic response.
- **Proinflammatory cytokines: IL-1, TNFα, IL-6, IL-8** — **produced within the first 24 hours**; they:
  - act on the **hypothalamus → pyrexia** and **augment the hypothalamic stress response**,
  - act on **skeletal muscle → proteolysis**,
  - induce **acute-phase protein production in the liver**,
  - cause **peripheral insulin resistance**.

### SIRS
- Driven initially by **IL-1, IL-6 and TNFα**; followed rapidly by ↑ **cytokine antagonists and soluble receptors (IL-1Ra, TNF-sR)**; if **prolonged or excessive** → **CARS**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pathway: nociceptive afferents → hypothalamus (CRF) → pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal (cortisol); sympathetic → adrenaline + glucagon.
- Acute phase: ↑ cortisol, glucagon, adrenaline. Chronic phase: hypothalamic suppression, low target-organ hormones.
- Cytokines (IL-1, TNFα, IL-6, IL-8) within 24 h: fever, proteolysis, acute-phase proteins, insulin resistance.
- SIRS → antagonists (IL-1Ra, TNF-sR) → CARS if excessive.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the neuroendocrine pathway of the stress response.</summary>

**Answer:** Afferent nociceptive neurones carry signals via the spinal cord and thalamus to the hypothalamus and pituitary: the hypothalamus releases CRF, which increases ACTH from the anterior pituitary, and ACTH stimulates cortisol secretion by the adrenals; hypothalamic sympathetic activation also releases adrenaline and glucagon. Insulin, GH, prolactin, thyroid and gonadal hormones are also altered.

**Why:** Classic pathway.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate the acute from the chronic phase of the neuroendocrine response.</summary>

**Answer:** The acute phase (short-term survival) has an actively secreting pituitary and elevated counter-regulatory hormones (cortisol, glucagon, adrenaline); the chronic phase (chronic wasting) shows hypothalamic suppression and low target-organ hormone levels.

**Why:** Two phases.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the effects of proinflammatory cytokines in injury?</summary>

**Answer:** IL-1, TNFα, IL-6 and IL-8, produced within the first 24 hours, cause pyrexia and augment the hypothalamic stress response, induce skeletal-muscle proteolysis, stimulate hepatic acute-phase protein production and cause peripheral insulin resistance.

**Why:** They mediate the systemic response.
</details>

## 3. The "Ebb and Flow" model

### Ebb phase
- **At the time of injury; lasts 24–48 hours**; **can be attenuated by resuscitation but not abolished**.
- **Features:** **hypovolaemia, ↓ basal metabolic rate, ↓ cardiac output, hypothermia, lactic acidosis.**
- **Hormones:** **catecholamines, cortisol, aldosterone (renin–angiotensin system).**
- **Magnitude depends on** the **degree of blood loss** and **stimulation of somatic afferent nerves at the injury site**.
- **Role:** **conserve circulating volume and energy stores** for recovery and repair.

### Flow phase
- After resuscitation the ebb phase evolves into the **hypermetabolic flow phase** (= **SIRS**): **mobilisation of energy stores** for recovery and repair and replacement of damaged tissue.
- **Features:** **tissue oedema** (vasodilatation, capillary leak), **↑ basal metabolic rate**, **↑ cardiac output**, **raised temperature**, **leukocytosis**, **↑ O₂ consumption**, **↑ gluconeogenesis**.
- **Subdivided into:**
  - ⭐ **Catabolic phase (3–10 days):** ↑ **counter-regulatory hormones (catecholamines, cortisol, insulin, glucagon)** and **cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNFα)** → **fat and protein mobilisation**, **weight loss**, **↑ urinary nitrogen excretion**; **↑ insulin with marked insulin resistance → poor glycaemic control**.
  - ⭐ **Anabolic phase (weeks):** recovery and repair.
- **Complications** aggravate the neuroendocrine and inflammatory responses → a **vicious catabolic cycle**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Ebb (24–48 h): hypovolaemia, ↓ BMR, ↓ cardiac output, hypothermia, lactic acidosis; catecholamines, cortisol, aldosterone; conserves volume and energy.
- Flow = hypermetabolic (SIRS): oedema, ↑ BMR, ↑ CO, fever, leukocytosis, ↑ O₂ consumption, ↑ gluconeogenesis.
- Catabolic (3–10 days): fat/protein breakdown, ↑ urinary nitrogen, insulin resistance. Anabolic (weeks): recovery. Complications → vicious catabolic cycle.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the ebb phase.</summary>

**Answer:** It occurs at the time of injury and lasts 24–48 hours (attenuated but not abolished by resuscitation); characterized by hypovolaemia, decreased basal metabolic rate and cardiac output, hypothermia and lactic acidosis; regulated by catecholamines, cortisol and aldosterone; its role is to conserve circulating volume and energy stores.

**Why:** Survival-oriented phase.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the flow phase.</summary>

**Answer:** The hypermetabolic phase following resuscitation (corresponds to SIRS) that mobilises energy stores for repair: tissue oedema, increased basal metabolic rate, cardiac output, temperature, leukocytosis, oxygen consumption and gluconeogenesis; subdivided into a catabolic phase (3–10 days) and an anabolic phase (weeks).

**Why:** Energy mobilisation for recovery.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What happens in the catabolic phase?</summary>

**Answer:** Counter-regulatory hormones (catecholamines, cortisol, insulin, glucagon) and cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNFα) mobilise fat and protein, causing weight loss and increased urinary nitrogen excretion, with marked insulin resistance and poor glycaemic control; complications worsen this and create a vicious catabolic cycle.

**Why:** Muscle proteolysis and insulin resistance.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Ebb = 24–48 h, ↓ metabolism (hypothermia, ↓ CO);** **flow = hypermetabolic (fever, ↑ CO).**
- **Ebb hormones: catecholamines, cortisol, aldosterone.**
- **Catabolic phase: 3–10 days;** anabolic: weeks.
- **Cytokines appear within 24 h:** IL-1, TNFα, IL-6, IL-8.
- **CRF → ACTH → cortisol.**
- **Acute phase = ↑ hormones; chronic phase = hypothalamic suppression.**
- **SIRS → CARS (immunosuppression).**
- **Insulin is HIGH but resistance causes poor glycaemic control.**
