## 1. Definition and types of cell injury

- **Homeostasis** = the **intracellular environment is tightly regulated** and stays fairly constant.
- Under **physiologic stress** (e.g. ↑ workload of the heart) or **potentially injurious conditions** (nutrient deprivation) cells **adapt** → a **new steady state** that preserves viability and function.
- If **adaptive capability is exceeded** or the stress is **inherently harmful/excessive** → **cell injury**.
  - **Within limits → reversible** (cells return to baseline).
  - **Severe, persistent or rapid-onset stress → irreversible injury and cell death.**
- **Cell death** results from **ischemia, infections, toxins and immune reactions**, but is also **normal and essential** in **embryogenesis, organ development and tissue homeostasis**.
- Two major pathways of cell death: ⭐ **necrosis** and ⭐ **apoptosis**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Homeostasis → adaptation → injury (reversible → irreversible) → death (necrosis or apoptosis).
- Injury develops when adaptive capacity is exceeded or the stress is harmful.
- Cell death is also physiologic (embryogenesis, organ development, tissue homeostasis).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define homeostasis and explain when cell injury develops.</summary>

**Answer:** Homeostasis is the tightly regulated, fairly constant intracellular environment. Cells adapt to stress to reach a new steady state; injury develops when the adaptive capability is exceeded or the external stress is inherently harmful or excessive.

**Why:** Adaptation precedes injury.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. When is injury reversible and when does it become irreversible?</summary>

**Answer:** Mild or transient stress produces reversible injury and the cells return to baseline; severe, persistent or rapid-onset stress produces irreversible injury and cell death.

**Why:** Depends on the type, duration and severity of the stimulus.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the two major pathways of cell death and say where cell death is physiologic.</summary>

**Answer:** Necrosis and apoptosis. Cell death is normal in embryogenesis, organ development and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis.

**Why:** Not all cell death is pathologic.
</details>

## 3. Reversible and irreversible cell injury

### I. Reversible injury
- Deranged function and morphology **can return to normal if the stimulus is removed**.
- Cells and organelles **swell** because they take in water — **failure of energy-dependent ion pumps** of the plasma membrane → loss of ionic and fluid homeostasis.
- **Two main morphologic correlates:**
  1. ⭐ **Cellular swelling** — seen with **↑ plasma membrane permeability**; hard to see with light microscopy but obvious in the **whole organ**: **pallor** (capillary compression), **↑ turgor**, **↑ organ weight**. Microscopy: **small clear cytoplasmic vacuoles** (distended pinched-off **ER**) → **hydropic change / vacuolar degeneration**; cytoplasm may become **eosinophilic**.
  2. ⭐ **Fatty change** — **triglyceride-containing lipid vacuoles**, mainly in organs of **lipid metabolism, e.g. the liver**.

### Other intracellular changes
- **Plasma membrane:** **blebbing, blunting/distortion of microvilli, loosening of intercellular attachments**.
- **Mitochondria:** **swelling** and **phospholipid-rich amorphous densities**.
- **ER:** **dilation, detachment of ribosomes, dissociation of polysomes**.
- **Nucleus:** **clumping of chromatin**.
- **Cytoplasm:** **myelin figures** (from damaged membranes), ↑ eosinophilia.
- **Early ischemic injury:** surface blebs, ↑ cytoplasmic eosinophilia, swelling of occasional cells.

### II. Irreversible injury — "point of no return"
Three phenomena:
1. **Inability to restore mitochondrial function** (oxidative phosphorylation/ATP) even after the injury is removed.
2. **Loss of structure/function of plasma and intracellular membranes**, and **loss of DNA and chromatin integrity**.
3. **Injury to lysosomal membranes** → **enzymatic dissolution** of the cell = culmination of necrosis.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Reversible: cellular swelling (ion-pump failure → water entry; hydropic change) and fatty change (liver).
- Ultrastructure: blebs, mitochondrial swelling, ER dilation, ribosome detachment, chromatin clumping, myelin figures.
- Irreversible: no mitochondrial/ATP recovery, membrane and DNA damage, lysosomal rupture.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the morphology of reversible cell injury.</summary>

**Answer:** Cellular swelling (clear vacuoles from distended ER = hydropic change; organ pallor, ↑ turgor and weight) and fatty change (triglyceride vacuoles, mainly in the liver); ultrastructurally: membrane blebs, mitochondrial swelling, ER dilation with ribosome detachment, chromatin clumping and myelin figures.

**Why:** Failure of ion pumps leads to water entry.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why do injured cells swell?</summary>

**Answer:** Failure of the energy-dependent ion pumps in the plasma membrane causes loss of ionic and fluid homeostasis, so water enters the cell and organelles.

**Why:** ATP depletion.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What characterizes the point of no return (irreversible injury)?</summary>

**Answer:** Inability to restore mitochondrial function (ATP generation), loss of plasma/intracellular membrane and DNA/chromatin integrity, and lysosomal membrane injury causing enzymatic dissolution of the cell.

**Why:** Three phenomena of irreversibility.
</details>

## 4. Cell death: necrosis vs apoptosis

### Two types of death
- **A. Accidental** — severe disturbance (loss of O₂/nutrients, toxins, infection, trauma) → rapid, uncontrollable → **necrosis** (Greek *necros* = death); end result of severe damage.
- **B. Regulated** — milder injury or normal elimination → molecular pathways → **apoptosis**; death with features of both = **necroptosis**.

### Apoptosis
- A **"clean" cell suicide** eliminating cells with intrinsic abnormalities and **clearing fragments without inflammation**.
- **Pathologic situations:** (1) DNA/proteins **damaged beyond repair**, (2) **loss of survival signals**.
- **Physiologic situations** (**programmed cell death**): eliminates **unwanted cells in development** and **maintains constant cell numbers**:
  1. **After serving their purpose** (lymphocytes/neutrophils when inflammation ends)
  2. **Self-reactive T cells**
  3. **Embryogenesis** (e.g. regression of the fetal tail)
  4. **Odd cells** (virus-infected, cancer, transplanted cells)
  5. **Old cells in highly proliferating organs** (intestinal crypts)
  6. **Hormonal** (castration → prostatic atrophy; menses; menopause; breast after weaning)
- **Mechanism:** cells activate enzymes that degrade their **own nuclear DNA and proteins**; the **plasma membrane stays intact** but the cell becomes a **target for phagocytes**; **no inflammation**. Regulated by the **balance of death vs survival signals** and **caspases**; **two pathways converge on caspases: the mitochondrial pathway and the death-receptor pathway**.
- **Morphology (H&E):** **chromatin condensation → karyorrhexis**; cells **shrink**, form **cytoplasmic buds** and fragment into **apoptotic bodies** (membrane-bound cytosol and organelles).

### Necrosis vs apoptosis
| Feature | **Necrosis** | **Apoptosis** |
|---|---|---|
| Cell size | **enlarged (swelling)** | **reduced (shrinkage)** |
| Nucleus | **pyknosis → karyorrhexis → karyolysis** | **fragmentation into nucleosome-sized fragments** |
| Plasma membrane | **disrupted** | **intact** (altered lipid orientation) |
| Cellular contents | **enzymatic digestion; leak out** | **intact; released in apoptotic bodies** |
| Adjacent inflammation | **frequent** | **none** |
| Role | **invariably pathologic** | **often physiologic**; may be pathologic after DNA/protein damage |

### Timing of cell death
- Function is lost **long before** morphologic change: myocardial cells become **non-contractile after 1–2 min** of ischemia but may not die until **20–30 min**.
- EM changes of ischemic myocyte death appear **2–3 h** after death; light-microscope changes only **6–12 h** later.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Necrosis = accidental, pathologic, swelling, membrane rupture, inflammation. Apoptosis = regulated, shrinkage, intact membrane, apoptotic bodies, no inflammation.
- Apoptosis is physiologic (embryogenesis, hormonal, old cells, self-reactive T cells) or pathologic (DNA/protein damage).
- Caspases; mitochondrial and death-receptor pathways.
- Myocytes: non-contractile at 1–2 min; dead at 20–30 min; LM changes at 6–12 h.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare necrosis and apoptosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Necrosis: cell swelling, pyknosis → karyorrhexis → karyolysis, disrupted membrane, enzymatic digestion and leakage, frequent inflammation, always pathologic. Apoptosis: cell shrinkage, nuclear fragmentation, intact membrane, contents in apoptotic bodies, no inflammation, often physiologic.

**Why:** Table in the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Mention the physiologic causes of apoptosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Cells that have served their purpose (lymphocytes and neutrophils after inflammation), self-reactive T cells, embryogenesis (fetal tail regression), odd cells (virus-infected, cancer, transplanted), old cells in highly proliferating organs (intestinal crypt) and hormonal causes (castration → prostatic atrophy, menses, menopause, breast after weaning).

**Why:** Programmed cell death.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the mechanism and morphology of apoptosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Regulated by the balance of death and survival signals and by caspases, activated through the mitochondrial and death-receptor pathways; the cell degrades its own DNA and proteins, shrinks, forms cytoplasmic buds and apoptotic bodies, with chromatin condensation and karyorrhexis, and is cleared by phagocytes without inflammation.

**Why:** Clean cell suicide.
</details>

## 5. Necrosis — mechanisms and morphology

- **Necrosis** = cell death in which **membranes fall apart, enzymes leak out and digest the cell**; it **elicits inflammation** (induced by substances released from dead cells) that **removes debris and starts repair**.
- Enzymes come from **lysosomes** (of the dying cell or of recruited leukocytes). Necrosis is often the **culmination of irreversible reversible injury** that couldn't be corrected.
- **Mechanisms:** (1) **failure of ATP generation** (↓ O₂ or mitochondrial damage); (2) **membrane damage** (plasma + lysosomal) → leakage of enzymes; (3) **irreversible damage to lipids, proteins and nucleic acids** (e.g. by **ROS**).

### Morphology
- **Cytoplasm:** ↑ **eosinophilia** (denatured proteins bind eosin + loss of RNA basophilia); more **homogeneous** appearance (loss of glycogen).
- **Nucleus** — one of three patterns (breakdown of DNA/chromatin):
  | Pattern | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | ⭐ **Pyknosis** | nuclear **shrinkage** and **↑ basophilia** (condensed solid mass) |
  | ⭐ **Karyorrhexis** | the pyknotic nucleus **fragments** |
  | ⭐ **Karyolysis** | **fading** of chromatin basophilia due to **DNase** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Necrosis: membranes disrupted, lysosomal enzymes digest, inflammation follows.
- Mechanisms: ATP failure, membrane damage, ROS damage to lipids/proteins/DNA.
- Nuclear changes: pyknosis (shrunken, dark) → karyorrhexis (fragments) → karyolysis (fades, DNase).
- Cytoplasm: ↑ eosinophilia, homogeneous.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define necrosis and mention its mechanisms.</summary>

**Answer:** Cell death in which cellular membranes disintegrate and leaked enzymes digest the cell, eliciting inflammation. Mechanisms: failure of ATP generation, damage to plasma and lysosomal membranes, and irreversible damage to lipids, proteins and nucleic acids (e.g. by ROS).

**Why:** Slide "Necrosis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the nuclear changes of necrosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Pyknosis (nuclear shrinkage with increased basophilia), karyorrhexis (fragmentation of the pyknotic nucleus) and karyolysis (pale chromatin due to DNase activity).

**Why:** All due to DNA/chromatin breakdown.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does the cytoplasm of necrotic cells look more eosinophilic?</summary>

**Answer:** Eosin binds more to denatured cytoplasmic proteins and the normal blue basophilia from cytoplasmic RNA is lost.

**Why:** Staining change.
</details>

## 6. Patterns of tissue necrosis

| Pattern | Features | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Coagulative** | **firm** tissue; **cell details lost but tissue architecture preserved**; homogeneous eosinophilic cytoplasm, **no nuclei** | **ischemic damage in all solid organs EXCEPT the brain** (e.g. **wedge-shaped kidney infarct**) |
| ⭐ **Liquefactive** | **complete digestion** → thick **liquid** in a cystic cavity | **focal pyogenic bacterial infection** (**pus**, creamy yellow) and **ischemic brain destruction** |
| **Gangrenous** | not a distinct pattern; usually a **limb** that lost blood supply with coagulative necrosis in multiple layers = **dry gangrene**; with **bacterial superinfection** (liquefactive action) = **wet gangrene** | limb ischemia |
| ⭐ **Caseous** | **architecture completely lost**, no cellular outlines; **friable yellow-white "cheese-like"**; microscopically **pink and granular**; bordered by inflammation | **tuberculous infection** |
| ⭐ **Fat necrosis** | **chalky white** foci; **vague fat-cell outlines with bluish calcium deposits (saponification)** | **acute pancreatitis** (**activated pancreatic lipases** liquefy fat cell membranes); **traumatic fat necrosis of the breast** |
| ⭐ **Fibrinoid** | **homogeneous bright pink** deposits of **immune complexes + fibrin** in vessel walls | **immune reactions involving blood vessels** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Coagulative: ischemia in solid organs except brain; architecture preserved.
- Liquefactive: bacterial infection (pus) and brain ischemia.
- Gangrene: dry (coagulative) vs wet (with bacterial liquefaction).
- Caseous: TB, cheese-like, architecture lost. Fat: pancreatitis (lipases, saponification), breast trauma. Fibrinoid: immune vasculitis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare coagulative and liquefactive necrosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Coagulative: firm, cell detail lost but tissue architecture preserved, homogeneous eosinophilic anucleate cells; typical of ischemic infarcts in solid organs except the brain. Liquefactive: complete digestion of dead cells into a liquid in a cystic cavity; seen in focal pyogenic bacterial infections (pus) and ischemic brain destruction.

**Why:** Different enzymatic fate of the dead tissue.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate dry and wet gangrene and describe caseous necrosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Dry gangrene is a limb that lost its blood supply with coagulative necrosis of multiple tissue layers; wet gangrene occurs when bacterial infection adds liquefactive change. Caseous necrosis shows complete loss of architecture with a friable, yellow-white, cheese-like, pink granular appearance, seen in tuberculosis and bordered by inflammation.

**Why:** Gangrene is a clinical term; caseous is a specific pattern.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain the mechanism and appearance of fat necrosis.</summary>

**Answer:** In acute pancreatitis activated pancreatic lipases liquefy the membranes of fat cells in the pancreas and peritoneum, producing chalky white areas with ghost-like fat cell outlines and bluish calcium deposits (saponification); it also occurs in the breast after trauma.

**Why:** Fat + calcium = soap.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Necrosis swells; apoptosis shrinks.** Necrosis = **inflammation + membrane rupture**; apoptosis = **intact membrane, no inflammation**.
- **Coagulative necrosis = ischemia everywhere EXCEPT the brain (brain = liquefactive).**
- **Pyknosis (shrink) → karyorrhexis (fragment) → karyolysis (fade).**
- **Caseous = TB; fat necrosis = pancreatitis/breast trauma; fibrinoid = immune vessel injury.**
- **Gangrene is not a distinct pattern:** dry = coagulative; wet = + bacterial liquefaction.
- **Myocyte non-contractile at 1–2 min, dead at 20–30 min.**
- **Reversible:** cellular swelling and fatty change; **irreversible:** mitochondrial failure + membrane/DNA damage + lysosomal rupture.
- **Necroptosis** = death with features of both.
