## 1. Reflex, reflex arc and classification

- ⭐ **Reflex** = an **involuntary and nearly instantaneous** movement (or action) in response to a stimulus.
- **Reflex arc** = the nerve pathway of a reflex: **receptor → afferent fibers → integrating center → efferent fibers → effector organ**.

| Classified by | Types |
|---|---|
| **Development** | **Innate** (genetically/developmentally determined, **unconditioned**, e.g. hunger with the smell of food) and **acquired** (learned, **conditioned**, e.g. **Pavlov's dog**) |
| **Response** | **Somatic** (skeletal muscle contractions: superficial and stretch reflexes) and **visceral/autonomic** (smooth and cardiac muscle, glands) |
| **Processing site** | **Spinal** reflexes (spinal cord) and **cranial** reflexes (brain) |
| **Complexity of circuit** | **Monosynaptic** (one synapse) and **polysynaptic** (two to several hundred synapses) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Reflex = involuntary, almost instant response; reflex arc has 5 parts.
- Classified by development, response, processing site and complexity.
- Monosynaptic = one synapse; polysynaptic = many.

</div>

## 2. Somatic and autonomic reflexes

- **Somatic motor reflexes** can be **monosynaptic or polysynaptic**.
- ⭐ **All autonomic reflexes are polysynaptic**, with **at least one synapse in the CNS and another in the autonomic ganglion**; some visceral reflexes are **spinal reflexes**.
- **Autonomic pathway:** stimulus → receptor → sensory neuron → **CNS integrating center** → **preganglionic neuron** → **autonomic ganglion** → **postganglionic neuron** → target cell → response.
- **Skeletal muscle reflexes (proprioceptors):** proprioceptors in **skeletal muscle, joint capsules and ligaments** → sensory neurons → CNS integrates → **somatic (alpha) motor neurons** → **contractile skeletal muscle fibers**.
- **Examples of proprioceptors:** **muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ, joint receptors**.
- **Visceral movement:** contraction of **cardiac and smooth muscle**, moving material in hollow organs by changing the organ's shape; controlled by the **ANS as a reflex**; some muscle creates its **own action potentials** and responds to hormones or neighbors via **gap junctions**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Autonomic reflexes are always polysynaptic (CNS synapse + ganglion synapse).
- Proprioceptors: muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ, joint receptors.
- Visceral movement is controlled by the ANS as a reflex; gap junctions link visceral muscle cells.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the features of autonomic reflexes?</summary>

**Answer:** All are polysynaptic with at least one synapse in the CNS and another in the autonomic ganglion; some visceral reflexes are spinal reflexes.

**Why:** Slide "Autonomic reflexes".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the skeletal muscle reflex pathway and give examples of proprioceptors.</summary>

**Answer:** Proprioceptors in skeletal muscle, joint capsules and ligaments carry input via sensory neurons to the CNS, which integrates the signal; somatic (alpha) motor neurons carry the output to contractile skeletal muscle fibers. Examples are the muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ and joint receptors.

**Why:** Slide "Visceral movement".
</details>

## 3. Nerve fiber types

| Fiber | Diameter (µm) | Velocity (m/s) | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aα** | 12–20 | 70–120 | **Proprioception, somatic motor** |
| **Aβ** | 5–12 | 30–70 | Touch, pressure |
| **Aγ** | 3–6 | 15–30 | **Motor to muscle spindles** |
| **Aδ** | 2–5 | 12–30 | Pain, cold, touch |
| **B** | < 3 | 3–15 | **Preganglionic autonomic** |
| **C (dorsal root)** | 0.4–1.2 | 0.5–2 | Pain, temperature |
| **C (sympathetic)** | 0.3–1.3 | 0.7–2.3 | **Postganglionic sympathetic** |

| Group | Origin | Fiber |
|---|---|---|
| **Ia** | Muscle spindle, **annulospiral ending** | Aα |
| **Ib** | **Golgi tendon organ** | Aα |
| **II** | Muscle spindle, **flower-spray ending**; touch, pressure | Aβ |
| **III** | Pain and cold receptors; some touch receptors | Aδ |
| **IV** | Pain, temperature and other receptors | Dorsal root C |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Larger diameter = faster: Aα fastest (70–120 m/s), C slowest.
- Aγ = motor to spindles; B = preganglionic autonomic; sympathetic C = postganglionic.
- Ia = spindle annulospiral; Ib = Golgi tendon organ; II = flower-spray.

</div>

## 4. Muscle spindle

- ⭐ **Muscle spindles** control **muscle tone** and prevent **overstretching**; found in **all muscles**, **tonically active**, firing increases as the muscle stretches.
- A **tonic receptor** adapts **slowly** and keeps firing; a **phasic receptor** adapts **rapidly** and stops.
- A spindle is a collection of **6–8 specialized fibers** (**intrafusal**; fusiform shape) inside the muscle mass; they do not contribute to force; they signal **length** and **rate of change of length (velocity)**. The working fibers are **extrafusal**.
- Muscles for **fine movements** contain **more spindles** than muscles for posture/coarse movements.

| Fiber type | Signals |
|---|---|
| **Nuclear chain** (nuclei in a single row) | **Static length** |
| **Static nuclear bag** (nuclei in a bundle) | **Static length** |
| **Dynamic nuclear bag** | **Velocity** (rate of change of length) |

- **Typical spindle:** **1 dynamic bag + 1 static bag + ~5 chain** fibers.
- **Afferents:** **Group Ia (primary, annulospiral endings)** wrap all 3 types → **length and velocity**; **Group II (secondary, flower-spray endings)** innervate chain and static bag fibers only → **length only**.
- **Efferents:** **alpha motor neurons** → **extrafusal** fibers (power); **gamma motor neurons** → **intrafusal** fibers (contract slightly, keep the spindle **taut and sensitive to stretch** over a wide range of lengths).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Spindle = length and velocity detector controlling tone and preventing overstretch.
- Ia → length + velocity (all 3 fibers); II → length only (no dynamic bag).
- Alpha → extrafusal (force); gamma → intrafusal (sensitivity).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of the muscle spindle.</summary>

**Answer:** 6–8 specialized intrafusal fibers inside the muscle: typically 1 dynamic nuclear bag, 1 static nuclear bag and about 5 nuclear chain fibers; they signal muscle length and velocity.

**Why:** Slides "Muscle spindles" and "3 types of muscle spindle fibers".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare the Group Ia and Group II afferents.</summary>

**Answer:** Group Ia (primary, annulospiral endings) wrap around the central portion of all three intrafusal fiber types and signal length and velocity; Group II (secondary, flower-spray endings) innervate the ends of the nuclear chain and static bag fibers, not the dynamic bag, and signal length only.

**Why:** Slide "Innervation of muscle spindles — afferent fibers".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the role of alpha and gamma motor neurons?</summary>

**Answer:** Alpha motor neurons innervate extrafusal fibers and give the muscle its power; gamma motor neurons innervate intrafusal fibers, which contract only slightly, to keep the spindle taut and sensitive to stretch across a wide range of muscle lengths.

**Why:** Slide "Innervation of muscle spindles — efferent fibers".
</details>

## 5. Stretch reflex, alpha-gamma coactivation and Golgi tendon organ

- **Tonic activity:** the spindle sensory neuron is **tonically active**; **alpha motor neurons** receive tonic input, so extrafusal fibers keep **tension even at rest**.
- **Stretch reflex:** increased spindle firing → increased alpha motor neuron signaling → **muscle contracts**.
- ⭐ **Alpha-gamma coactivation:** if alpha fires **alone**, the muscle shortens, stretch on the intrafusal fibers falls and **spindle firing decreases**; if **gamma axons are cut**, the spindle **loses activity** when the muscle contracts. When **alpha and gamma fire together**, intrafusal fibers do not slacken and **spindle firing remains constant**.
- **Golgi tendon organ:** responds to **tension changes** and prevents injury from **excessively strong contractions**; when it fires, the efferent signal is **inhibitory** → **loss of contraction strength**.

| | **Muscle spindle** | **Golgi tendon organ** |
|---|---|---|
| **Senses** | **Length** (and velocity) | **Tension** |
| **Effect** | **Reflex contraction** (stretch reflex) | **Inhibition** of contraction |
| **Protects against** | **Overstretching** | **Excessively strong contraction** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Alpha-gamma coactivation keeps the spindle working during contraction.
- Cutting gamma axons → spindle silent when the muscle contracts.
- Golgi tendon organ = tension sensor with inhibitory output.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is alpha-gamma coactivation and why is it needed?</summary>

**Answer:** When an alpha motor neuron fires, the muscle shortens and would unload the spindle; the gamma motor neuron fires at the same time, so the intrafusal fibers do not slacken and the spindle's firing rate stays constant, maintaining spindle function during contraction.

**Why:** Slides "Alpha-gamma coactivation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the function of the Golgi tendon organ.</summary>

**Answer:** It is a sensory receptor in muscle that responds to tension changes and attempts to prevent injury from excessively strong contractions; when it fires, the efferent signal is inhibitory so contraction strength falls.

**Why:** Slide "Proprioceptors — Golgi tendon organs".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does the tonic activity of the muscle spindle produce?</summary>

**Answer:** The tonically active sensory neuron gives tonic input to alpha motor neurons, so the extrafusal fibers maintain a certain tension even at rest (muscle tone).

**Why:** Slide "Muscle spindles".
</details>

## 6. Knee jerk, flexion and crossed extensor reflexes

- ⭐ **Patellar tendon (knee jerk) reflex** = **monosynaptic stretch reflex** + **reciprocal inhibition** of the antagonist.
  1. **Stimulus:** tap on the tendon stretches the muscle.
  2. **Receptor:** muscle spindle stretches and fires.
  3. **Afferent path:** sensory neuron.
  4. **Integrating center:** sensory neuron synapses in the **spinal cord**.
  5. **Efferent path 1:** **somatic motor neuron** → **quadriceps contracts**, swinging the lower leg forward.
  6. **Efferent path 2:** **interneuron** inhibits the motor neuron to the **hamstring**, which stays relaxed (**reciprocal inhibition**).
- **Flexion (withdrawal) reflex:** a painful stimulus activates a **nociceptor**; the sensory neuron enters the cord and diverges; one collateral activates **ascending pathways** (pain sensation and postural adjustment); **flexors contract, extensors inhibited** → the foot is pulled away.
- **Crossed extensor reflex:** on the **opposite side**, **extensors contract and flexors are inhibited** as the weight shifts to the other leg, supporting the body.
- **Control:** reflexes are managed by the **spinal cord, cerebellum and brain stem**; no cerebrum is needed, but sensory input goes to the cerebrum for **awareness**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Knee jerk: monosynaptic stretch reflex with reciprocal inhibition (quadriceps contracts, hamstring relaxes).
- Flexion reflex withdraws the limb; crossed extensor reflex supports the body on the other side.
- Reflexes need no cerebrum; sensory input reaches it for awareness.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the steps of the knee jerk reflex.</summary>

**Answer:** A tendon tap stretches the muscle; the muscle spindle fires; the sensory neuron carries the signal and synapses in the spinal cord; a somatic motor neuron contracts the quadriceps (leg swings forward) while an interneuron inhibits the hamstring's motor neuron so it stays relaxed (reciprocal inhibition).

**Why:** Slides "Patellar tendon (knee jerk) reflex".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the flexion and crossed extensor reflexes.</summary>

**Answer:** A painful stimulus activates a nociceptor; the sensory neuron diverges in the cord: flexors contract and extensors are inhibited, pulling the foot away (withdrawal), while on the opposite side the extensors contract and flexors are inhibited as the weight shifts, supporting the body (crossed extensor reflex). A collateral also sends pain and postural information to the brain.

**Why:** Slides "Flexion reflex and the crossed extensor reflex".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which parts of the CNS manage reflexes?</summary>

**Answer:** The spinal cord, cerebellum and brain stem; they do not require the cerebrum, although sensory input is sent to the cerebrum so we are aware of what happens.

**Why:** Slide "Integration of muscle reflexes".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **All autonomic reflexes are polysynaptic**, not monosynaptic.
- **Group Ia = length AND velocity (all 3 bag/chain types); Group II = length only.**
- **Alpha neurons → extrafusal; gamma neurons → intrafusal.**
- **If alpha fires alone the spindle goes silent; alpha-gamma coactivation prevents this.**
- **Golgi tendon organ = tension, inhibitory output; muscle spindle = length, excitatory stretch reflex.**
- **Knee jerk is monosynaptic but includes an interneuron for reciprocal inhibition of the hamstring.**
- **Ia and Ib are both Aα fibers;** II is Aβ.
