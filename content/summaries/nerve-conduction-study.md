# Basic Nerve Conduction Study — Exam Night Review

## 1. What NCS is and when to use it

- **NCS = diagnostic test of electrical conduction in motor and sensory nerves.**
- **Indications:** **pathophysiology, severity, level of the neurologic deficit, prognosis.** Used mainly for **paresthesias (numbness, tingling, burning) and/or muscle weakness**, with history and examination guiding the study.
- **Often combined with needle EMG.** May be indicated in limb pain, weakness from spinal nerve compression, and axial lumbar, thoracic or cervical pain.
- ⭐ **Disorders:** **carpal tunnel, cubital tunnel, Guillain-Barré, Guyon's canal, peripheral neuropathy, peroneal neuropathy, disc herniation, tarsal tunnel, ulnar neuropathy.**
- ⭐ **Components:** **motor NCS, sensory NCS, F-wave, H-reflex.**

**Key points**
- NCS tests conduction; it localizes, quantifies and gives prognosis.
- Four components: motor, sensory, F wave, H reflex.

## 2. Motor NCS and the CMAP

- ⭐ **Records the CMAP (M response):** **biphasic, initial negativity.** Measured per site: **latency, amplitude, duration, area.** **Conduction velocity needs two sites (distal and proximal).**
- **Set-up:** **active electrode over the muscle belly (motor endplate)**, **reference distal 3-4 cm**, **stimulator over the nerve with the cathode nearest the recording electrode.**
- **CMAP = summation of all muscle fiber action potentials.** Raise current until the CMAP stops growing, then add **20%** (**supramaximal**; usually **20-50 mA**).

| Parameter | Meaning | Normal / notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Latency (ms)** | **Stimulus onset → initial negative deflection**; = **nerve conduction + NMJ delay + muscle depolarization** | Only major difference between proximal and distal CMAPs |
| **Conduction velocity** | **Fastest axons**; **distance (mm) ÷ (proximal − distal latency, ms)** | **> 50 m/s upper limb; > 40 m/s lower limb** |
| **Amplitude** | **Number of depolarizing muscle fibers** (baseline-to-peak) | **> 3 mV**; low in **axon loss** or **demyelinating conduction block** |
| **Duration** | **Initial deflection → first baseline crossing**; **increases with slowing of some fibers (demyelination)** | **14-16.5 ms** |
| **Area** | **Amplitude × duration** | Baseline to negative peak |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Velocity = distance ÷ latency difference between two sites.
- Low amplitude = axonal loss (or conduction block); prolonged duration = demyelination.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How do you calculate conduction velocity?</summary>

**Answer:** Divide the distance between the proximal and distal stimulation sites (mm) by the proximal latency minus the distal latency (ms).

**Why:** Slide "Conduction velocity".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the components of CMAP latency?</summary>

**Answer:** Nerve conduction time, delay across the neuromuscular junction and depolarization time across the muscle.

**Why:** Slide "Latency".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What causes low CMAP amplitude and prolonged duration?</summary>

**Answer:** Low amplitude: loss of axons or conduction block from demyelination. Prolonged duration: slowing of some motor fibers, e.g. demyelination.

**Why:** Slides "Amplitude" and "Duration".
</details>

## 3. Sensory NCS and the SNAP

- **Recording:** electrodes **G1 (active, nearest the stimulator) and G2, 3-4 cm apart**; **ring electrodes for fingers.**
- ⭐ **SNAP:** **biphasic or triphasic, initial negative deflection.** **Sensory fibers have a lower stimulation threshold than motor fibers.**
- **Onset latency** reflects the **fastest fibers.** **Amplitude = sum of depolarizing sensory fibers; low = axonal degeneration.** **Only one stimulation site is needed for velocity.**
- ⭐ **Localization:** the **DRG** (in the intervertebral foramen, sensory cell body). **Lesions proximal to the DRG (root, cord) preserve the SNAP despite sensory symptoms** because **axonal transport from the DRG stays intact.**
- **Axonal loss:** **amplitudes fall; conduction velocity normal or slightly slow; distal latency normal or slightly prolonged; morphology unchanged between sites.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Preserved SNAP + sensory loss = lesion proximal to the DRG.
- Axonal loss: low amplitude, near-normal velocity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does the SNAP help localize a lesion?</summary>

**Answer:** Lesions proximal to the DRG (root or spinal cord) leave the SNAP intact despite sensory abnormalities because axonal transport from the DRG continues.

**Why:** Slides "SNAP and localizing lesion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the findings in axonal loss?</summary>

**Answer:** Decreased amplitudes, normal or slightly slowed velocity, normal or slightly prolonged distal latency, unchanged morphology between sites.

**Why:** Slide "Axonal loss".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare sensory and motor studies.</summary>

**Answer:** Sensory: SNAP, lower threshold, one site gives velocity, ring electrodes for fingers; motor: CMAP, two sites needed for velocity.

**Why:** Slides "Sensory conduction studies" and "Velocity".
</details>

## 4. Late responses: the F wave

- **Late responses:** **F wave, H reflex, A wave.**
- ⭐ **F wave:** stimulation travels **directly to muscle (M response)** and **retrograde to the spinal cord neuron**, then is **re-propagated down the axon → delayed F response.**
- **Small late motor response, 1-5% of CMAP, supramaximal stimulation, pure motor, not a true reflex (no synapse).** **Configuration and latency vary** (different **anterior horn cells** each time), usually **polyphasic.**
- **Measures:** **minimal and maximal latency, chronodispersion, persistence.**
- **Significance:** **early mild polyneuropathy, proximal lesions (early GBS), plexopathy.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- F wave = retrograde then antegrade motor response, not a reflex.
- Useful for proximal lesions such as early GBS.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is the F wave produced?</summary>

**Answer:** Depolarization travels retrograde up the motor axon to the anterior horn cell and is re-propagated back down to the muscle as a delayed small response.

**Why:** Slide "F wave".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the F wave not a true reflex?</summary>

**Answer:** No synapse lies along the stimulated pathway; it is a pure motor response.

**Why:** Slide "F wave".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the clinical value of the F wave?</summary>

**Answer:** Detecting early mild polyneuropathy, isolated proximal lesions such as early GBS, and plexopathy.

**Why:** Slide "Significance of F wave".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Velocity needs two sites for motor, one for sensory.**
- **Motor velocity normal: > 50 m/s upper, > 40 m/s lower limb.**
- **F wave: 1-5% of CMAP, pure motor, not a reflex.**
- **Preserved SNAP with sensory loss = lesion proximal to the DRG.**
- **Duration is measured to the first baseline crossing.**
