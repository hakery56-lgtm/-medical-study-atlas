## 1. The electron transport chain

- ⭐ **ETC** = a series of **membrane-bound electron carriers** embedded in the **inner mitochondrial membrane**.
- Electrons pass from **NADH and FADH₂** to the complexes; each complex hands them to the next.
- With each transfer **energy is lost** and used to **pump H⁺ from the matrix to the intermembrane space** → **proton gradient**.
- NADH comes from glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle (FADH₂ from the Krebs cycle).

| Component | Name | Function | Figure details |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Complex I** | **NADH dehydrogenase** (NADH-Q oxidoreductase) | NADH (matrix) → **coenzyme Q** | **FMN, Fe-S** |
| **Complex II** | **Succinate dehydrogenase** (succinate-Q reductase) | **FADH₂** → coenzyme Q | **FAD, Fe-S** |
| **Coenzyme Q** | **Ubiquinone** | **Lipid-soluble quinone**; takes e⁻ from I and II and diffuses in the inner membrane to III | – |
| **Complex III** | **Cytochrome c reductase** (Q-cytochrome c oxidoreductase) | Coenzyme Q → **cytochrome c** | **Cyt b, cyt c₁, Fe-S** |
| **Cytochrome c** | – | Diffuses along the **surface** of the inner membrane: III → IV | – |
| **Complex IV** | **Cytochrome oxidase** | Cyt c → **O₂** → **H₂O**; **unique: contains copper** | **CuA, cyt a, cyt a₃-CuB** |

- ⭐ **Proton pumping at complexes I, III and IV** (figure: **4 H⁺, 4 H⁺, 2 H⁺**); **complex II does not pump**.
- Flow: **NADH → I → Q → III → cyt c → IV → O₂** (FADH₂ → II → Q).
- Slide 3 shows a **gluconeogenesis** diagram (irreversible steps bypassed by glucose-6-phosphatase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, PEP carboxykinase and pyruvate carboxylase); it is not discussed in the text.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ETC in the inner mitochondrial membrane; NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons.
- Complex I (NADH), II (FADH₂) → CoQ → III → cyt c → IV → O₂ → H₂O.
- Proton pumping at I, III, IV; complex IV contains copper.
- CoQ is lipid-soluble; cytochrome c is a surface carrier.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the electron transport chain and how does it create a proton gradient?</summary>

**Answer:** A series of membrane-bound electron carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electrons from NADH and FADH₂ pass from complex to complex, losing energy at each step; this energy pumps H⁺ from the matrix into the intermembrane space, establishing a proton gradient.

**Why:** Slides 4 and 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the four complexes and the two mobile carriers.</summary>

**Answer:** Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) passes electrons from NADH to coenzyme Q; complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) passes electrons from FADH₂ to coenzyme Q; coenzyme Q (lipid-soluble ubiquinone) delivers them to complex III (cytochrome c reductase; cytochrome b and c₁), which gives them to cytochrome c; cytochrome c carries them to complex IV (cytochrome oxidase, contains copper), which transfers them to O₂ to make water.

**Why:** Slides 9 to 11 and the complex figures.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Where does proton pumping occur?</summary>

**Answer:** At complexes I, III and IV; complex II does not pump protons.

**Why:** Slides 14 and 19 (review question 3).
</details>

## 2. ATP synthase and the chemiosmotic theory

- ⭐ **ATP synthase** = membrane-bound enzyme (**F₀ and F₁**) that uses the **proton gradient** to make **ATP from ADP + Pi**.
- The **higher negative charge in the matrix** attracts H⁺ back, and the accumulation of H⁺ in the intermembrane space drives them into the matrix; **most protons return through ATP synthase**.
- Intermembrane space: **low pH, high H⁺**; matrix: **low H⁺**.
- ⭐ **Chemiosmotic theory:** the energy for the high-energy bond of ATP is **not in a chemical bond but in a proton gradient**; the free energy change is sufficient for pumping at **I, III and IV**.

| Electron donor | ATP per electron pair (lecture) |
|---|---|
| **NADH** | **3 ATP** |
| **FADH₂** | **2 ATP** |

- **Complete oxidation of glucose to CO₂ = 36 to 38 ATP.**
- Note: the slide values (3 and 2 ATP per pair) are the older teaching figures; many current textbooks give about 2.5 and 1.5 ATP.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ATP synthase (F₀F₁) makes ATP from ADP + Pi using the proton gradient.
- Protons flow back mostly through ATP synthase.
- Chemiosmotic theory: energy stored as a proton gradient; pumping at I, III, IV.
- NADH 3 ATP, FADH₂ 2 ATP; glucose 36–38 ATP (lecture values).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is the proton motive force converted into ATP?</summary>

**Answer:** The matrix is more negative and the intermembrane space accumulates H⁺, so protons flow back into the matrix, mostly through ATP synthase, which uses this energy to synthesize ATP from ADP and Pi.

**Why:** Slide 12 (ATP synthase) and the ATP synthase figures.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. State the chemiosmotic theory.</summary>

**Answer:** The energy for the high-energy bond of ATP is not found in a chemical bond but in a proton gradient; there are three sites where the free energy change is enough for proton pumping: complexes I, III and IV.

**Why:** Slide 14 "Chemiosmotic Theory".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How many ATP come from NADH, FADH₂ and one glucose in this lecture?</summary>

**Answer:** 3 ATP per electron pair of NADH, 2 ATP per electron pair of FADH₂, and 36 to 38 ATP from complete oxidation of glucose to CO₂.

**Why:** Slide 14.
</details>

## 3. Uncouplers and inhibitors

- ⭐ **Uncouplers** allow the ETC to run but **prevent phosphorylation of ADP to ATP**: H⁺ crosses the membrane toward the **lower H⁺ side**, so the **proton gradient is not formed**; the inner membrane becomes **abnormally permeable to protons** and the energy is **released as heat**.
- **Physiological uncouplers:** **thermogenin, thyroxine, bilirubin**.

| Site | Inhibitors (lecture) |
|---|---|
| **Complex I** | **Insecticide and fish poison**; **barbiturates** (sedative) |
| **Complex II** | **Carboxin** |
| **Complex III** | **Antimycin**; "antidote of war gas" |
| **Complex IV** | **Carbon monoxide, cyanide (CN⁻), azide (N₃⁻), hydrogen sulphide (H₂S)** |
| **Oxidative phosphorylation** | **Oligomycin**: blocks proton flow through **F₀** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Uncouplers: ETC continues, no ATP, heat produced.
- Thermogenin, thyroxine and bilirubin are physiological uncouplers.
- Complex IV inhibitors: CO, CN⁻, N₃⁻, H₂S; complex II: carboxin; complex III: antimycin.
- Oligomycin blocks F₀ of ATP synthase.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are uncouplers and how do they work?</summary>

**Answer:** Substances that allow electron transport but prevent ADP phosphorylation. They let H⁺ cross the inner membrane toward the lower-concentration side, so no proton gradient forms; the membrane becomes abnormally permeable to protons and the energy is released as heat.

**Why:** Slides 16 and 17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name physiological uncouplers.</summary>

**Answer:** Thermogenin, thyroxine and bilirubin.

**Why:** Slide 17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the inhibitors of the ETC and oxidative phosphorylation.</summary>

**Answer:** Complex I: insecticide and fish poison, barbiturates; complex II: carboxin; complex III: antimycin (and an "antidote of war gas"); complex IV: carbon monoxide, cyanide, azide, hydrogen sulphide; oxidative phosphorylation: oligomycin, which blocks proton flow through F₀.

**Why:** Slide 18.
</details>

## 4. Pentose phosphate pathway (hexose monophosphate shunt)

- ⭐ **PPP / HMP shunt** = alternative route for **glucose oxidation** (described as an **anabolic pathway**) in the **cytoplasm**; **no ATP** is generated.
- **Two functions:** (1) **NADPH** for reductive biosynthesis; (2) **ribose 5-phosphate (R5P)** for **nucleotides** (ATP, NAD, FAD, CoA) and **RNA/DNA**.

| Phase | Type | Reactions | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | **Oxidative, irreversible** | **G6P dehydrogenase** (G6PD) → 6-phosphogluconolactone → **6-phosphogluconate hydrolase** → 6-phosphogluconate → **6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase** (CO₂) | **2 NADPH + CO₂**; **ribulose-5-P → ribose-5-P** |
| **II** | **Nonoxidative, reversible** | **Epimerase, isomerase, transketolase (TPP), transaldolase** | **Glyceraldehyde 3-P and fructose 6-P** (return to glycolysis) |

- **Phase II steps (figure):** ribulose-5-P → xylulose-5-P (3-epimerase) and ribose-5-P (isomerase); transketolase: xylulose-5-P + ribose-5-P → G3P + sedoheptulose-7-P; transaldolase → F6P + erythrose-4-P; transketolase: xylulose-5-P + erythrose-4-P → F6P + G3P.
- ⭐ **Regulation:** at **G6PD**, controlled by the **supply of NADP⁺**; **insulin induces G6PD**.
- Oxidative reactions are **irreversible**; nonoxidative reactions are **reversible**. Tissues with high need for **NADPH or R5P** use the PPP most.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PPP = cytosolic, produces NADPH and R5P, no ATP.
- Phase I oxidative irreversible (G6PD, 2 NADPH, CO₂); phase II nonoxidative reversible (transketolase with TPP, transaldolase).
- Regulated at G6PD by NADP⁺ and induced by insulin.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the functions of the pentose phosphate pathway?</summary>

**Answer:** It generates NADPH for reductive biosynthesis and converts glucose carbons into ribose 5-phosphate for nucleotide (ATP, NAD, FAD, CoA) and nucleic acid (RNA, DNA) synthesis; it does not generate ATP and operates in the cytoplasm.

**Why:** Slide 20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the two phases of the PPP.</summary>

**Answer:** Phase I is oxidative and irreversible: glucose-6-phosphate is dehydrogenated and decarboxylated to give a pentose (ribulose-5-phosphate, isomerized to ribose-5-phosphate) and 2 NADPH. Phase II is nonoxidative and reversible: transketolase and transaldolase reactions produce glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and fructose 6-phosphate.

**Why:** Slides 21 to 23 (including the phase figures).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is the PPP regulated?</summary>

**Answer:** At the first enzyme, G6PD, by the supply of NADP⁺ (more NADP⁺ lets the reaction proceed); insulin enhances the pathway by inducing G6PD.

**Why:** Slide 24 "Regulation of Pentose Phosphate Pathway".
</details>

## 5. NADPH, glutathione and the red cell

- **Significance of NADPH:** biosynthesis of **fatty acids, cholesterol, steroid hormones, neurotransmitters**; **detoxification** (microsomal **cytochrome P450 monooxygenase**; reduction of oxidized glutathione); **phagocytosis** in white cells.
- **In RBCs:** NADPH maintains **reduced glutathione**, which protects the membrane from **H₂O₂** (reduced to H₂O) and keeps hemoglobin iron in the **ferrous (Fe²⁺)** state, **preventing methemoglobin**.
- **Glutathione (GSH)** = **tripeptide: γ-glutamate, cysteine, glycine**; two GSH form **GSSG** by a **disulfide bond** between cysteine sulfhydryls when oxidized.

| Reaction | Enzyme |
|---|---|
| **H₂O₂ + 2 GSH → 2 H₂O + GSSG** | **Glutathione peroxidase** (contains **Se**) |
| **GSSG + NADPH → 2 GSH + NADP⁺** | **Glutathione reductase** (needs NADPH) |

- ⭐ **Erythrocytes** critically need the **oxidative PPP** to make NADPH, regenerate GSH and **repair oxidized membrane lipids**.
- **Disorder:** **G6PD deficiency** (named in the lecture; no details are given on the slide).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- NADPH: biosynthesis, detoxification, glutathione reduction, phagocytosis, keeps Hb iron as Fe²⁺.
- Glutathione peroxidase (Se) uses GSH to remove H₂O₂; glutathione reductase uses NADPH to regenerate GSH.
- RBCs depend on the PPP for NADPH; G6PD deficiency is the PPP disorder.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the roles of NADPH.</summary>

**Answer:** Biosynthesis of fatty acids, cholesterol, steroid hormones and neurotransmitters; detoxification of drugs by cytochrome P450 monooxygenase and reduction of oxidized glutathione; keeping RBC glutathione reduced and hemoglobin iron ferrous (preventing methemoglobin); phagocytosis by white blood cells.

**Why:** Slide "Significance of Pentose Phosphate Pathway".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain how glutathione protects the red blood cell.</summary>

**Answer:** Glutathione peroxidase (selenium-containing) uses 2 GSH to reduce H₂O₂ to water, forming GSSG; glutathione reductase then regenerates 2 GSH from GSSG using NADPH from the PPP. This protects the RBC membrane and lipids from oxidative damage.

**Why:** Slides 27 and 28 (role of NADPH in disposal of H₂O₂).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why do erythrocytes depend on the oxidative reactions of the PPP?</summary>

**Answer:** They need NADPH to regenerate oxidized glutathione through the glutathione peroxidase reaction so that it can continuously repair oxidized membrane lipids; the PPP produces no ATP but is their NADPH source.

**Why:** Slide with the PPP summary check-points.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Complex II does NOT pump protons; pumping is at I, III and IV.**
- **Complex I takes NADH; complex II takes FADH₂ (succinate dehydrogenase).**
- **Coenzyme Q = lipid-soluble, between I/II and III; cytochrome c = between III and IV.**
- **Complex IV is the only complex with COPPER; its inhibitors: CO, CN⁻, N₃⁻, H₂S.**
- **Uncouplers: electron transport continues, ATP is NOT made, heat is released.**
- **Oligomycin blocks F₀ (not an uncoupler).**
- **PPP makes NADPH (not NADH) and NO ATP; it is cytosolic.**
- **Oxidative phase = irreversible; nonoxidative phase = reversible.**
- **Slide ATP values: NADH 3, FADH₂ 2, glucose 36–38.**
