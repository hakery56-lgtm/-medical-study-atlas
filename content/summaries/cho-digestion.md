## 1. Complex carbohydrates & the stages of digestion

- Dietary **complex carbohydrates** = **starch, glycogen and cellulose** (all built from glucose units; **cellulose is not digested**).

### Stages
| Site | Enzyme | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Mouth** | **salivary α-amylase** | converts **starch → smaller polysaccharides (α-dextrins)** — **digestion starts here** |
| ⭐ **Stomach** | — | digestion **halts temporarily**: salivary α-amylase is **inactivated by the acidity (HCl)** — very little carbohydrate digestion occurs |
| ⭐ **Duodenum** | **pancreatic α-amylase** | hydrolyses the **α-1,4 glycosidic bonds** → **maltose, isomaltose, maltotriose** and branched/unbranched oligosaccharides |
| ⭐ **Jejunum (brush border)** | **sucrase, maltase, isomaltase, lactase** | hydrolyse the **disaccharides → monosaccharides**, which are then absorbed |

### The brush-border disaccharidases
| Disaccharide | Enzyme | Products |
|---|---|---|
| **Maltose** | **maltase** | **glucose + glucose** |
| **Isomaltose** | **isomaltase** | **glucose + glucose** |
| **Sucrose** | **sucrase** | **glucose + fructose** |
| **Lactose** | **lactase** | **glucose + galactose** |

- **α-limit dextrins** = oligosaccharides with **α-1,6 branches** left after amylase action (cleaved by isomaltase).
- End products: **glucose, fructose, galactose** → absorbed → **portal circulation to the liver**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Starch digestion begins in the mouth (salivary α-amylase → α-dextrins); it stops in the stomach (acid inactivates the enzyme); resumes in the duodenum (pancreatic α-amylase → maltose, isomaltose, maltotriose).
- Brush-border enzymes: maltase, isomaltase, sucrase, lactase → glucose, fructose, galactose.
- Cellulose is not digested (excreted).
- Lactose → glucose + galactose; sucrose → glucose + fructose; maltose and isomaltose → 2 glucose.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Where does carbohydrate digestion begin and why is there little digestion in the stomach?</summary>

**Answer:** It begins in the mouth, where salivary α-amylase converts starch into smaller polysaccharides (α-dextrins). Digestion halts temporarily in the stomach because the acidity (HCl) inactivates salivary α-amylase.

**Why:** Mouth → stomach pause.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe carbohydrate digestion in the small intestine.</summary>

**Answer:** Pancreatic α-amylase in the duodenum hydrolyses the α-1,4 glycosidic linkages of starch to give maltose, isomaltose, maltotriose and branched or unbranched oligosaccharides. Brush-border enzymes of the intestinal cells (sucrase, maltase, isomaltase and lactase) then hydrolyse the disaccharides into the monosaccharides glucose, fructose and galactose, which are absorbed.

**Why:** Luminal then membrane digestion.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the enzyme and products for maltose, isomaltose, sucrose and lactose.</summary>

**Answer:** Maltose — maltase — glucose + glucose; isomaltose — isomaltase — glucose + glucose; sucrose — sucrase — glucose + fructose; lactose — lactase — glucose + galactose.

**Why:** A 4-row table.
</details>

## 2. Overview of digestion along the GI tract

| Site | Substrates / enzymes |
|---|---|
| **Mouth** | starch, glycogen, sucrose, maltose, cellulose → **α-amylase** |
| **Stomach** | **low pH stops** salivary amylase |
| **Duodenum** | pancreatic amylase on starch, isomaltose, maltose, lactose, sucrose, cellulose |
| **Jejunum** | **oligosaccharidases (maltase, lactase, sucrase)** act on isomaltose, maltose, lactose, sucrose |
| **Products** | **glucose, fructose, galactose** → **portal circulation → liver** |
| **Cellulose** | **not digested** — excreted |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Mouth: α-amylase; stomach: stops; duodenum: pancreatic amylase; jejunum: oligosaccharidases.
- Monosaccharides go to the liver via the portal circulation; cellulose is excreted undigested.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Summarize carbohydrate digestion from the mouth to the jejunum.</summary>

**Answer:** Mouth — salivary α-amylase starts starch digestion; stomach — acid stops it; duodenum — pancreatic α-amylase continues; jejunum — brush-border oligosaccharidases (maltase, isomaltase, sucrase, lactase) produce glucose, fructose and galactose, which reach the liver through the portal circulation. Cellulose is not digested.

**Why:** One line per site.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Where do the monosaccharides go after absorption?</summary>

**Answer:** Through the portal circulation to the liver, the first destination of all absorbed dietary monosaccharides.

**Why:** Portal vein → liver.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which dietary carbohydrate is not digested and why?</summary>

**Answer:** Cellulose, because humans lack the enzyme that hydrolyses its β-linkages (standard biochemistry); it passes unchanged and is excreted.

**Why:** Fibre.
</details>

## 3. Lactose intolerance & the clinical case

### Case study 1
A **newborn** with **severe abdominal distension, severe pain and diarrhea after breast milk**; **urine contains a reducing sugar**.
- **Likely defect:** **lactase deficiency (lactose intolerance)** — the sugar in milk is lactose.
- **Confirmatory tests** (standard): **lactose hydrogen breath test / lactose tolerance test**, stool pH and reducing substances.
- **Treatment:** **lactose-free diet/formula** (avoid lactose or give lactase).

### Lactose intolerance — mechanism
- **Deficiency of lactase** → **lactose accumulates in the gut** → **bacterial fermentation in the large intestine** → **H₂ and CO₂ gases** and **low-molecular-weight acids (e.g. acetic acid)** that are **osmotically active**.
- **Abdominal cramps and flatulence** (gas) + **water drawn from the intestinal cells into the lumen** → **diarrhea and dehydration**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Lactase deficiency → lactose is not hydrolysed → colonic bacteria ferment it → gas (H₂, CO₂) + osmotically active acids → cramps, flatulence, osmotic diarrhea, dehydration.
- Treatment: avoid lactose (lactose-free diet).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the pathophysiology of lactose intolerance.</summary>

**Answer:** In lactase deficiency, lactose is not digested and accumulates in the gut; bacteria in the large intestine ferment it, producing H₂ and CO₂ gases and low molecular weight acids such as acetic acid. The gases cause abdominal cramps and flatulence, and the osmotically active products draw water from the intestinal cells into the lumen, causing diarrhea and dehydration.

**Why:** Fermentation → gas + osmotic water loss.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Discuss the clinical case of the breast-fed newborn with diarrhea and reducing sugar in the urine.</summary>

**Answer:** The likely defect is lactase deficiency (lactose intolerance): lactose in breast milk is not hydrolysed, ferments in the gut and causes distension, pain and osmotic diarrhea. Diagnosis is confirmed by tests such as the lactose hydrogen breath/tolerance test; treatment is a lactose-free feed.

**Why:** Diagnosis, test, treatment.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why does lactose intolerance cause diarrhea?</summary>

**Answer:** The undigested lactose and its fermentation products (acids) are osmotically active and pull water into the intestinal lumen.

**Why:** Osmotic diarrhea.
</details>

## 4. Absorption of glucose

### Lumen → intestinal cell (apical membrane)
- **Sodium-dependent glucose transporter 1 (SGLT-1)** — a **carrier that cotransports glucose with Na⁺**.
- The Na⁺ is later **expelled by the Na⁺-K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in)** using ATP → energy is needed **indirectly** = ⭐ **secondary active transport**.
- (Figure: **2 Na⁺ + 1 glucose/galactose** enter via SGLT; **fructose and mannose** enter via **GLUT-5**.)

### Intestinal cell → blood (basolateral membrane)
- **GLUT-2** releases glucose into the blood — **not sodium-dependent** (facilitated diffusion).

### Clinical application — oral rehydration therapy
- Treatment of **diarrhea**: **oral rehydration fluid contains glucose and sodium**; **glucose enables Na⁺ uptake (SGLT-1 cotransport)** to replenish body NaCl (and water follows).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Apical: SGLT-1 (Na⁺-glucose cotransport; secondary active transport). Basolateral: GLUT-2 (Na⁺-independent).
- The Na⁺-K⁺ pump keeps the Na⁺ gradient that drives SGLT-1.
- Oral rehydration fluid works because glucose carries sodium (and water) across the intestinal wall.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the absorption of glucose from the lumen to the blood.</summary>

**Answer:** Glucose enters the intestinal epithelial cell at the luminal (brush-border) surface by the sodium-dependent glucose transporter SGLT-1, which carries glucose together with sodium. The sodium is expelled by the Na⁺-K⁺ pump using ATP, so energy is needed indirectly (secondary active transport). Glucose leaves the cell on the side facing the capillaries by GLUT-2, a sodium-independent transporter, and enters the blood.

**Why:** Two transporters: SGLT-1 in, GLUT-2 out.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the Na⁺-K⁺ pump important for glucose absorption?</summary>

**Answer:** It removes the sodium that entered with glucose and maintains the low intracellular sodium and the sodium gradient that drives SGLT-1, so glucose uptake is secondary active transport.

**Why:** Indirect energy use.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain the principle of oral rehydration solution.</summary>

**Answer:** The solution contains glucose and sodium; the presence of glucose allows SGLT-1-mediated uptake of sodium, replenishing body sodium chloride (with water following) in patients with diarrhea.

**Why:** A clinical application of cotransport.
</details>

## 5. Glucose transporters (GLUT family)

| Transporter | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **GLUT1** | **RBC, brain, kidney, colon, retina, placenta** | glucose uptake in most cells |
| ⭐ **GLUT2** | **serosal surface of intestinal cells, liver, β cells of pancreas** (and kidney) | **low affinity**; glucose uptake in liver; **glucose sensor in β cells** |
| **GLUT3** | **neurons, brain** | **high affinity**; glucose into brain cells |
| ⭐ **GLUT4** | **skeletal and heart muscle, adipose tissue** | **insulin-mediated** glucose uptake |
| **GLUT5** | **small intestine, testis, sperm, kidney** | **fructose transporter**; poor ability to transport glucose |
| **GLUT7** | **liver endoplasmic reticulum** | glucose from the ER to the cytoplasm |
| **SGLT** | **intestine, kidney** | **cotransport** from the lumen into the cell |

### GLUT4 and insulin
- **GLUT4** = the major transporter of **skeletal muscle and adipose tissue**, **under insulin control**: **insulin induces movement of intracellular GLUT4 molecules to the cell surface** → ↑ glucose uptake.
- In **type 2 diabetes mellitus**, **membrane GLUT4 is reduced** → **insulin resistance** in muscle and fat cells.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- GLUT1 (RBC, brain), GLUT2 (liver, intestine serosa, β cells; low affinity; glucose sensor), GLUT3 (neurons; high affinity), GLUT4 (muscle, fat; insulin-dependent), GLUT5 (fructose), GLUT7 (liver ER), SGLT (intestine and kidney).
- Insulin moves GLUT4 to the surface; reduced GLUT4 in type 2 DM → insulin resistance.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Tabulate the glucose transporters, their locations and functions.</summary>

**Answer:** GLUT1 — RBC, brain, kidney, colon, retina, placenta — glucose uptake in most cells; GLUT2 — serosal surface of intestinal cells, liver, pancreatic β cells — low affinity, hepatic uptake, glucose sensor; GLUT3 — neurons and brain — high affinity; GLUT4 — skeletal and heart muscle and adipose tissue — insulin-mediated uptake; GLUT5 — small intestine, testis, sperm, kidney — fructose transporter; GLUT7 — liver ER — glucose from ER to cytoplasm; SGLT — intestine and kidney — cotransport from the lumen into the cell.

**Why:** A table question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is GLUT4 and how is it related to diabetes?</summary>

**Answer:** GLUT4 is the major glucose transporter in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, controlled by insulin, which moves intracellular GLUT4 molecules to the cell surface and so increases glucose uptake. In type 2 diabetes mellitus the number of GLUT4 molecules on the membrane is reduced, causing insulin resistance in muscle and fat cells.

**Why:** Insulin-regulated transporter.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which transporter carries fructose, and where is GLUT2 found?</summary>

**Answer:** GLUT5 carries fructose (it transports glucose poorly), found in the small intestine, testis, sperm and kidney. GLUT2 is found on the serosal surface of intestinal cells, in the liver, in pancreatic β cells and in the kidney.

**Why:** Two transporters, two locations.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- Digestion starts in the **mouth** (salivary **α-amylase**) and **pauses in the stomach** (acid).
- **Pancreatic α-amylase** cleaves **α-1,4** bonds; **isomaltase** handles the **α-1,6** branch points.
- **Sucrose** → glucose + **fructose**; **lactose** → glucose + **galactose**.
- **SGLT-1** = apical, **Na⁺-dependent**; **GLUT-2** = basolateral, **Na⁺-independent**; **GLUT-5** = **fructose**.
- Glucose absorption by SGLT-1 = **secondary active transport** (energy from the Na⁺-K⁺ pump).
- **Cellulose** is **not digested**.
- **GLUT4 is insulin-dependent** (muscle, fat, heart); **GLUT2 is not**.
- Lactose intolerance diarrhea is **osmotic** (fermentation products), not secretory.
