## 2. Drug metabolism

- ⭐ **The liver is the most important site of drug metabolism**, mostly by the **cytochrome P450 (CYP450)** enzyme superfamily.
- Why metabolism matters:
  1. It is **one of the main mechanisms of drug inactivation** (e.g. **ethanol**).
  2. Some drugs are **activated** by metabolism — **pro-drugs** (e.g. **enalapril → enalaprilat** by hydrolysis).
  3. Forming **polar metabolites from non-polar drugs** allows **efficient urinary excretion**.
- Some metabolites are **active with a longer half-life** than the parent: **diazepam** (t½ 20–50 h) → **desmethyldiazepam** (t½ ≈ 100 h).

### Phases of metabolism
| Phase | What happens | Products |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase I** | **modification** by introducing a **polar group (–OH, –NH₂)**: **oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis**; mostly **CYP450** | **active or inactive** |
| **Phase II** | **synthetic conjugation** reactions: **acetylation, methylation, glucuronidation, sulphation, glutathione/mercapturic acid conjugation** | **more polar**, excreted in **urine (or less often bile)**; **usually but not always inactive** |

- **P450 isoforms** show **considerable genetic variability** between individuals and racial groups.
- **Order can be reversed:** **isoniazid** is first **acetylated (Phase II)** then **hydrolyzed to isonicotinic acid (Phase I)**.
- **Phase I not involving P450:** **amine oxidation** (catecholamines, histamine), **alcohol dehydrogenation** (ethanol), **esterases** (pravastatin), **hydrolysis** (procaine).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Liver + CYP450 = main metabolic site; metabolism inactivates drugs, activates pro-drugs (enalapril → enalaprilat), and makes polar metabolites for excretion.
- Phase I = oxidation/reduction/hydrolysis (adds –OH, –NH₂); Phase II = conjugation (glucuronidation, acetylation, etc.), usually inactive and more polar.
- Isoniazid: Phase II then Phase I. Diazepam → desmethyldiazepam (t½ ≈ 100 h).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is drug metabolism important?</summary>

**Answer:** It inactivates drugs (e.g. ethanol), activates pro-drugs (enalapril to enalaprilat), and forms polar metabolites from non-polar drugs so that they can be excreted efficiently in the urine.

**Why:** Three points of the slide "Drug metabolism is important due to".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare Phase I and Phase II reactions.</summary>

**Answer:** Phase I introduces a polar functional group (–OH or –NH₂) by oxidation, reduction or hydrolysis, mostly by CYP450, and gives active or inactive products. Phase II are synthetic conjugation reactions (acetylation, methylation, glucuronidation, sulphation, glutathione conjugation) that give more polar, usually inactive metabolites excreted in urine or less often bile.

**Why:** Phase II increases polarity for excretion.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give an example in which the order of the phases is reversed.</summary>

**Answer:** Isoniazid is first acetylated (Phase II) and then hydrolyzed to isonicotinic acid (Phase I).

**Why:** Not every drug follows Phase I then Phase II.
</details>

## 3. Enzyme induction and inhibition

| | **Enzyme induction** | **Enzyme inhibition** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | **↑ enzyme activity**, usually by **↑ enzyme synthesis** | **↓ enzyme activity** |
| Examples | **nicotine, ethanol, rifampicin, carbamazepine** | **allopurinol, cimetidine, fluconazole, voriconazole, ritonavir, methotrexate, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs** |
| Consequences | **↑ activity of pro-drugs**; **↓ life span (t½) of most drugs** | **drug accumulation and toxicity** (↑ t½ and plasma conc.); **↓ efficacy of pro-drugs** → therapy failure |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Induction (rifampicin, carbamazepine, nicotine, ethanol) → faster metabolism, ↓ t½, ↑ pro-drug activation.
- Inhibition (cimetidine, fluconazole, ritonavir, allopurinol…) → accumulation, toxicity, ↓ pro-drug efficacy.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define enzyme induction and mention its consequences and examples.</summary>

**Answer:** A process in which enzyme activity is enhanced, usually through increased synthesis (nicotine, ethanol, rifampicin, carbamazepine); it increases the activity of pro-drugs and decreases the life span of most drugs.

**Why:** More enzyme = faster metabolism.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the consequences of enzyme inhibition?</summary>

**Answer:** Drug accumulation and toxicity (increased half-life and plasma concentration) and reduced efficacy of pro-drugs, which can cause therapeutic failure. Examples: allopurinol, cimetidine, fluconazole, ritonavir, methotrexate, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs.

**Why:** Less metabolism = more drug, less activation.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does enzyme inhibition affect a pro-drug such as enalapril?</summary>

**Answer:** The pro-drug is not converted efficiently to its active form (enalaprilat), so efficacy is reduced and therapy may fail.

**Why:** Pro-drugs need metabolism to become active.
</details>

## 4. Kinetics of metabolism

| | **First-order (linear)** | **Zero-order (non-linear)** |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | **Michaelis–Menten:** v = Vmax·[C] / Km | **[C] ≫ Km** → v = Vmax·[C]/[C] = **Vmax** |
| Rate of metabolism | **directly proportional to free drug concentration**; **not saturable** | **constant, independent of concentration**; **enzyme saturated** |
| Amount metabolized | a **constant fraction** per unit time | a **constant amount** per unit time |
| Drugs | most drugs | **aspirin, ethanol, phenytoin** (very large doses) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- First-order: rate ∝ concentration, not saturable.
- Zero-order: enzyme saturated, constant rate regardless of concentration (aspirin, ethanol, phenytoin).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate between first-order and zero-order kinetics.</summary>

**Answer:** In first-order kinetics the rate of metabolism and elimination is directly proportional to the free drug concentration (not saturable). In zero-order kinetics the enzyme is saturated, so a constant amount of drug is metabolized per unit time regardless of the concentration.

**Why:** Saturation of the enzyme at high doses.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give examples of drugs that follow zero-order kinetics and explain why.</summary>

**Answer:** Aspirin, ethanol and phenytoin; their doses are very large so [C] ≫ Km, the velocity equation becomes v = Vmax and the enzyme is saturated.

**Why:** Michaelis–Menten at high substrate concentration.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Write the equation of first-order metabolism.</summary>

**Answer:** v = rate of metabolism = Vmax [C] / Km.

**Why:** Michaelis–Menten when [C] is small.
</details>

## 5. Elimination of drugs

- Efficient excretion requires the agent to be **polar**; the **most important route is the kidney (urine)**.

### Renal elimination — 3 steps
1. **Glomerular filtration** — **free drug** (not bound to albumin) passes into **Bowman's space**.
2. **Proximal tubular secretion** — **two energy-requiring active transport systems** (**one for anions, one for cations**); drugs **compete** for the carriers (e.g. **probenecid with penicillin**).
3. **Distal tubular reabsorption** — concentration rises; **uncharged drug diffuses back** into the circulation.

### Ion trapping
- Altering **urine pH** to **increase the ionized form** in the lumen **increases clearance**.
  - **Weak acids** ↑ eliminated by **alkalinizing** urine (e.g. **phenobarbital overdose → bicarbonate**).
  - **Weak bases** ↑ eliminated by **acidifying** urine (e.g. **amphetamine → NH₄Cl**).

### Other routes
- **Intestines, bile, lungs** (**halothane, isoflurane**), **milk of nursing mothers**; small amounts in **sweat, saliva, tears, hair, skin**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Kidney = main route; filtration (free drug) → proximal secretion (anion/cation carriers, probenecid–penicillin competition) → distal reabsorption (uncharged).
- Ion trapping: alkalinize urine for weak acids (phenobarbital); acidify for weak bases (amphetamine).
- Other routes: bile, intestine, lungs (halothane, isoflurane), milk.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the steps of renal elimination of a drug.</summary>

**Answer:** (1) Glomerular filtration of free (unbound) drug into Bowman's space; (2) proximal tubular secretion by two active transport systems (anions and cations) with possible competition (probenecid and penicillin); (3) distal tubular reabsorption of the uncharged drug back into the circulation.

**Why:** Net renal clearance = filtration + secretion – reabsorption.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is ion trapping and how is it applied?</summary>

**Answer:** Changing the urine pH to increase the ionized form of the drug in the lumen so it cannot be reabsorbed and is eliminated faster: weak acids (phenobarbital overdose) by alkalinization with bicarbonate; weak bases (amphetamine) by acidification with NH₄Cl.

**Why:** Ionized drugs are not reabsorbed.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List other routes of drug elimination.</summary>

**Answer:** The intestines, bile, lungs (halothane, isoflurane), and milk in nursing mothers; sweat, saliva, tears, hair and skin to a small extent.

**Why:** Lecture slide "Other routes".
</details>

## 6. Important concepts

- ⭐ **Half-life (t½)** = the **time required to change the amount of drug in the body by one-half**.
- ⭐ **Steady state** = **drug intake = drug clearance**; a **maintenance dose** is needed to preserve it.
- **Creatinine clearance (Ccr):** creatinine is an **end product of muscle metabolism** (from **creatine phosphate**), **produced continuously and excreted in urine**; it assesses **renal function** — the **dose must be adjusted if Ccr is reduced**.
  - **Normal Ccr: men 97–137 mL/min; women 88–128 mL/min.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- t½ = time for the amount of drug in the body to fall by half.
- Steady state: intake = clearance; maintenance dose.
- Ccr assesses renal function; adjust dose when reduced; men 97–137, women 88–128 mL/min.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define half-life and steady state.</summary>

**Answer:** Half-life is the time required for the amount of drug in the body to change by one-half. Steady state is the state in which drug intake equals drug clearance; a maintenance dose is required to preserve it.

**Why:** Definitions from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is creatinine clearance and why is it clinically useful?</summary>

**Answer:** Creatinine is an end product of muscle creatine phosphate metabolism, produced continuously and excreted in urine; its clearance reflects the kidney's ability to eliminate drugs, so doses must be adjusted when it is reduced.

**Why:** Guides dosing in renal impairment.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the normal creatinine clearance values?</summary>

**Answer:** Healthy men 97–137 mL/min; healthy women 88–128 mL/min.

**Why:** Values given on the slide.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Phase II = conjugation (usually inactive, more polar); Phase I = oxidation/reduction/hydrolysis (active or inactive).**
- **Isoniazid:** Phase II (acetylation) **first**, then Phase I.
- **Induction ↓ t½; inhibition ↑ t½.** Inhibitors → **toxicity**; inhibitors of pro-drug activation → **failure of therapy**.
- **Zero-order drugs:** aspirin, ethanol, phenytoin (saturation).
- **Weak ACID → alkalinize urine (bicarbonate); weak BASE → acidify urine (NH₄Cl).**
- **Only free (unbound) drug is filtered.**
- **Probenecid competes with penicillin** at tubular secretion.
- **Diazepam t½ 20–50 h; desmethyldiazepam ≈ 100 h.**
