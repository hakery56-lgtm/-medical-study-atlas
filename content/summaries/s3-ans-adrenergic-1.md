## 1. Adrenergic drugs and neurotransmission

- ⭐ **Adrenergic drugs** act on **adrenoceptors (α and β)** stimulated by **adrenaline and noradrenaline.** **Sympathomimetics = agonists**; **sympatholytics = antagonists.**

| Step | Event | Drug targets |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Synthesis** | **Tyrosine → DOPA → dopamine → NE**; **tyrosine hydroxylase = rate-limiting** | **Metirosine** inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase |
| **2. Storage** | Dopamine enters vesicles via **VMAT**, becomes NE | **Reserpine** blocks VMAT (depletes stores) |
| **3. Release** | Action potential → **Ca²⁺ entry → exocytosis** | **Amphetamine** (indirect) increases release |
| **4. Receptor binding** | Postsynaptic **α and β**; presynaptic **α₂ reduce release** | **Clonidine** (α₂ agonist) |
| **5. Removal** | **Neuronal reuptake via NET** (dominant); MAO and COMT | **TCAs, cocaine, SNRIs** block NET |

- **Biosynthesis:** tyrosine —tyrosine hydroxylase→ **L-DOPA** —DOPA decarboxylase→ **dopamine** —dopamine β-hydroxylase→ **noradrenaline** —**PNMT** (adrenal medulla)→ **adrenaline.**
- **Termination of NA:** (1) diffuses out to the circulation, (2) **COMT** metabolism in the synapse, (3) **reuptake** (dominant); NET is **Na⁺/Cl⁻-dependent** and blocked by **imipramine (TCA), duloxetine (SNRI), cocaine** → more synaptic NA.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Tyrosine → L-DOPA → dopamine → NA → adrenaline; tyrosine hydroxylase is rate-limiting.
- Reserpine blocks VMAT; TCAs and cocaine block NET.
- Reuptake is the main way noradrenaline action ends.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the five steps of adrenergic neurotransmission.</summary>

**Answer:** Synthesis (tyrosine → DOPA → dopamine → NE), storage (VMAT), release (Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis), receptor binding (post-synaptic α and β, presynaptic α₂ feedback) and removal (mainly NET reuptake, plus MAO and COMT).

**Why:** Slide "Neurotransmission at adrenergic neurons".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which drugs act at synthesis, storage and reuptake?</summary>

**Answer:** Metirosine (tyrosine hydroxylase inhibitor), reserpine (blocks VMAT) and cocaine, tricyclics and SNRIs (block NET).

**Why:** Slides "Catecholamine biosynthesis" and "Termination of noradrenaline".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does blocking NET change the response to catecholamines?</summary>

**Answer:** Less reuptake leaves more noradrenaline in the synapse, enhancing the response.

**Why:** Slide "Drug detective".
</details>

## 2. Catecholamines versus noncatecholamines and mechanisms

| | **Catecholamines** | **Noncatecholamines** |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | **Catechol ring** (3,4-dihydroxybenzene) | **No catechol OH groups** |
| **Examples** | **Adrenaline, noradrenaline, isoproterenol, dopamine** | **Phenylephrine, ephedrine, amphetamine** |
| **Metabolism** | **Rapid COMT + MAO** | **Not COMT substrates; poor MAO substrates** |
| **Duration / route** | **Brief; ineffective orally** | **Prolonged; many effective orally** |
| **CNS** | **Poor penetration (polar)** | **Lipid soluble, CNS effects** |

- **Mechanisms:** **1. direct** (agonist or antagonist at the receptor: adrenaline, NA, isoproterenol, dopamine, phenylephrine; propranolol), **2. indirect** (block reuptake or cause release: **amphetamine, tyramine**), **3. mixed** (**ephedrine**).
- **Other targets:** **NET inhibition** (cocaine, tricyclics), **α₂ activation** (**clonidine**; **methyldopa** → α-methylnoradrenaline false transmitter), **MAO inhibition**, **store depletion** (**reserpine**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Catecholamines: potent, short-acting, not oral, poor CNS.
- Phenylephrine = direct; amphetamine = indirect; ephedrine = mixed.
- Clonidine/methyldopa act through α₂.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare catecholamines and noncatecholamines.</summary>

**Answer:** Catecholamines have a catechol ring, are potent, rapidly inactivated by COMT and MAO, short-acting, not orally effective and poorly CNS-penetrant; noncatecholamines lack the catechol OH groups, last longer, are lipid soluble, enter the CNS and are often oral.

**Why:** Slides "Catecholamines" and "Noncatecholamines".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Classify phenylephrine, amphetamine and ephedrine.</summary>

**Answer:** Phenylephrine direct, amphetamine indirect, ephedrine mixed.

**Why:** Slide "How drugs affect adrenergic transmission".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do clonidine and methyldopa lower noradrenaline release?</summary>

**Answer:** Clonidine activates presynaptic α₂ receptors; methyldopa forms α-methylnoradrenaline, a false transmitter with α₂ activity.

**Why:** Slide "Further drug targets".
</details>

## 4. Adrenaline

- ⭐ **Direct α₁, β₁, β₂ agonist.** **Uses:** **anaphylaxis: IM into the anterolateral thigh (first-line; adult 0.5 mg IM, 1:1000)**; **cardiac arrest: IV/IO**; **with local anesthetic** (↓ bleeding, ↑ duration); rare **topical mydriatic.** Subcutaneous use is historical.
- **Actions:**

| System | Receptor and effect |
|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular** | **β₁ ↑ HR and contractility** (↑ CO, ↑ O₂ demand); **β₁ renin release**; **α₁ vasoconstriction** (skin, viscera); **β₂ vasodilation** (skeletal muscle) |
| **Respiratory** | **β₂ bronchodilation**; **inhibits mast-cell mediator release** |
| **Hyperglycemia** | **β₂ ↑ hepatic glycogenolysis, ↑ glucagon**; **α₂ ↓ insulin** |
| **Lipolysis** | **β₁/β₂/β₃ → Gs → cAMP → PKA → hormone-sensitive lipase** → TG → FFA + glycerol |

- **Adverse effects:** **vasoconstriction (cold extremities, ischemia/necrosis), tachycardia, palpitations, arrhythmias, tremor**; caution in heart disease, hypertension, arrhythmias, hyperthyroidism, but **never delay IM adrenaline in anaphylaxis.**
- **Interactions:** **nonselective β blockers** → unopposed α → marked pressor response; **TCAs** → NET inhibition, potentiation, arrhythmias.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- IM thigh for anaphylaxis; IV/IO for cardiac arrest.
- β₁ heart/renin; β₂ bronchodilation + glucose; α₂ ↓ insulin; β lipolysis.
- β blockers or TCAs make adrenaline more dangerous.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give the clinical uses of adrenaline with routes.</summary>

**Answer:** Anaphylaxis (IM, anterolateral thigh), cardiac arrest (IV/IO), with local anesthetic (vasoconstriction reduces bleeding and prolongs action) and rarely as a topical mydriatic.

**Why:** Slides "Therapeutic uses of adrenaline" and "Direct-acting drugs: 1) adrenaline".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the metabolic and cardiovascular actions of adrenaline.</summary>

**Answer:** β₁ increases heart rate, contractility and renin; α₁ vasoconstricts skin and viscera while β₂ dilates muscle vessels; β₂ gives glycogenolysis and glucagon, α₂ lowers insulin, and β receptors cause lipolysis through Gs-cAMP-PKA.

**Why:** Slide "Actions of adrenaline".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What interactions make adrenaline hazardous?</summary>

**Answer:** Nonselective β blockers (unopposed α vasoconstriction, marked pressor response) and tricyclic antidepressants (NET inhibition potentiating catecholamines and arrhythmias).

**Why:** Slide "Adrenaline: interactions".
</details>

## 5. Noradrenaline

| | **Adrenaline** | **Noradrenaline** | **Isoprenaline** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Receptors** | **α + β** | **α ≫ β₁** | **β₁ + β₂** |
| **BP / HR** | SBP ↑, DBP variable, HR ↑ | **SBP ↑, DBP ↑, SVR ↑; reflex bradycardia** | HR ↑, CO ↑, **DBP ↓, SVR ↓** |
| **Link** | Anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest | **Vasopressor in shock** | Bradycardia / heart block |

- **Receptors:** **α₁ (Gq)** vasoconstriction → SVR ↑; **β₁ (Gs)** ↑ contractility (**chronotropy masked by reflex bradycardia**); **α₂ (Gi)** modulates release; **β₂ minimal.**
- **Effects:** ↑ SBP and DBP, ↑ afterload and O₂ demand, **baroreceptor reflex → vagal ↑ → reflex bradycardia**, renal/splanchnic flow may fall.
- ⭐ **Use:** **first-line vasopressor in septic shock** when hypotension persists after fluids; **continuous IV infusion**, titrated with BP/ECG monitoring; **central line preferred.**
- **Adverse effects:** **peripheral/digital ischemia**, **arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, hypertension**; **extravasation → local necrosis** (stop infusion; treat per protocol, e.g. **phentolamine**). **Monitor perfusion, not BP alone.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Noradrenaline: α₁ vasoconstriction + β₁ inotropy; reflex bradycardia.
- First-line vasopressor in septic shock via central IV infusion.
- Extravasation → necrosis → phentolamine.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare adrenaline, noradrenaline and isoprenaline.</summary>

**Answer:** Adrenaline (α + β) raises SBP and HR with variable DBP; noradrenaline (α ≫ β₁) raises SBP, DBP and SVR with reflex bradycardia; isoprenaline (β₁ + β₂) raises HR and CO but lowers DBP and SVR.

**Why:** Slide "Three sympathomimetics compared".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does noradrenaline cause reflex bradycardia?</summary>

**Answer:** Strong vasoconstriction raises BP, activating the baroreceptor reflex and vagal activity, which masks its direct β₁ chronotropic effect.

**Why:** Slide "Noradrenaline: Receptors and Actions".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the precautions in giving noradrenaline?</summary>

**Answer:** Central line infusion with BP/ECG monitoring; watch for digital ischemia, arrhythmias and extravasation necrosis (stop infusion, treat, e.g. phentolamine); monitor perfusion, not BP alone.

**Why:** Slide "Noradrenaline: Clinical uses and adverse effects".
</details>

## 6. Dopamine

- **Dose-dependent receptors:** **low dose: D₁/D₂ → renal and mesenteric vasodilation; moderate dose: β₁ → ↑ HR and contractility; high dose: α₁ → vasoconstriction and ↑ BP.** It is the **precursor of noradrenaline.**
- **Actions:** positive inotropy and chronotropy; **↑ kidney/visceral flow at low dose**; vasoconstriction dominates at high dose.
- **Uses:** **cardiogenic shock; septic shock with low cardiac output; severe hypotension; severe heart failure; resistant bradycardia.**
- **Adverse effects:** **tachycardia, arrhythmias, hypertension, nausea**, excess sympathetic stimulation at high dose.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Low = D₁/D₂ (kidney); moderate = β₁ (heart); high = α₁ (vessels).
- Clinical effect depends on infusion dose.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the dose-dependent effects of dopamine.</summary>

**Answer:** Low dose: D₁/D₂ renal and mesenteric vasodilation; moderate dose: β₁ increases heart rate and contractility; high dose: α₁ vasoconstriction and higher BP.

**Why:** Slide "3. Dopamine".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the therapeutic uses of dopamine.</summary>

**Answer:** Cardiogenic shock, septic shock with low cardiac output, severe hypotension, severe heart failure and resistant bradycardia.

**Why:** Slide "Therapeutic uses".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the adverse effects of dopamine.</summary>

**Answer:** Tachycardia, arrhythmias, hypertension, nausea and excess sympathetic stimulation at high doses.

**Why:** Slide "Adverse effects".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Tyrosine hydroxylase is rate-limiting; metirosine blocks it; reserpine blocks VMAT.**

- **Catecholamines are NOT given orally; noncatecholamines are.**
- **Phenylephrine direct; amphetamine indirect; ephedrine mixed.**
- **Noradrenaline: reflex bradycardia; isoprenaline lowers DBP.**
- **Nonselective β blockers + adrenaline → marked pressor response.**
- **Dopamine: low = renal, moderate = β₁, high = α₁.**
