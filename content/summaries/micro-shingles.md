## 1. Infectious diseases affecting the PNS

| Pathogen | Disease / effect |
|---|---|
| **Mycobacterium leprae** | **Leprosy** — chronic infection, peripheral neuropathy |
| **Poliovirus** | **Poliomyelitis** — acute **flaccid paralysis** |
| **Corynebacterium diphtheriae** (toxin) | **Diphtheritic neuropathy** |
| **Borrelia burgdorferi** | **Lyme disease** — radiculopathy, cranial neuropathy |
| **HIV**, **COVID-19** | Neuropathy |
| **Varicella-Zoster Virus** | **Shingles** |
| **Treponema pallidum** | **Neurosyphilis** (tabes dorsalis) |
| **Rabies virus** | Axonal spread to the CNS |
| **Arboviruses (e.g. West Nile)** | Polyradiculitis |
| **Cryptococcus, Candida, Aspergillus** | Radiculopathy in the immunocompromised |

### How viruses reach the nervous system
- **Retrograde axonal transport** from the PNS to the CNS: **HSV, VZV** (alpha-herpesviruses).
- Along the axon from the **neuromuscular junction** to the cell body: **rabies virus, poliovirus**.
- Via the **olfactory epithelium and olfactory nerve** to the olfactory bulb (HSV, influenza A…).
- Across the **blood–brain barrier** inside infected leukocytes ("Trojan horse": HIV, HTLV-1) or by infecting **brain microvascular endothelial cells**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Leprosy = chronic neuropathy; polio = flaccid paralysis; diphtheria = toxin; Lyme = Borrelia; VZV = shingles.
- HSV/VZV: retrograde axonal transport; rabies/polio: from the NMJ; HIV: inside leukocytes.

</div>

## 2. Shingles (herpes zoster)

- Caused by **reactivation of Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV)**.
- After **primary infection (chickenpox)** the virus stays **latent in the dorsal root ganglia** (and cranial nerve ganglia).
- Reactivation → a **painful vesicular rash along a single dermatome**.
- Common in the **elderly and immunocompromised**.
- Major complications: ⭐ **post-herpetic neuralgia**, **ophthalmic zoster**, **CNS involvement**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Chickenpox first, shingles later (reactivation).
- Latency: dorsal root and cranial nerve ganglia.
- Dermatomal, unilateral, painful vesicular rash.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is shingles and what causes it?</summary>

**Answer:** Reactivation of latent Varicella-Zoster Virus that has stayed in the dorsal root ganglia since chickenpox, producing a painful vesicular rash along a dermatome.

**Why:** Core definition.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Who is most at risk?</summary>

**Answer:** The elderly and immunocompromised patients (aging, HIV infection, immunosuppressive drugs such as corticosteroids or biologics, stress).

**Why:** Reactivation follows weakened cell-mediated immunity.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the major complications of shingles.</summary>

**Answer:** Post-herpetic neuralgia, ophthalmic zoster (vision loss) and CNS involvement (meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis).

**Why:** Always asked together.
</details>

## 3. Microbiology of VZV

| Feature | VZV |
|---|---|
| **Family / subfamily / genus** | **Herpesviridae / Alphaherpesvirinae / Varicellovirus** (HHV-3) |
| **Genome** | **Linear double-stranded DNA, ~125 kb** |
| **Structure** | **Icosahedral nucleocapsid**, **tegument** proteins, **envelope** with **glycoprotein spikes** |
| **Replication** | In the **nucleus** |
| **Latency** | **Dorsal root and cranial nerve ganglia** |
| **Reactivation** | → **herpes zoster (shingles)** |

- **Replication cycle:** attachment and fusion → uncoating → viral DNA duplication in the nucleus → **immediate early (IE) → early (E) → late (L)** proteins → nucleocapsid assembly → budding through the nuclear membrane → envelopment in **Golgi/vesicles** → release.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Enveloped, icosahedral, dsDNA alpha-herpesvirus (HHV-3).
- Nuclear replication; IE → E → L gene expression.
- Latent in sensory ganglia.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify VZV and describe its structure.</summary>

**Answer:** Family Herpesviridae, subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae, genus Varicellovirus. Linear dsDNA (~125 kb) in an icosahedral nucleocapsid surrounded by tegument proteins and an envelope with glycoprotein spikes.

**Why:** Typical short-answer question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Where does VZV replicate and where does it become latent?</summary>

**Answer:** It replicates in the host cell nucleus and becomes latent in the dorsal root and cranial nerve ganglia.

**Why:** Replication site vs latency site.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the sequence of gene expression in lytic replication?</summary>

**Answer:** Immediate early (IE) proteins, then early (E) proteins, then late (L) structural proteins.

**Why:** Replication cascade of herpesviruses.
</details>

## 4. Pathogenesis of VZV

1. **Primary infection → chickenpox (varicella):** entry via the respiratory tract, replication in the **tonsils/Waldeyer's ring**, **viraemia**, infection of **T cells** that deliver the virus to the **skin** (maculopapular → vesiculopustular rash starting on the scalp/head and trunk; **more severe in adults — interstitial pneumonia**).
2. **Latency:** the virus travels by **retrograde axonal transport** to the **sensory dorsal root ganglia** (or **trigeminal ganglion**).
3. **Reactivation:** triggered by **immunosuppression, stress or aging**.
4. **Spread:** **anterograde** transport along the sensory nerve to the skin of **one dermatome**.
5. **Cytopathic effect:** **multinucleated giant cells** (polykaryocytes) and ⭐ **Cowdry type A intranuclear inclusions**.
6. **Clinical picture:** painful vesicular rash restricted to a dermatome.

### Mechanism of the symptoms
- **Prodromal pain** = **inflammation of the dorsal root ganglion** with **necrosis of sensory neurons**.
- **Vesicles** = **acantholysis** (loss of connections between epidermal cells).
- **Post-herpetic neuralgia** = **nociceptor sensitization, deafferentation and aberrant connections**; about **15% of elderly patients**.
- Reactivation in the **trigeminal ganglion** → **herpes zoster ophthalmicus** → **corneal scarring (keratitis)** with lasting visual deficit.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Chickenpox → latency → reactivation → shingles.
- Cowdry A inclusions + multinucleated giant cells.
- Prodromal pain = DRG inflammation; vesicles = acantholysis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the pathogenesis of shingles step by step.</summary>

**Answer:** Primary infection causes chickenpox; the virus travels along sensory axons to the dorsal root ganglia where it stays latent; immunosuppression, stress or aging trigger reactivation; the virus travels along the sensory nerve to the skin and causes a painful vesicular rash in the dermatome, with multinucleated giant cells and Cowdry type A inclusions.

**Why:** The six steps from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does the rash stay in one dermatome?</summary>

**Answer:** Reactivation happens in one sensory ganglion, so the virus spreads down the nerve of that single spinal or cranial nerve to its dermatome.

**Why:** Anatomical explanation of the pattern.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does post-herpetic neuralgia arise?</summary>

**Answer:** Damage and death of sensory neurons lead to peripheral and central nociceptor sensitization, deafferentation of central neurons and formation of aberrant connections, so pain persists after the rash heals.

**Why:** Mechanism in one sentence.
</details>

## 5. Clinical features of shingles

- **Prodrome:** **pain, burning or tingling** in the affected dermatome.
- **Rash:** **unilateral vesicular** eruption in a **dermatomal** distribution.
- **Systemic:** fever and malaise (sometimes).
- **Stages of the lesions:**

| Stage | Days | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| **Early** | 1–5 | Smaller rash, shallow bumps |
| **Mid** | 5–7 | Larger rash, fluid-filled bumps |
| **Late** | 7–10 | Blisters **crusted over** |

- **Locations:** **ribs and waist (most common)**, shoulders (common), side of the face (more serious), **eyes or mouth (emergency)**.
- **Complications:** **post-herpetic neuralgia**, **ophthalmic zoster → vision loss**, **meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis**, **Ramsay Hunt syndrome** (geniculate ganglion), cranial nerve palsies.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pain first, then unilateral dermatomal vesicles.
- Stages: 1–5 / 5–7 / 7–10 days (crusts).
- Face (eye/mouth) = urgent.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical features of shingles.</summary>

**Answer:** Prodromal pain, burning or tingling in a dermatome, then a unilateral vesicular rash in that dermatome, sometimes with fever and malaise. Complications include post-herpetic neuralgia, ophthalmic zoster and neurological disease.

**Why:** Main exam question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the stages of the lesions.</summary>

**Answer:** Early (days 1–5): small shallow bumps; mid (days 5–7): larger fluid-filled bumps; late (days 7–10): blisters crusted over.

**Why:** Fig. of the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which locations are most common and which are an emergency?</summary>

**Answer:** Most common: ribs and waist (also shoulders). Emergency: lesions near the eyes or mouth (ophthalmic zoster, risk to vision).

**Why:** Urgent referral for the eye.
</details>

## 6. Laboratory diagnosis of VZV

| Method | Role |
|---|---|
| **Clinical** | Typical rash distribution |
| **Tzanck smear** | **Multinucleated giant cells** — **low specificity** |
| ⭐ **PCR** | Detects **VZV DNA** — **gold standard / method of choice** (vesicle fluid, CSF, tissue, BAL, EDTA blood, amniotic fluid) |
| **Direct/indirect immunofluorescence** | Viral **antigens** — alternative method (cell-rich vesicle contents) |
| **Virus isolation (cell culture)** | Only when special investigations are required |
| **PCR/RFLP/sequencing** | Differentiate **wild-type from vaccine-derived** virus (specialized labs) |
| **Plaque reduction assay** | **Resistance to antiviral drugs** |
| **Serology** | **IgM = acute**, **IgG = immunity**; immune status and retrospective confirmation |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PCR = gold standard; Tzanck = giant cells but not specific.
- IgM acute / IgG immune.
- Culture, RFLP and plaque reduction are for special situations.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the gold standard for diagnosing VZV and which samples are used?</summary>

**Answer:** PCR for VZV DNA, on vesicle fluid, CSF, tissue, bronchoalveolar lavage, EDTA blood or amniotic fluid.

**Why:** Method of choice in the lecture's table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What does a Tzanck smear show and what is its limitation?</summary>

**Answer:** Multinucleated giant cells; it has low specificity because herpes simplex gives the same picture.

**Why:** Rapid but non-specific.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the role of serology?</summary>

**Answer:** VZV IgM indicates acute infection and IgG indicates immunity; antibody testing determines immune status and confirms varicella or zoster retrospectively.

**Why:** Not used for the acute diagnosis of a typical rash.
</details>

## 7. Prevention and treatment

- **Prevention:** **varicella vaccine** (childhood) and **zoster vaccine** for older adults and the immunocompromised — the **recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV)**.
- **Antivirals:** **acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir** — **early treatment reduces severity**.
- **Pain control:** **NSAIDs, opioids, gabapentin**.
- **Corticosteroids:** sometimes, to reduce inflammation.
- **Complications:** ophthalmic zoster → **urgent referral**.

### Clinical case (from the lecture)
A **65-year-old man** with **burning chest pain** followed by a **unilateral vesicular rash in the T5 dermatome**, with chickenpox in childhood → ⭐ **herpes zoster (shingles)**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Two vaccines: varicella (children), zoster/RZV (older adults).
- Antivirals early + pain control (gabapentin).
- Eye involvement = urgent referral.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is shingles treated?</summary>

**Answer:** Early antivirals (acyclovir, valacyclovir or famciclovir) plus pain control (NSAIDs, opioids, gabapentin); corticosteroids are sometimes added; complications such as ophthalmic zoster need urgent referral.

**Why:** Two arms: antiviral and analgesic.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which vaccines prevent chickenpox and shingles?</summary>

**Answer:** The varicella vaccine in childhood prevents chickenpox; the zoster vaccine (recombinant zoster vaccine, RZV) is given to older adults and the immunocompromised to prevent shingles.

**Why:** Two vaccines for two diseases.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. A 65-year-old has burning right chest pain followed by a vesicular rash along T5. Diagnosis?</summary>

**Answer:** Herpes zoster (shingles), reactivation of VZV from childhood chickenpox.

**Why:** Unilateral + dermatomal + prior chickenpox.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Shingles is a reactivation**, not a new infection; chickenpox is the primary infection.
- **PCR is the gold standard**; the **Tzanck smear** is non-specific.
- **IgM = acute, IgG = immunity.**
- **VZV is an enveloped dsDNA alpha-herpesvirus** that replicates in the **nucleus** — not an RNA virus.
- **Poliomyelitis = flaccid paralysis**, not spastic.
- **Eye or mouth shingles = emergency**; ribs and waist are the most common sites.
- Latency is in **sensory ganglia** (dorsal root, trigeminal), not in motor neurons.

8. **Treatment/prevention:** early acyclovir/valacyclovir/famciclovir + NSAIDs/opioids/gabapentin; varicella vaccine; RZV for older adults.
