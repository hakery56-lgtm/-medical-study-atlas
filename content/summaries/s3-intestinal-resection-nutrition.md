## 1. Intestinal physiology and effects of resection

- ⭐ **Up to 50%** of the small bowel can be removed or bypassed without permanent harm; with **< 150 cm remaining** → **short bowel syndrome.**
- **Motility:** **3× slower in the ileum** than the jejunum; ileocaecal valve may slow transit. Small bowel handles **5–6 L endogenous + 2–3 L exogenous** fluid per day; most is reabsorbed. **Ileum = critical for fluid and electrolyte conservation.**
- **Table 17.5 (as printed):** duodenum 9000 mL/24 h; jejunum 5000 mL, water absorption **44%**, sodium **13%**; ileum 1500 mL, **70%** and **72%**; colon 100 mL, **93%** and **99%**.
- **Ileum:** the **only site of vitamin B₁₂ and bile salt absorption**; bile salts needed for **fat and fat-soluble vitamin** absorption; **enterohepatic circulation** maintains the pool; after ileal resection bile salt loss ↑ and synthesis does not compensate → **fat malabsorption**; bile salts in the colon ↓ salt and water absorption → ↑ stool losses.
- **Colon:** transit **24–150 h**; **> 90%** water and salt absorption; ferments carbohydrate to **short-chain fatty acids** (↑ water/salt absorption; **trophic to colonocytes**).
- **Effects of resection:** **proximal jejunum** → no significant fluid/electrolyte change (ileum and colon adapt); **ileum** → ↑ gastric motility, **faster transit**, colon overloaded with fluid + bile salts → **diarrhoea.** **100 cm ileum** → **steatorrhoea** (oral **cholestyramine**); **> 100 cm** → **fat restriction + regular parenteral B₁₂.**
- **Complications of short bowel syndrome:** **peptic ulceration** (gastric hypersecretion), **cholelithiasis** (interrupted enterohepatic cycle), **hyperoxaluria** → renal stones.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Short bowel syndrome: < 150 cm remaining; up to 50% can be removed safely.
- Ileum: B12 and bile salts; ileal resection → steatorrhoea, diarrhoea, B12 injections.
- Complications: peptic ulcer, gallstones, oxalate renal stones.

</div>

## 2. Enteral nutrition

- ⭐ **Indication:** **5 days of inadequate intake** (or anticipated); earlier if pre-existing malnutrition. **Enteral = nutrients into the GI tract; use the gut whenever possible** (food, **sip feeds**, tubes into stomach, duodenum or jejunum).
- **Formulations:** differ in energy, osmolarity, fat, nitrogen and complexity; up to **1–2 kcal/mL** and **0.6 g/mL protein**; newer: **glutamine, fibre, immunonutrients (arginine, fish oils).** **Sip feed:** **200 kcal + 2 g nitrogen / 200 mL.**
- **Techniques:** **nasogastric; fine-bore stomach tubes; surgical or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG); nasojejunal (post-pyloric); jejunostomy.** A **dietician** supervises.
- **Regimen:** **20–30 mL/h** → goal in **48–72 h**; **stop 4–5 h overnight** (gastric pH recovery). Beyond **a week** use a **fine-bore tube** (polyurethane or silicone, **< 3 mm**) → fewer erosions. **Insertion:** semi-recumbent, lubricated wire, nose → stomach, remove wire, tape, **confirm by abdominal X-ray.**
- **Gastrostomy/PEG:** tube through the abdominal wall into the stomach, endoscopic with local anaesthesia; preferred for **4–6 weeks or more**. **PEG complications:** **necrotising fasciitis, abdominal wall abscess, peri-PEG sepsis, persistent gastric fistula.**
- **Jejunostomy:** ↑ popular, **↓ aspiration and better tolerance**; **nasojejunal** (fluoroscopy or endoscopy) or **surgical** (needle jejunostomy at laparotomy).
- **Complications:** **tube-related** (malposition, displacement, blockage, leakage, erosion), **GI** (diarrhoea, bloating, nausea, vomiting, cramps, aspiration, constipation), **metabolic** (electrolytes, vitamin/mineral/trace element deficiency, drug interactions), **infective** (exogenous handling, endogenous).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Start nutritional support after 5 days of inadequate intake; use the gut if it works.
- 20–30 mL/h to goal in 48–72 h; 4–5 h overnight break; fine-bore for > 1 week.
- PEG for 4–6 weeks; complications: necrotising fasciitis, abscess, sepsis, fistula.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. When is nutritional support indicated?</summary>

**Answer:** In any patient with 5 days of inadequate intake or anticipated inadequate intake; earlier if there is pre-existing malnutrition.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe tube feeding technique and routine.</summary>

**Answer:** Start 20–30 mL/h and reach goal in 48–72 hours, stop 4–5 hours overnight for gastric pH; use a fine-bore tube (< 3 mm, polyurethane or silicone) if over a week; insert with a wire in a semi-recumbent patient and confirm by abdominal X-ray.

**Why:** Slides 20–22.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the complications of enteral nutrition and of PEG.</summary>

**Answer:** Enteral: tube-related, gastrointestinal, metabolic and infective. PEG: necrotising fasciitis, abdominal wall abscess, sepsis around the site and persistent gastric fistula.

**Why:** Slides 25 and 28–29.
</details>

## 3. Parenteral nutrition

- ⭐ **TPN** = **all** nutritional requirements **IV, without the GI tract**; indicated when energy and protein needs **cannot be met enterally**: **massive small bowel resection, intestinal fistulae, prolonged intestinal failure.**
- **Route:** **central vein** or **peripheral line.** Mixed bags (glucose, fat, amino acids; **isotonic non-toxic fat emulsions**); energy = **glucose + fat**, rarely > **2000 kcal/day (25–30 kcal/kg/day).**
- **Peripheral:** **≤ 2 weeks**; avoids central complications but limited by **thrombophlebitis.**
- **Central:** **subclavian, internal or external jugular**; **chest X-ray before use** (exclude pneumothorax, tip in **distal SVC** to reduce thrombosis).

### Complications of parenteral nutrition
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Nutrient deficiency** | Hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypophosphataemia, hypomagnesaemia, essential fatty acid, zinc, mineral and trace element deficiencies |
| **Excess glucose** | Hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolar dehydration, **hepatic steatosis**, hypercapnia, ↑ sympathetic activity, fluid retention, electrolyte abnormalities |
| **Excess fat** | Hypercholesterolaemia, **lipoprotein X**, hypertriglyceridaemia, hypersensitivity |
| **Excess amino acids** | **Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis**, hypercalcaemia, aminoacidaemia, **uraemia** |
| **Sepsis** | Catheter-related sepsis, predisposition to systemic sepsis |
| **Line insertion** | Pneumothorax, arterial damage, air embolism, thoracic duct damage, cardiac perforation/tamponade, pleural effusion, hydromediastinum |
| **Long-term line** | Occlusion, venous thrombosis |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- TPN indications: massive resection, fistulae, prolonged intestinal failure.
- Peripheral ≤ 2 weeks (thrombophlebitis); central: CXR before use.
- Complications by group: deficiency, glucose/fat/amino acid excess, sepsis, line.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define TPN and give its indications.</summary>

**Answer:** Provision of all nutritional requirements intravenously without the GI tract; indicated for massive small bowel resection, intestinal fistulae and prolonged intestinal failure when enteral feeding cannot meet needs.

**Why:** Slide 31.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare peripheral and central parenteral nutrition.</summary>

**Answer:** Peripheral: up to 2 weeks, avoids central complications but limited by thrombophlebitis. Central: via subclavian or jugular veins with a post-insertion chest X-ray to exclude pneumothorax and confirm the tip in the distal SVC.

**Why:** Slides 34–35.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Classify the complications of parenteral nutrition.</summary>

**Answer:** Nutrient deficiency; over-feeding with glucose, fat or amino acids; sepsis; and line-related complications (on insertion and long-term).

**Why:** Slides 37–40.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Short bowel syndrome = < 150 cm remaining; up to 50% resection is tolerated.**
- **Ileum = ONLY site of B12 and bile salt absorption; jejunal resection causes little problem.**
- **PEG = 4–6 weeks or more; fine-bore tube = more than a week.**
- **Peripheral PN ≤ 2 weeks (thrombophlebitis); central: CXR before feeding.**
- **Excess glucose → steatosis and hypercapnia; excess fat → lipoprotein X; excess amino acids → hyperchloraemic acidosis and uraemia.**
- **Nutritional support after 5 days of inadequate intake.**
