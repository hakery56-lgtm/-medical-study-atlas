## 1. Glycogenesis and glycogenolysis

- ⭐ **Glycogenesis** = glycogen from glucose; needs **ATP and UTP**; in **muscle and liver.** **Glycogenolysis** = glycogen → **glucose-6-phosphate (muscle)** or **glucose (liver).**
- **Glycogenesis steps:** glucose → **glucose-6-phosphate** (hexokinase) → **glucose-1-phosphate** (**phosphoglucomutase**) → **UDP-glucose** (**UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase**, **UTP → PPi**) → **glycogen synthase** adds glucose to the **glycogenin primer** (**α-1,4 linkages**) → **branching enzyme** moves **6 residues** to make an **α-1,6 branch.**
- **Glycogenolysis steps:** **glycogen phosphorylase** releases **glucose-1-phosphate** sequentially and **stops near the branch**; **transferase** moves a **trisaccharide** to another branch; **debranching enzyme** hydrolyses the **α-1,6 bond** (free glucose); phosphorylase continues. **Glucose-1-P → glucose-6-P** (phosphoglucomutase); **liver: glucose-6-phosphatase → free glucose.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Synthase = α-1,4; branching enzyme = α-1,6 (6 residues).
- Phosphorylase → glucose-1-P; debranching enzyme frees glucose.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Outline glycogen synthesis from glucose.</summary>

**Answer:** Glucose → glucose-6-P → glucose-1-P → UDP-glucose (UTP, PPi released) → glycogen synthase extends the glycogenin primer with α-1,4 links; the branching enzyme transfers 6 residues to form α-1,6 branches.

**Why:** Slides 3-5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Outline glycogen breakdown.</summary>

**Answer:** Phosphorylase removes glucose-1-P units until near a branch; the transferase moves a trisaccharide, the debranching enzyme cleaves the α-1,6 bond releasing glucose, and phosphorylase continues.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the fate of glucose-1-phosphate from glycogen?</summary>

**Answer:** Converted to glucose-6-phosphate by phosphoglucomutase; in the liver glucose-6-phosphatase releases free glucose.

**Why:** Slides 3 and 8.
</details>

## 2. Significance in liver and muscle

- **Liver:** after a meal excess glucose is stored (**glycogenesis**); **between meals** **glycogenolysis** keeps **blood glucose normal.**
- **Muscle:** glycogen is a **local fuel for contraction**; muscle **cannot release glucose into blood** because it **lacks glucose-6-phosphatase**; it is **used exclusively by muscle.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Liver glycogen maintains blood glucose.
- Muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the significance of liver glycogen?</summary>

**Answer:** It stores glucose after meals and releases it between meals to keep blood glucose within the normal range.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why can muscle glycogen not raise blood glucose?</summary>

**Answer:** Muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, so glucose-6-phosphate cannot be converted to free glucose; it is used only within muscle.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is muscle glycogen important?</summary>

**Answer:** It is a readily available glucose source inside muscle during contraction.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

## 3. Regulation

- **Key enzymes:** **glycogen phosphorylase** (breakdown) and **glycogen synthase** (synthesis).
- **Allosteric:** **ATP, glucose-6-phosphate, AMP.** **Hormonal:** phosphorylase is **activated by glucagon and epinephrine** (**hypoglycemia**); synthase is **decreased by glucagon and epinephrine** and **enhanced by insulin** (**hyperglycemia**).

| Enzyme | Active form | Inactive form |
|---|---|---|
| **Glycogen synthase** | **a** (dephosphorylated) | **b** (phosphorylated) |
| **Glycogen phosphorylase** | **a** (phosphorylated) | **b** (dephosphorylated) |

- **Cascade:** **glucagon (liver) / epinephrine (muscle, liver)** → **ATP → cAMP** → **protein kinase A** → **phosphorylase kinase (active)** → **phosphorylase b → a** → **glycogenolysis**; the same kinase **phosphorylates synthase a → b**, **inhibiting glycogenesis.** **Insulin** (protein phosphatase) **activates synthase and inhibits phosphorylase.**
- **Review questions on slide 15 (standard teaching):** glucose from glycogen gives **one more ATP** in glycolysis (no initial phosphorylation); glucose-6-P enters **glycogenesis when well-fed**, **glycolysis when energy is needed**, **hydrolysis to free glucose in liver when blood glucose is low.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Phosphorylation activates phosphorylase and inactivates synthase.
- Insulin favours glycogen synthesis; glucagon/epinephrine favour breakdown.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is glycogen synthase regulated?</summary>

**Answer:** Active dephosphorylated form a; glucagon and epinephrine phosphorylate it to inactive b; insulin opposes this and enhances synthesis in hyperglycemia.

**Why:** Slides 9-10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is glycogen phosphorylase regulated?</summary>

**Answer:** Phosphorylase a (phosphorylated) is active; glucagon and epinephrine phosphorylate it through cAMP, protein kinase A and phosphorylase kinase in hypoglycemia.

**Why:** Slides 9, 12-13.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the hormonal cascade of glycogenolysis.</summary>

**Answer:** Hormone → cAMP → active protein kinase → active phosphorylase kinase → phosphorylase a → glycogenolysis.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

## 4. Glycogen storage diseases

| Type | Name | Enzyme | Organ | Main features |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | **Von Gierke's** | **Glucose-6-phosphatase** | Liver, kidney | **Hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, hyperlipidemia, ketosis, hyperuricemia** |
| **II** | **Pompe's** | **Lysosomal α-1,4-glucosidase (acid maltase)** | All organs with lysosomes | **Infantile, cardiac failure, early death** |
| **III** | **Cori's / Forbe's (limit dextrinosis)** | **Debranching enzyme** | Liver, skeletal muscle, heart | **Short outer chains, hypoglycemia** |
| **IV** | **Andersen's (amylopectinosis)** | **Branching enzyme** | Liver | **Few branches, early death (cardiac/liver failure)** |
| **V** | **McArdle** | **Muscle phosphorylase** | Skeletal muscle | **Exercise pain, cramps, no lactate rise** |
| **VI** | **Hers'** | **Liver phosphorylase** | Liver | **High liver glycogen, mild hypoglycemia, ketosis** |
| **VII** | **Tarui** | **Phosphofructokinase** | Muscle, RBC | **As V + hemolytic anemia** |

- **Von Gierke's:** **most common**; fasting hypoglycemia (glucose cannot leave the liver); **glucose-6-P → HMP shunt → ribose and purines → uric acid (hyperuricemia)**; **glycogen deposits in the liver**, massive enlargement may cause **cirrhosis.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Type I = glucose-6-phosphatase; II = acid maltase; III = debranching; IV = branching; V = muscle phosphorylase; VI = liver phosphorylase; VII = PFK.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe Von Gierke's disease.</summary>

**Answer:** Glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency: fasting hypoglycemia, hyperlipidemia, lactic acidosis, ketosis, hyperuricemia and a massively enlarged glycogen-laden liver that may become cirrhotic.

**Why:** Slides 16-17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why does hyperuricemia develop in type I?</summary>

**Answer:** Accumulated glucose-6-phosphate enters the HMP shunt, giving more ribose and purine nucleotides that are catabolized to uric acid.

**Why:** Slide 17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare McArdle's disease and Tarui disease.</summary>

**Answer:** Both cause exercise-induced muscle pain, cramps and low lactate rise; McArdle lacks muscle phosphorylase, Tarui lacks phosphofructokinase and also shows hemolytic anemia.

**Why:** Slide 18 table.
</details>
