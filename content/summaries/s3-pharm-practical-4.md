## 1. The clinical problem: angina and nitrates

- **Case:** **55-year-old heavy-smoker male**, **retrosternal chest pain** radiating to the **left arm, neck or jaw**, brought on by **exertion or emotional stress**, **relieved within minutes by rest** = **angina pectoris**. **GTN sublingual** for the attack; **GTN transdermal** for prophylaxis; **patch removed at night for 8 hours**.
- ⭐ **Angina:** chest pain from **myocardial ischemia** (reduced O₂ supply, commonly reduced coronary flow). **Risk factors:** **hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, age, male sex, family history**.
- **Nitrates (GTN):** release **nitric oxide → guanylyl cyclase → ↑ cGMP → smooth muscle relaxation** → **venodilation → ↓ preload → ↓ myocardial O₂ demand**; also **↓ afterload** (BP). Use for **treatment and prevention** (**5–10 min before** effort).
- **Other indications:** **acute heart failure (pulmonary edema), hypertensive emergencies, esophageal spasm, topical for anal fissure**. **Side effects:** **dizziness, hypotension, syncope, reflex tachycardia, vascular headache**. **Other antianginals:** **β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, antiplatelets (aspirin), statins**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Nitrates: NO → cGMP → venodilation → ↓ preload.
- Take GTN 5–10 min before predictable triggers; side effects = hypotension, reflex tachycardia, headache.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is angina and what are its risk factors?</summary>

**Answer:** Chest pain or discomfort from myocardial ischemia due to reduced blood flow to the heart muscle; risk factors are hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, age, male sex and family history of coronary disease.

**Why:** Clinical relevance answers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the mechanism of action of nitrates.</summary>

**Answer:** They release nitric oxide, activating guanylyl cyclase and raising cGMP, which relaxes smooth muscle; venodilation lowers preload and myocardial oxygen demand, and they also reduce afterload.

**Why:** Clinical relevance answers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List other indications and side effects of GTN.</summary>

**Answer:** Indications: acute heart failure (pulmonary edema), hypertensive emergencies, esophageal spasm and topically anal fissure. Side effects: dizziness, hypotension, syncope, reflex tachycardia and vascular headache.

**Why:** Clinical relevance answers.
</details>

## 2. Why GTN is not given orally; first-pass metabolism

- **GTN:** **extensive, nearly complete FPM** (**> 90%** deactivated) → **oral dose ineffective**; **sublingual, IV, transdermal bypass the liver**.
- **Isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN):** FPM **less extensive**; parent drug and **active metabolites (isosorbide mononitrates)** reach the circulation → **oral long-term prophylaxis**.
- ⭐ **First-pass metabolism:** pre-systemic elimination of an **oral** drug in the **liver and/or gut wall** (**extensive = > 50%** extracted). **Significance:** ↓ **bioavailability**; **oral dose much higher than IV**; determines **route**.
- **Examples:** **GTN, propranolol (and metoprolol, not atenolol), morphine, lidocaine, estradiol**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- GTN > 90% first-pass; ISDN less, so oral.
- FPM = ↓ bioavailability; propranolol, morphine, lidocaine, estradiol, GTN.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is GTN not given orally but ISDN can be?</summary>

**Answer:** GTN undergoes almost complete first-pass hepatic metabolism (over 90% inactivated) so oral doses are ineffective, while sublingual, IV and transdermal routes bypass the liver; ISDN undergoes less extensive first-pass metabolism and its active mononitrate metabolites still produce an effect orally.

**Why:** Q1 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define first-pass metabolism and its significance.</summary>

**Answer:** Metabolism of an orally given drug in the liver and/or gut wall during its first passage before it reaches the systemic circulation (extensive means more than 50% extracted); it reduces bioavailability, necessitates higher oral than IV doses and determines the route of administration.

**Why:** Q2 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give examples of drugs with extensive FPM.</summary>

**Answer:** Nitroglycerin, propranolol (and metoprolol but not atenolol), morphine, lidocaine and estradiol.

**Why:** Q2 answer.
</details>

## 3. Bypassing FPM and liver impairment

- **Bypass routes:** **IV, IM, SC, sublingual/buccal, transdermal, inhalation**, and **partly rectal**.
- **Rectal:** **lower rectum → systemic** (bypass); **upper rectum → portal** (FPM) → **partial, unpredictable**.
- **Liver impairment (cirrhosis, hepatitis):** **↓ FPM capacity** → more oral drug reaches the circulation → **↑ bioavailability** → **toxic concentrations** with the usual dose.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Rectal = lower systemic, upper portal.
- Liver disease → ↑ bioavailability of high-FPM drugs.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How can FPM be bypassed?</summary>

**Answer:** By routes that avoid the portal venous system: IV, IM, SC, sublingual/buccal, transdermal and inhalation, and partially the rectal route.

**Why:** Q3 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain rectal absorption with respect to FPM.</summary>

**Answer:** Drug in the lower rectum is absorbed to the systemic circulation (bypassing FPM) while drug in the upper rectum goes to the portal circulation (subject to FPM), so the bypass is only partial and unpredictable.

**Why:** Q3 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What happens to high-FPM drugs in liver impairment?</summary>

**Answer:** First-pass capacity falls, so a larger proportion of the oral dose escapes extraction; bioavailability rises greatly and the standard oral dose can cause unexpectedly high, potentially toxic concentrations.

**Why:** Q4 answer.
</details>

## 4. Hepatic metabolism, induction and inhibition

| Phase | Reactions | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase I** | **Oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis** (**CYP450**) | Active → inactive, active → active metabolite, or **prodrug → active** |
| **Phase II** | **Conjugation** (glucuronic acid, sulfate, acetate, glycine) | **More water soluble**, excreted in bile/urine; usually **inactive** |

- **Inducible:** **both**, but **phase I (microsomal CYP450)** is the most clinically significant.
- **Alcohol:** **acute** = **inhibitor**; **chronic** = **potent CYP2E1 inducer** → ↑ metabolism of **warfarin, phenytoin** (↓ effect) and ↑ toxic metabolites of **paracetamol** (liver damage).

| Inducers (↑ metabolism, ↓ effect) | Inhibitors (↓ metabolism, ↑ effect/toxicity) |
|---|---|
| **Rifampin** (very potent), **phenobarbital, carbamazepine, phenytoin**, **chronic alcohol**, **smoking** | **Ketoconazole, itraconazole** (azoles), **erythromycin, clarithromycin, cimetidine, grapefruit juice (CYP3A4 in gut wall)**, **acute alcohol, ciprofloxacin** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Phase I = CYP450 (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis); phase II = conjugation.
- Chronic alcohol and smoking induce; azoles, macrolides, cimetidine and grapefruit inhibit.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the two phases of hepatic drug metabolism.</summary>

**Answer:** Phase I: oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis, mainly by CYP450, converting active to inactive, active to active metabolite or prodrug to active drug. Phase II: conjugation with glucuronic acid, sulfate, acetate or glycine, increasing water solubility for biliary or urinary excretion, usually abolishing activity.

**Why:** Q5 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does alcohol affect hepatic metabolism?</summary>

**Answer:** Acute drinking inhibits metabolism; chronic drinking is a potent inducer of CYP2E1, raising metabolism of warfarin and phenytoin (reducing effectiveness) and increasing toxic paracetamol metabolites (liver damage).

**Why:** Q5 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give examples of enzyme inducers and inhibitors.</summary>

**Answer:** Inducers: rifampin, phenobarbital, carbamazepine, phenytoin, chronic alcohol and smoking. Inhibitors: ketoconazole, itraconazole, erythromycin, clarithromycin, cimetidine, grapefruit juice, acute alcohol and ciprofloxacin.

**Why:** Q5 table.
</details>

## 5. Nitrate tolerance and the patient guide

- **Nitrate tolerance (tachyphylaxis)** with **continuous** nitrate exposure → ↓ antianginal effect; **remove the patch for 8–12 h daily** (often at night) = **nitrate-free interval / drug holiday**.
- **GTN tablet steps:** (1) **sit down** (dizziness, BP drop); (2) tablet **under the tongue**, **dissolve** completely, **do not chew, crush or swallow**; (3) **no rinsing, drinking or smoking for 5–10 min**; (4) **wait 5 min** → **second dose** if pain persists → wait 5 min → **still pain = emergency, call for help** (possible heart attack).
- **Storage:** **dark glass original bottle, tightly closed** (light, air, moisture); spray more stable but keep from heat.
- ⚠️ **Never take sildenafil (Viagra) with GTN** → **sudden severe life-threatening hypotension**. **Headaches may decrease** with continued use.
- **Formulations:** **tablets or spray** under the tongue (**400, 500, 600 µg/dose**); **patches** (**5–10 mg/24 h**); **IV** injection/infusion (ampoule **50 µg/10 ml**, vial **50 mg/50 ml**); **ointment 0.2%**.
- **Summative assessment names:** **glyceryl trinitrate (nitroglycerin)** and **isosorbide dinitrate**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Patch off 8–12 h to prevent tolerance.
- Sit, tablet under tongue, repeat once after 5 min, then call for help; never with sildenafil.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the transdermal GTN patch removed at night?</summary>

**Answer:** Continuous nitrate exposure causes rapid tolerance (tachyphylaxis); a daily 8-12 hour nitrate-free interval (drug holiday), often at night when angina is less likely, restores responsiveness.

**Why:** Q6 answer.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What would you tell the patient about taking sublingual GTN?</summary>

**Answer:** Sit down, place one tablet under the tongue and let it dissolve (do not chew, crush or swallow), avoid rinsing, drinking and smoking for 5-10 minutes, wait 5 minutes and take a second dose if pain persists; if still not relieved after another 5 minutes call for medical help immediately; store tablets in the original dark closed bottle.

**Why:** Patient's guide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the key drug interaction warning with GTN?</summary>

**Answer:** Never use erectile dysfunction drugs such as sildenafil with GTN because of a sudden, severe, life-threatening fall in blood pressure.

**Why:** Patient's guide.
</details>
