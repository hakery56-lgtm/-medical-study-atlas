## 1. Memory and its three processes

- ⭐ **Memory** = the **ability to retain information over time** through **three processes: encoding (forming), storing and retrieving**.
- Memories are **not exact copies** but **representations of the world** that **vary in accuracy and are subject to error and bias**.

| Process | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **1. Encoding** | **making mental representations of information so it can be placed into memory** |
| **2. Storing** | **placing encoded information into relatively permanent storage for later recall**; **easier when associated with old/familiar information** |
| **3. Retrieving** | **getting or recalling information from short-term or long-term storage** |

- **Three types of memory:** **sensory → short-term (working) → long-term.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Memory = retain information over time via encoding, storing, retrieving.
- Memories are representations, not copies (error and bias).
- Association with known information makes retrieval easier.

</div>

## 2. Sensory memory

- **First stage of memory:** receives information from the environment and holds it **from an instant to a few seconds**; **no attention → disappears; attention → moves to short-term memory**.
- The brain's **"mental video-audio recorder"** holds input for **seconds or less**, long enough to decide if it is **important or interesting**.

| | **Iconic memory (visual)** | **Echoic memory (auditory)** |
|---|---|---|
| Information | **visual (images)** — "iconic" = image | **auditory (sounds)** |
| Duration | **≈ 0.25 s or slightly longer** | **≈ 1–2 s** |
| Example | **blinking: no darkness is perceived** | **recalling a friend's exact words after "What did you say?"** |
| Function | maintains visual information briefly | **"plays back" sounds; recognizes that sequences of sounds form words**; **lengthens from childhood to adulthood** |

- **Sperling's experiment:** **12 letters in 3 rows for 50 ms** → **immediate recall ≈ 9 letters; after 0.5 s ≈ 6; after 1 s ≈ 4** (iconic memory fades within about a second).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Sensory memory lasts an instant to a few seconds; attention moves information to short-term memory.
- Iconic ≈ 0.25 s (visual); echoic ≈ 1–2 s (auditory).
- Sperling: 12 letters, 50 ms → 9 → 6 → 4 letters with 0, 0.5, 1 s delay.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe sensory memory.</summary>

**Answer:** The first stage of memory; it receives information from the environment and keeps it for an instant to a few seconds, acting as a mental recorder that gives time to decide whether the information is important; unattended information disappears while attended information moves to short-term memory.

**Why:** Slides 7–8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare iconic and echoic memory.</summary>

**Answer:** Iconic memory holds visual information for about 0.25 s (e.g. during a blink); echoic memory holds auditory information for about 1–2 s (e.g. recalling a friend's last words) and lengthens from childhood to adulthood.

**Why:** Slide 9 table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe Sperling's experiment.</summary>

**Answer:** Participants saw 12 letters in 3 rows for 50 ms; they recalled about 9 letters immediately, about 6 after a 0.5-second delay and about 4 after 1 second, showing rapid fading of iconic memory.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

## 3. Short-term (working) memory

- **Attention** transfers information from sensory to short-term memory.
- **Capacity ≈ 7 items; duration 2–30 s**; **two characteristics: limited duration + limited capacity**.
- **Maintenance rehearsal** = intentionally repeating information to keep it longer.
- **Interference** = new information **overwrites or pushes out** old information; **few errors with 7–8 digits, some with 8–9, many with > 9 digits.**
- **Three steps (infographic):** **attending → rehearsing → storing (to long-term memory)**; **brain area: prefrontal cortex**.
- **Why "working" memory?** it is an **active process** — it **stores and works with (processes, manipulates) information** and helps in **decision making**.
- **Chunking** increases capacity: **1 9 8 7 2 0 2 4 (8 items) → 1987 – 2024 (2 chunks)**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ≈ 7 items, 2–30 s; maintenance rehearsal prolongs it; interference limits it.
- Attend → rehearse → store; prefrontal cortex.
- Working = active manipulation; chunking increases capacity.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the capacity and duration of short-term memory.</summary>

**Answer:** It holds about 7 items for 2–30 seconds, so it has limited capacity and duration; maintenance rehearsal prolongs retention, while interference from new information overwrites old information.

**Why:** Slides 11–12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is short-term memory called working memory?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it is an active process: it does not only store information temporarily but also processes and manipulates it and helps in decision making.

**Why:** Infographic.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is chunking? Give an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Grouping information into meaningful units to increase capacity, e.g. 19872024 (8 separate digits) becomes 1987–2024 (2 chunks).

**Why:** Infographic.
</details>

## 4. Long-term memory

- **Almost unlimited capacity and duration**; information moves from short-term memory by **rehearsal and processing**.
- **Two types:** **declarative (explicit)** and **procedural (nondeclarative)**.

| | **Declarative** | **Procedural** |
|---|---|---|
| Content | **facts and events** | **skills and learned behaviors** (and some emotional responses learned by conditioning) |
| Awareness | **conscious; can be recalled and described** | **mostly unconscious; difficult to recall or describe** |
| Knowing | **what** | **how** |
| Examples | **definition of depression, personal events** | **riding a bicycle, playing tennis, driving, typing, reading, playing an instrument; fear of spiders** |

- **Declarative subtypes:** **semantic** = **facts, knowledge, words, concepts, definitions, rules** (most class learning; penguin cannot fly, meaning of a medical term); **episodic** = **personal experiences and events — when, where, what** (breakfast, first day at medical college, a trip, a conversation).
- **H.M.:** after brain surgery he **could not form new declarative memories (semantic or episodic)** but **could learn motor skills (mirror-drawing)** — **he learned a skill without remembering that he had learned it.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Long-term: unlimited; declarative (semantic + episodic) vs procedural.
- Declarative = conscious "knowing what"; procedural = unconscious "knowing how".
- H.M.: lost new declarative memory, kept procedural learning.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate declarative from procedural memory.</summary>

**Answer:** Declarative memory holds facts and events, is conscious and can be recalled and described ("knowing what": definition of depression, personal events); procedural memory holds skills and learned behaviors, is mostly unconscious and hard to describe ("knowing how": riding a bicycle, playing tennis), and also includes conditioned emotional responses such as fear of spiders.

**Why:** Compare & contrast slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate semantic from episodic memory.</summary>

**Answer:** Semantic memory stores facts, words, concepts, definitions and rules (most class learning); episodic memory stores personal experiences and specific events with their time and place.

**Why:** Slides 15–16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does the case of H.M. teach?</summary>

**Answer:** After brain surgery he could not form new semantic or episodic memories but could learn and improve motor skills such as mirror-drawing, showing that procedural memory is separate from declarative memory.

**Why:** Infographic.
</details>

## 5. Brain areas, memory problems and psychopathology

| Brain area | Role | Memory type |
|---|---|---|
| **Cerebral cortex** | **holds short-term memories; stores long-term memories** (words, songs, facts, events) | short- and long-term (declarative) |
| **Hippocampus** | **"Save" button — transfers declarative information to long-term memory**; **damage → cannot form new declarative memories, procedural learning intact** | declarative |
| **Amygdala** | **adds emotional meaning (fear, threat); facial emotions**; damage → memory intact but **less emotional impact** | emotional |
| **Basal ganglia** | **skills and habits** (riding a bicycle) | procedural |
| **Cerebellum** | **motor coordination and balance** (playing tennis) | procedural |
| **Motor cortex** | **storage and execution of learned motor patterns** (typing, instrument) | procedural |
- **Procedural memory does not depend on the hippocampus.** **Memories learned before brain damage remain because they are already stored in the cortex.**

### Common causes of memory problems
1. **Age** (age-associated memory impairment — forgetting names, misplacing objects).
2. **Stress and anxiety** (concentration, learning, retrieval; stress hormones affect the **hippocampus**).
3. **Lack of sleep.**
4. **Medical conditions:** **thyroid disorders, vitamin B12 deficiency, infections, head injuries, Alzheimer's disease, other dementias.**
5. **Medications and substances:** **drugs for anxiety, sleep disorders, depression; alcohol and other substances.**

### Psychopathology of memory
- **Amnesia** = partial/complete memory loss (**injury, medical conditions, trauma, psychiatric disorders**): **retrograde = old memories lost; anterograde = new memories lost; transient global amnesia = temporary, usually a few hours.**
- **Memory intrusions** = **involuntary unwanted recall of traumatic memories — PTSD.**
- **Memory distortions** = **inaccurate recall — depression, anxiety disorders, schizophrenia.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hippocampus = save button; amygdala = emotion; basal ganglia + cerebellum + motor cortex = procedural.
- Causes of memory problems: age, stress/anxiety, sleep loss, medical conditions (B12, thyroid, dementia), drugs/alcohol.
- Amnesia: retrograde (old lost) vs anterograde (new lost); intrusions (PTSD); distortions (depression, anxiety, schizophrenia).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the role of the hippocampus and amygdala in memory.</summary>

**Answer:** The hippocampus acts like a save button transferring declarative information from short-term to long-term memory, and its damage prevents new declarative memories while procedural learning remains; the amygdala adds emotional meaning (especially fear), and its damage leaves the memory intact but reduces the emotional impact.

**Why:** Brain areas infographic.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Where is procedural memory stored?</summary>

**Answer:** In the basal ganglia (skills and habits), the cerebellum (motor coordination) and motor cortical areas (learned motor patterns); it does not depend on the hippocampus.

**Why:** Brain areas infographic.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define amnesia and its types, and name memory intrusions and distortions.</summary>

**Answer:** Amnesia is partial or complete memory loss: retrograde (old memories lost), anterograde (new memories lost) and transient global amnesia (temporary, a few hours). Intrusions are involuntary unwanted recall of traumatic memories (PTSD); distortions are inaccuracies in recall (depression, anxiety disorders, schizophrenia).

**Why:** Slide "Psychopathology of memory".
</details>
