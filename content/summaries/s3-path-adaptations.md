## 1. Subcellular responses to injury

1. ⭐ **Autophagy** — an **adaptation to nutrient deprivation**: **lysosomal digestion of the cell's own components**. Organelles are **sequestered in an autophagic vacuole** → the vacuole **fuses with a lysosome** → **phagolysosome** → components digested by lysosomal enzymes.
2. **Induction of smooth endoplasmic reticulum.**
3. **Mitochondrial alterations:**
   - **↑ number in hypertrophy**, **↓ number in atrophy**;
   - **megamitochondria** (extremely large, abnormal shapes) — in **hepatocytes** with **nutritional deficiencies and alcoholic liver disease**.
4. **Cytoskeletal abnormalities** (heading on the slide).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Autophagy: adaptation to nutrient deprivation; autophagic vacuole + lysosome = phagolysosome.
- Mitochondria: ↑ in hypertrophy, ↓ in atrophy; megamitochondria in nutritional deficiency and alcoholic liver disease.

</div>

**Answer:** Lysosomal digestion of the cell's own components as an adaptation to nutrient deprivation: organelles are sequestered in an autophagic vacuole, which fuses with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome where lysosomal enzymes digest the contents.

**Answer:** Extremely large, abnormally shaped mitochondria seen in hepatocytes in nutritional deficiencies and alcoholic liver disease.

**Why:** Mitochondrial alteration in injury.

## 2. Cellular adaptations to stress

- **Adaptations** = **reversible** changes in the **number, size, phenotype, metabolic activity or function** of cells in response to environmental change.
  - **Physiologic:** response to **normal stimulation** by hormones or chemical mediators (breast and uterus in pregnancy).
  - **Pathologic:** responses to **stress** that let cells **modulate structure/function and escape injury**.

### Hypertrophy — ↑ cell SIZE
- **Bigger cells, no new cells** (more structural proteins and organelles); occurs in cells with **limited capacity to divide**.
- **Physiologic** (↑ functional demand, growth factors, hormones): **pregnant uterus** (estrogen-driven smooth-muscle hypertrophy + hyperplasia).
- **Pathologic:** **cardiac enlargement** (**hypertension, aortic valve disease**). The number of myocardial fibers never increases; normal LV wall = **1.2–1.5 cm**. Beyond a limit → **degenerative changes → ventricular dilation and heart failure**.

### Hyperplasia — ↑ cell NUMBER
- Occurs only if the tissue has **cell populations capable of replication**; stimulated by **growth factors**; may coexist with hypertrophy.
- **Physiologic:**
  - **Hormonal:** **female breast epithelium at puberty and pregnancy.**
  - **Compensatory:** after partial **liver resection**, mitosis begins as early as **12 h**, restoring normal weight (GF from uninjured hepatocytes), then proliferation is "turned off".
- **Pathologic** (excess hormones/GF): **wound healing (connective tissue)**, **HPV warts**, **benign prostatic hyperplasia (androgens)**, **endometrial hyperplasia** (↑ estrogen relative to progesterone).
- ⭐ **Remains controlled** — this **responsiveness to regulatory control distinguishes pathologic hyperplasia from cancer.**

### Atrophy — ↓ cell SIZE (loss of cell substance)
- **Causes:** **↓ workload** (limb immobilization), **loss of innervation**, **↓ blood supply**, **inadequate nutrition**, **loss of endocrine stimulation**, **aging (senile atrophy)**.
- Example: **brain atrophy** with aging and atherosclerosis → **narrow gyri, wide sulci**.

### Metaplasia — one ADULT cell type replaced by another
- **Reversible**; a cell type sensitive to a stress is replaced by one **better able to withstand it**.
- **Smokers:** **ciliated columnar → stratified squamous** epithelium of trachea/bronchi → protective **mucus secretion and ciliary clearance lost**; if persistent → **malignant transformation** (cancers arise later in these foci).
- **Chronic gastric reflux:** lower esophageal **squamous → gastric/intestinal-type columnar** epithelium (Barrett).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hypertrophy = ↑ size (no new cells): pregnant uterus (physiologic), LV in hypertension/aortic valve disease (pathologic → dilation and HF).
- Hyperplasia = ↑ number (needs replicating cells): breast, liver regeneration, BPH, endometrial, HPV warts; controlled → not cancer.
- Atrophy = ↓ size: ↓ workload, innervation, blood supply, nutrition, hormones, aging.
- Metaplasia = adult cell type replaced by another: smoker's bronchus (squamous), reflux esophagus (columnar).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define cellular adaptation and name its four types.</summary>

**Answer:** Reversible changes in the number, size, phenotype, metabolic activity or functions of cells in response to environmental changes. The types are hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy and metaplasia.

**Why:** Lecture objective.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate hypertrophy from hyperplasia with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Hypertrophy is an increase in cell size without new cells (pregnant uterus; cardiac enlargement in hypertension or aortic valve disease, which may end in ventricular dilation and heart failure). Hyperplasia is an increase in cell number in tissues able to replicate (breast at puberty, compensatory liver regeneration, benign prostatic hyperplasia, endometrial hyperplasia, warts due to HPV); it stays under regulatory control, unlike cancer.

**Why:** Size vs number.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define metaplasia and give examples with its consequences.</summary>

**Answer:** A reversible change in which one adult cell type is replaced by another that better tolerates the stress. In smokers, ciliated columnar bronchial epithelium becomes stratified squamous, losing mucus secretion and ciliary clearance and predisposing to malignancy; in chronic gastric reflux the lower esophageal squamous epithelium becomes gastric/intestinal-type columnar.

**Why:** Protective but with a price.
</details>

## 3. Intracellular accumulations

- Cells may accumulate abnormal amounts of substances (harmless or injurious), **within organelles (typically lysosomes) or in the nucleus**.

### A. Fatty change (steatosis)
- **Abnormal accumulation of triglycerides within parenchymal cells**; **most often the liver**, also the **heart**.
- **Causes:** ⭐ **alcohol, diabetes mellitus, obesity, protein malnutrition, anoxia.**
- **Gross (liver):** mild = no change; progressive = **enlarged, yellow, soft, greasy**.
- **Micro:** small vacuoles around the nucleus → **coalesce into one large clear space displacing the nucleus to the periphery (macrovesicular steatosis).**

### B. Pigments (colored substances)
**Exogenous:**
- **Carbon (coal dust)** — the commonest; phagocytosed by **alveolar macrophages** → carried to **tracheobronchial lymph nodes** → **anthracosis** (blackened nodes and lung); heavy accumulation → **fibroblastic reaction → coal workers' pneumoconiosis**.

**Endogenous:** lipofuscin, melanin, hemoglobin derivatives.
| Pigment | Features |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Lipofuscin** | **insoluble brownish-yellow granules**; in **heart, liver, brain** with **age or atrophy**; **lipid–protein complexes**; **not injurious**; **marker of past free-radical injury** |
| ⭐ **Bilirubin** | normal **bile pigment from Hb but contains NO iron**; excess → **jaundice**; in **bile-flow obstruction** (e.g. common bile duct) appears as **green-brown globules** in canaliculi, Kupffer cells, hepatocytes → **greenish liver** |
| ⭐ **Melanin** | **brown-black**, produced by **melanocytes** from **tyrosine → DOPA** oxidation |
| ⭐ **Hemosiderin** | **iron-containing aggregates of ferritin**, **golden-brown**, **blue with Prussian blue**; normal small stores in **macrophages of bone marrow, liver, spleen** |

- **Hemosiderosis:** accumulation of hemosiderin **mainly within tissue macrophages**, **no tissue/organ damage**.
- **Hemochromatosis:** **more extensive accumulation, often in parenchymal cells**, with **tissue damage, scarring, organ dysfunction**; **hereditary (primary)** or **secondary** (e.g. **multiple blood transfusions**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Steatosis = triglycerides in parenchymal cells (liver, heart): alcohol, DM, obesity, protein malnutrition, anoxia; macrovesicular with peripheral nucleus.
- Carbon → anthracosis → coal workers' pneumoconiosis.
- Lipofuscin (wear-and-tear, brown-yellow, harmless), bilirubin (no iron; jaundice), melanin (tyrosine, melanocytes), hemosiderin (iron, Prussian blue).
- Hemosiderosis = macrophages, no damage; hemochromatosis = parenchymal cells + damage.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is fatty change, its causes and its morphology in the liver?</summary>

**Answer:** Abnormal accumulation of triglycerides within parenchymal cells, mostly in the liver (also heart). Causes: alcohol, diabetes mellitus, obesity, protein malnutrition, anoxia. Grossly the liver is enlarged, yellow, soft and greasy; microscopically small perinuclear vacuoles coalesce into a large clear space pushing the nucleus to the periphery.

**Why:** Slide "Fatty change".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe lipofuscin and bilirubin.</summary>

**Answer:** Lipofuscin: insoluble brownish-yellow lipid–protein granules accumulating in the heart, liver and brain with age or atrophy; harmless but a marker of past free-radical injury. Bilirubin: normal bile pigment derived from hemoglobin without iron; excess causes jaundice and, in obstruction of bile flow, green-brown globules in canaliculi, Kupffer cells and hepatocytes.

**Why:** Endogenous pigments.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate hemosiderosis from hemochromatosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Hemosiderosis is accumulation of hemosiderin mainly in tissue macrophages with no tissue damage; hemochromatosis is more extensive accumulation often in parenchymal cells with tissue damage, scarring and organ dysfunction, either hereditary (primary) or secondary to multiple blood transfusions.

**Why:** Location and damage differ.
</details>

## 4. Pathologic calcification

- **Abnormal deposition of calcium salts.**

| | **Dystrophic calcification** | **Metastatic calcification** |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue | **dead or dying tissue** | **normal tissue** |
| Serum calcium | **NORMAL** (no calcium metabolic derangement) | **↑ (hypercalcemia)** — derangement of calcium metabolism |
| Occurs in | **areas of necrosis**, **advanced atherosclerosis**, **aging or damaged heart valves** (→ **calcific aortic stenosis in the elderly**) | **interstitial tissues of vessels, kidneys, lungs, gastric mucosa** |
| Appearance | **fine white granules/clumps, gritty**; micro: **basophilic bluish** deposits (intra/extracellular) | widespread |

### Four major causes of hypercalcemia
1. **↑ parathyroid hormone secretion**
2. **Destruction of bone**
3. **Vitamin D-related disorders**
4. **Renal failure**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Dystrophic = dead tissue, normal Ca²⁺ (necrosis, atherosclerosis, aortic valve → aortic stenosis).
- Metastatic = normal tissue, hypercalcemia (↑ PTH, bone destruction, vitamin D disorders, renal failure); affects vessels, kidneys, lungs, gastric mucosa.
- Appearance: gritty white granules; basophilic blue on H&E.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate dystrophic from metastatic calcification.</summary>

**Answer:** Dystrophic calcification occurs in dead or dying tissue with normal serum calcium (necrosis, atherosclerosis, damaged heart valves); metastatic calcification occurs in normal tissue and reflects hypercalcemia, mainly affecting the interstitium of vessels, kidneys, lungs and gastric mucosa.

**Why:** Tissue state and serum calcium.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Mention the major causes of hypercalcemia.</summary>

**Answer:** Increased secretion of parathyroid hormone, destruction of bone, vitamin D-related disorders and renal failure.

**Why:** These lead to metastatic calcification.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give an important clinical consequence of dystrophic calcification and its appearance.</summary>

**Answer:** Calcification of aortic valves is an important cause of aortic stenosis in the elderly; grossly fine white granules or gritty clumps, microscopically intra- or extracellular basophilic bluish deposits.

**Why:** Typical exam example.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Hypertrophy = size; hyperplasia = number;** both may coexist (pregnant uterus).
- **Hyperplasia needs cells able to divide; hypertrophy occurs when cells can't divide (e.g. cardiac myocytes).**
- **Hyperplasia is controlled → differs from cancer.**
- **Metaplasia = adult → adult cell; reversible; can predispose to cancer.**
- **Bilirubin has NO iron; hemosiderin has iron (Prussian blue).**
- **Lipofuscin = wear-and-tear pigment; harmless.**
- **Hemosiderosis = no damage; hemochromatosis = damage.**
- **Dystrophic: normal Ca²⁺; metastatic: ↑ Ca²⁺.**
- **Fatty change causes:** alcohol, DM, obesity, protein malnutrition, anoxia.
