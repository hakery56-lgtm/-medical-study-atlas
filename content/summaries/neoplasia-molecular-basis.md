## 1. Fundamental principles of carcinogenesis

- ⭐ **Nonlethal genetic damage (mutation)** lies at the heart of carcinogenesis; it may be **acquired** (chemicals, radiation, viruses) or **inherited in the germ line**.
- **Tumors are monoclonal:** clonal expansion of a **single precursor (founding) cell** with genetic damage.
- **Four classes of genes** are the principal targets of damage:

| Gene class | Behavior | Needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Growth-promoting proto-oncogenes** | **Dominant** | Damage to **one allele** |
| **Growth-inhibiting cancer suppressor genes** | **Recessive** | Damage to **both alleles** |
| **Genes that regulate apoptosis** | — | — |
| **DNA repair genes** | Their failure predisposes to **widespread mutations** | — |

- **No single mutation is sufficient:** carcinogenesis is **multistep**, from **accumulation of multiple mutations**, which build **subclones** with different abilities to grow, invade, metastasize and resist or respond to therapy.
- **Tumor progression:** with time tumors enlarge, become **more aggressive** and gain **greater malignant potential**.
- **Eight hallmarks:** 1 self-sufficiency in growth signals, 2 insensitivity to growth-inhibitory signals, 3 altered cellular metabolism, 4 evasion of apoptosis, 5 limitless replicative potential, 6 sustained angiogenesis, 7 invasion and metastasis, 8 evasion of immune surveillance.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Nonlethal mutation = heart of carcinogenesis; tumors are monoclonal.
- Proto-oncogenes dominant (one allele); suppressor genes recessive (both alleles).
- Multistep process; eight hallmarks.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What does "tumors are monoclonal" mean?</summary>

**Answer:** A tumor forms from clonal expansion of a single precursor cell (the founding cell) that acquired genetic damage.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name the four classes of genes targeted by genetic damage and contrast proto-oncogenes with suppressor genes.</summary>

**Answer:** Proto-oncogenes, cancer suppressor genes, apoptosis-regulating genes and DNA repair genes. Proto-oncogenes are dominant (one damaged allele transforms), suppressor genes are recessive (both alleles must be damaged).

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is carcinogenesis called multistep?</summary>

**Answer:** No single mutation is enough; multiple mutations accumulate independently in different clonal cells, generating subclones with varying ability to grow, invade, metastasize and resist therapy.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

## 2. Hallmark 1 — Self-sufficiency in growth signals (oncogenes)

- ⭐ **Gain-of-function mutations** convert **proto-oncogenes → oncogenes**, which encode **oncoproteins** that drive growth **without normal stimulation**.
- Five groups of oncoproteins:

| Group | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Growth factors** | Normally **paracrine**; cancer cells make their own → **autocrine loop** | **Glioblastoma: PDGF + receptor**; **sarcomas: TGF-α** |
| **B. Growth factor receptors** | **Mutant receptors** give continuous signals; **overexpression** = hyper-responsive to normal GF | **HER2/NEU (ERBB2)** amplified in **~20% of breast cancers** |
| **C. Signal transduction proteins** | Carry signal from inner cell membrane to nucleus | **Ras** (mutant in **~30%** of cancers; colon, pancreatic adenocarcinoma); **ABL** (translocated from **chr 9 → 22**, **BCR-ABL** constitutively active tyrosine kinase in **CML** and some acute leukemias; **target of treatment**) |
| **D. Nuclear transcription factors** | Autonomous growth | **MYC** (dysregulated in **Burkitt lymphoma**; amplified in some breast, colon, lung carcinomas) |
| **E. Cyclins and CDKs** | Control the cell cycle (**G0, G1, S, G2, M**); dysregulation favors proliferation | **Cyclin D** overexpressed in breast, esophageal and liver cancers |

- Note: **Mutant Ras is the most common oncogene abnormality** in human tumors.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Gain-of-function: proto-oncogene → oncogene → oncoprotein.
- Autocrine loop (PDGF in glioblastoma, TGF-α in sarcoma); HER2 ~20% of breast cancers.
- Ras ~30%; BCR-ABL (chr 9→22) in CML; MYC in Burkitt; cyclin D in breast, esophagus, liver.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How do oncogenes produce self-sufficiency in growth?</summary>

**Answer:** Gain-of-function mutations convert proto-oncogenes to oncogenes; their oncoproteins promote growth even without growth-promoting signals, so cells proliferate on their own.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Name the five groups of oncoproteins with one example each.</summary>

**Answer:** Growth factors (PDGF, TGF-α), growth factor receptors (HER2/NEU), signal transduction proteins (Ras, ABL), nuclear transcription factors (MYC), cyclins and CDKs (cyclin D).

**Why:** Slides 11-16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe BCR-ABL.</summary>

**Answer:** In CML and some acute leukemias, ABL moves from chromosome 9 to chromosome 22, creating a chimeric gene that encodes a constitutively active oncogenic BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase, which is a target of treatment.

**Why:** Slide 14.
</details>

## 3. Hallmark 2 — Insensitivity to growth-inhibitory signals (tumor suppressor genes)

- Suppressor genes inhibit growth by **(1)** stimulating an **antigrowth signal** so the cell enters **G0**, and **(2)** preventing **G1 → S**. Their disruption makes cells **resistant to inhibition** and **increases proliferation**.

| Gene | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **RB** ("**governor of the cell cycle**") | **First discovered** suppressor gene (lost in **retinoblastoma**); also lost in **breast, bladder, lung cancer and osteosarcoma**; **both alleles** must be mutant; regulates the **G1–S checkpoint** (hypophosphorylated RB holds **E2F**) |
| **APC** | Lost in **70–85% of sporadic colon carcinomas**; one mutant allele from birth → **hundreds to thousands of adenomatous polyps**, malignant change in one or more by the **second decade** |
| **TP53** ("**guardian of the genome**") | One of the **most commonly mutated** genes (**> 70%** of malignant tumors); prevents transformation by **temporary arrest (quiescence), permanent arrest (senescence), apoptosis**; homozygous loss → unrepaired DNA, fixed mutations, **one-way street** to malignancy; mostly **acquired**, inherited in **Li-Fraumeni syndrome** (sarcomas, breast cancer, leukemia, brain and adrenal tumors) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- RB = governor of the cell cycle; first suppressor gene; G1–S checkpoint (E2F).
- APC: 70–85% of sporadic colon cancers; polyposis if one allele inherited mutant.
- p53 = guardian of the genome: quiescence, senescence, apoptosis; > 70% of tumors; Li-Fraumeni.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How do tumor suppressor genes inhibit growth?</summary>

**Answer:** By stimulating an antigrowth signal that sends cells to G0 and by preventing passage from G1 to S phase.

**Why:** Slide 18.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the RB gene.</summary>

**Answer:** The governor of the cell cycle and first suppressor gene discovered (retinoblastoma); also lost in breast, bladder, lung cancer and osteosarcoma; both alleles must be mutant; it regulates the G1–S checkpoint.

**Why:** Slides 19-20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is p53 the "guardian of the genome"?</summary>

**Answer:** It prevents neoplastic transformation through temporary cell cycle arrest, permanent arrest (senescence) and apoptosis; with homozygous loss, DNA damage stays unrepaired and mutations become fixed.

**Why:** Slides 21-22.
</details>

## 4. Hallmarks 3–8 — Metabolism, apoptosis, immortality, angiogenesis, invasion, immune evasion

- ⭐ **3. Altered metabolism — Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis):** high **glucose uptake** and conversion to **lactate** even with enough O₂ (Otto Warburg, Nobel Prize **1931**); basis of the **PET scan**; supplies **intermediates for building cell components**.
- **4. Evasion of apoptosis:**

| Pathway | Mediated by | Inhibited by |
|---|---|---|
| **Extrinsic** | **TNF–Fas receptor** | **FLICE protein (FLIP)** |
| **Intrinsic (mitochondrial)** | **BAX, BAK** | **bcl2** |

  Tumor changes: **↓ Fas (CD95)**, **↓ BAX** (from **loss of p53**), **↑ Bcl2** (as in **85% of B-cell lymphomas**).
- **5. Limitless replication:** normal cells manage **50–70 doublings** because **telomeres shorten** with each division; malignant tumors have **increased telomerase** that **restores telomere length**.
- **6. Sustained angiogenesis:** tumors cannot exceed **1–2 mm** without vessels; angiogenesis supplies **nutrients and O₂**, endothelial cells **stimulate tumor growth** by secreting polypeptides, and is important for **metastasis**; main inducers **VEGF** and **FGF**.
- **7. Invasion and metastasis:** two phases: **(1) invasion of the extracellular matrix**, **(2) vascular dissemination and homing**.
- **8. Evasion of immune surveillance:** cancer cells express antigens, but the response to established tumors is **ineffective** because of acquired changes that avoid anti-tumor responses.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Warburg = aerobic glycolysis → PET scan.
- Extrinsic: Fas / FLIP; intrinsic: BAX-BAK / bcl2; ↑ Bcl2 in 85% of B-cell lymphomas.
- Telomerase restores telomeres; > 1–2 mm needs VEGF/FGF angiogenesis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the Warburg effect and its clinical use?</summary>

**Answer:** Cancer cells take up large amounts of glucose and convert it to lactate even when oxygen is plentiful (aerobic glycolysis); this glucose hunger is the basis of the PET scan.

**Why:** Slide 24.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do tumors evade apoptosis?</summary>

**Answer:** Decreased Fas (CD95), reduced pro-apoptotic BAX because of p53 loss, and increased Bcl2 (85% of B-cell lymphomas).

**Why:** Slide 26.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do cancer cells achieve limitless replication?</summary>

**Answer:** Normal cells stop after 50–70 doublings as telomeres shorten; malignant tumors have increased telomerase, which restores telomere length.

**Why:** Slide 28.
</details>

## 5. Genomic instability — defects in DNA repair

- **Genomic instability** = increased tendency to accumulate DNA alterations. Cancer is relatively rare despite mutagens because cells **repair DNA** or **die** when damage is unrepairable. Three repair systems:

| Defective system | Disease | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Mismatch repair** ("spell checkers", e.g. G–T instead of A–T) | **HNPCC (Lynch syndrome)** | Familial colon carcinoma, mostly **cecum and proximal colon**; **microsatellite instability (MSI)**: repeats of 1–6 nucleotides change length |
| **Nucleotide excision repair** | **Xeroderma pigmentosum** | **Autosomal recessive**; **UV cross-links pyrimidines**; greatly **increased risk of skin cancer in sun-exposed skin** |
| **Homologous recombination** | **BRCA1 / BRCA2** | **Germ line** mutations = **~50% of familial breast cancer**; BRCA1 also **epithelial ovarian** (and slightly **prostate** in men); BRCA2 breast in **men and women**, ovary, prostate, pancreas; cells develop **chromosomal breaks** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Mismatch repair → HNPCC with MSI; excision repair → xeroderma pigmentosum; homologous recombination → BRCA1/2.
- BRCA1/2 = ~50% of familial breast cancer.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the three DNA repair defects and their example diseases.</summary>

**Answer:** Mismatch repair (HNPCC), nucleotide excision repair (xeroderma pigmentosum), homologous recombination (BRCA1/BRCA2 breast and ovarian cancer).

**Why:** Slides 36-39.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is microsatellite instability?</summary>

**Answer:** Microsatellites are repeats of one to six nucleotides whose length is constant in normal people; in mismatch repair defects (HNPCC) they become unstable and change in length.

**Why:** Slide 37.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why do patients with xeroderma pigmentosum get skin cancer?</summary>

**Answer:** UV causes cross-linking of pyrimidine residues; nucleotide excision repair normally fixes this, but inherited loss of one of its proteins leads to greatly increased risk of cancers in sun-exposed skin.

**Why:** Slide 38.
</details>

## 6. Carcinogenic agents — initiation, promotion, chemicals and radiation

- **Three classes:** **chemicals, radiant energy, microbial products.**
- ⭐ **Initiation → promotion:**

| | **Initiator** | **Promoter** |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | **Permanent, irreversible** DNA damage (mutation) | Induces tumor **in initiated cells** |
| Alone | **No tumor** | **No tumor** (non-tumorigenic by itself) |
| Speed | **Rapid (minutes)**; e.g. atomic bomb damage lasting 2–3 generations | Comes **after** the initiator |
| Dose | **Dose-independent** (even small doses; pregnant woman avoids X-ray) | **Dose-dependent** (e.g. estrogen up to a level) |
| Reversibility | Irreversible | **Reversible**; examples: **drugs, hormones** |

- **Chemical carcinogens:** **direct-acting** (no activation; weak; e.g. **alkylating chemotherapy** that cures e.g. **Hodgkin lymphoma** but may cause a second cancer, usually **leukemia**) vs **indirect-acting** (need **liver enzymes**): **benzo[a]pyrene** (cigarette smoke, smoked meat), **aromatic amines/azo dyes** (**β-naphthylamine → bladder carcinoma**). Others: **aflatoxin B1 → hepatocellular carcinoma**, **nitrosamines → gastric carcinoma**, **asbestos → bronchogenic carcinoma**, **vinyl chloride → angiosarcoma**, **chromium → lung**, **nickel → nasopharyngeal carcinoma**.
- **Ionizing radiation** (X-ray, gamma, proton, neutron): at risk are **radiologists, unprotected miners, atomic bomb survivors, therapeutic irradiation** (head/neck → **papillary thyroid cancer** years later); causes **leukemia, thyroid, lung cancer**; acts as **initiator** (**chromosome breakage** commonest, translocation, rarely point mutation); **long latent period**.
- **UV light:** **melanoma, squamous cell and basal cell carcinoma**; **fair-skinned** people (Australia, New Zealand); forms **pyrimidine dimers** repaired by nucleotide excision repair, overwhelmed with heavy exposure.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Initiator: irreversible, rapid, dose-independent; promoter: reversible, dose-dependent, not tumorigenic alone.
- Indirect carcinogens need liver activation (benzo[a]pyrene, β-naphthylamine).
- Radiation = initiator with long latency; UV = pyrimidine dimers.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Contrast an initiator with a promoter.</summary>

**Answer:** The initiator causes rapid, permanent, irreversible, dose-independent DNA damage; the promoter acts after it, is dose-dependent and reversible, and is non-tumorigenic alone. Neither alone causes a tumor.

**Why:** Slides 42-44.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare direct and indirect-acting chemical carcinogens with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Direct agents need no metabolic activation (weak; alkylating chemotherapy that can cause second leukemias); indirect agents need liver enzymes (benzo[a]pyrene, aromatic amines such as β-naphthylamine causing bladder carcinoma).

**Why:** Slides 45-46.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the carcinogenic mechanism of ionizing radiation.</summary>

**Answer:** It acts as an initiator through its mutagenic effect: chromosome breakage (commonest), translocation and less commonly point mutation, with a long latent period of years.

**Why:** Slide 49.
</details>

## 7. Viral and microbial carcinogenesis

| Agent | Tumors / mechanism |
|---|---|
| **HTLV-1** (only human retrovirus firmly implicated) | **T-cell leukemia/lymphoma** (endemic in **Japan**) |
| **HPV** | Warts (types **1, 2, 4, 7**); genital warts (low-risk **6, 11**); **cervical and anogenital squamous cell carcinoma** (high-risk **16, 18**); **≥ 20% of oropharyngeal carcinomas**. **E7** binds **RB** and frees **E2F**; **E6** binds and degrades **p53 and BAX**; infection alone is **not sufficient** (needs host mutations, e.g. **RAS**) |
| **EBV** (herpesvirus; **first virus linked to a human tumor**) | **Burkitt lymphoma**, **B-cell lymphomas in immunosuppressed**, subset of **Hodgkin lymphoma**, subset of **nasopharyngeal carcinoma**; infects **B cells via CD21** → polyclonal proliferation and immortal lines; **EBNA2** transactivates **cyclin D** |
| **HBV / HCV** | **70–85% of hepatocellular carcinomas**; multifactorial, dominant effect = **immune-mediated chronic inflammation** → hepatocyte death, regeneration, genomic damage |
| **Helicobacter pylori** (**first bacterium** classified as carcinogen) | **Gastric adenocarcinoma and gastric lymphoma**; sequence **gastritis → atrophy → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → cancer**, takes **decades**, only **3%** of infected; **CagA** injected into gastric cells mimics growth factor signaling; lymphoma: **H. pylori-reactive T cells → polyclonal B-cell proliferation → monoclonal tumor** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- HPV E6 → p53/BAX degradation; E7 → RB inactivation; types 16/18 high risk, 6/11 genital warts.
- EBV via CD21: Burkitt, B-cell lymphomas, Hodgkin subset, nasopharyngeal carcinoma subset.
- HBV/HCV = 70–85% of HCC; H. pylori = first bacterial carcinogen (CagA).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does HPV cause cancer?</summary>

**Answer:** Through E6 and E7: E7 binds RB and displaces E2F, driving the cell cycle; E6 binds and degrades p53 and BAX. HPV alone is insufficient; host mutations such as RAS are also needed.

**Why:** Slides 53-54.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the tumors associated with EBV and its receptor.</summary>

**Answer:** Burkitt lymphoma, B-cell lymphomas in immunosuppressed individuals, a subset of Hodgkin lymphoma and a subset of nasopharyngeal carcinoma; it infects B cells through CD21.

**Why:** Slides 55-56.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe H. pylori carcinogenesis.</summary>

**Answer:** Chronic inflammation/gastritis → atrophy → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → cancer over decades in about 3% of infected patients; CagA mimics growth-factor signaling; for lymphoma, reactive T cells drive polyclonal B-cell proliferation from which a monoclonal tumor emerges.

**Why:** Slides 58-59.
</details>

## 8. Tumor immunity

- **Immune surveillance** = recognition and destruction of abnormal/potentially malignant cells. **Tumor markers** = tumor substances/antigens used for **diagnosis or screening**.
- **Classic classes:** **tumor-specific** (only tumor cells) vs **tumor-associated** (tumor cells and some normal cells) — imperfect.
- ⭐ **Modern classification of tumor antigens (6):**

| # | Class | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Products of mutated genes** (never seen by immune system → non-self) | Mutated proto-oncogene / suppressor proteins |
| 2 | **Overexpressed/aberrantly expressed** cellular proteins | **Tyrosinase** (melanocytes, melanomas) |
| 3 | **Oncogenic virus antigens** | **HPV, EBV, HBV** |
| 4 | **Oncofetal antigens** | **CEA** (GIT, pancreas, biliary, breast); **AFP** (hepatocellular carcinoma) |
| 5 | **Altered glycolipids/glycoproteins** | **CA-125** (ovary), **CA-19-9** (pancreas, biliary), **MUC-1** (breast) |
| 6 | **Cell type-specific differentiation antigens** (identify tissue of origin) | **CD10, CD20** (B-cell lymphoma) |

- **Effector mechanisms:** **cell-mediated immunity** dominates; **CTLs** recognize peptides on **class I MHC**, protect against **virus-associated tumors** (EBV, HPV); **NK cells** and **macrophages** also contribute.
- **Immune evasion:** **(1) selective outgrowth of antigen-negative variants**, **(2) loss/reduced MHC (HLA class I)**, **(3) lack of costimulation**, **(4) inhibition of T-cell activation — immune checkpoints:** **PD-L1** on tumor cells engages **PD-1** on CTLs → CTLs become unresponsive.
- **Checkpoint inhibitors:** antibodies blocking checkpoints; response **10–30%** in solid tumors (melanoma, lung, bladder), higher in **Hodgkin lymphoma**; side effect: **autoimmune manifestations such as colitis**.
- Note: the slides number the immune-evasion list 1, 2, 3 and then "5"; four mechanisms are described.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Six classes of tumor antigens; CEA and AFP = oncofetal; CA-125, CA-19-9, MUC-1 = glycoproteins.
- CTLs (class I MHC) are the main antitumor defense.
- Evasion: antigen-negative variants, ↓ MHC I, no costimulation, PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify tumor antigens and give an example of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Mutated gene products; overexpressed cellular proteins (tyrosinase); oncogenic virus antigens (HPV, EBV, HBV); oncofetal antigens (CEA, AFP); altered glycolipids/glycoproteins (CA-125, CA-19-9, MUC-1); differentiation antigens (CD10, CD20).

**Why:** Slides 62-67.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which cells mediate the effective antitumor response?</summary>

**Answer:** Cell-mediated immunity: cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognizing peptides on class I MHC (especially against EBV- and HPV-associated tumors), plus NK cells and macrophages.

**Why:** Slide 68.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain how tumors evade immunity and how checkpoint inhibitors help.</summary>

**Answer:** Tumors select antigen-negative variants, lose MHC class I, lack costimulation and use PD-L1 to engage PD-1 on CTLs. Antibodies blocking these checkpoints release the brakes on immunity (10–30% response in solid tumors), with autoimmune side effects such as colitis.

**Why:** Slides 69-72.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Proto-oncogenes are dominant (one allele); suppressor genes are recessive (both alleles).**
- **Initiator = irreversible and dose-independent; promoter = reversible and dose-dependent; neither alone causes a tumor.**
- **Intrinsic apoptosis = BAX/BAK, inhibited by bcl2; extrinsic = Fas, inhibited by FLIP.**
- **HPV: E7 inactivates RB, E6 degrades p53; types 16/18 = cancer, 6/11 = genital warts.**
- **Do not mix BRCA1 (ovarian) with BRCA2 (male breast, pancreas); both are homologous recombination repair.**
- **CEA (GIT/pancreas/biliary/breast) vs AFP (hepatocellular carcinoma); CA-125 ovary, CA-19-9 pancreas, MUC-1 breast.**
- **Ras ~30% (colon, pancreas); HER2 ~20% of breast cancers; TP53 > 70%; APC 70–85% of sporadic colon cancers.**
