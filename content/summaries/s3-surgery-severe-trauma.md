## 1. Identification of severe trauma, trauma team and primary survey

- Severely injured patients with **multiple injuries** have **unique diagnostic and treatment challenges**; assessment and management **begin in the prehospital environment.**
- **Key information:** age and gender, **mechanism of injury**, **vital signs** (RR, PR, BP, temperature) and **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS).**
- **Trauma team:** every hospital managing severe trauma should have one; it applies **ATLS** principles to **rapidly identify and treat life-threatening injuries during the primary survey.**
- ⭐ **Primary survey = cABCDE:** **c** Control of massive external haemorrhage; **A** Airway with cervical spine protection; **B** Breathing and ventilation; **C** Circulation and haemorrhage control; **D** Disability (neurological status); **E** Exposure (assess for other injuries).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Assessment starts prehospital: demographics, mechanism, vital signs, GCS.
- Trauma team applies ATLS in the primary survey.
- cABCDE: the small c (massive external bleeding) comes first.

</div>

## 2. c: Exsanguinating external haemorrhage and A: Airway

- ⭐ **Exsanguinating external haemorrhage** from massive **arterial** bleeding is controlled **even before the airway**; usually due to **gunshot or blasts.**
- Control: **packs and pressure** onto the wound and artery; if direct pressure fails in a limb, apply a **tourniquet proximal to the wound.**
- **Tourniquet:** the limb becomes **ischaemic**; **record the time applied**; the patient needs **urgent surgical control** of bleeding to **reperfuse** the limb.
- **Airway:** **immobilise and protect the cervical spine** in all trauma patients; assess the airway immediately.
- **Compromised airway, stepwise:** 1) clear it (**suction** secretions or blood); 2) simple manoeuvres (**jaw thrust, chin lift**); 3) **oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway**; 4) advanced airway: **cuffed endotracheal tube.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Massive external bleeding is controlled before the airway.
- Pressure first, then a tourniquet proximal to the wound; record the time.
- Airway ladder: suction, manoeuvres, adjuncts, cuffed ETT, with C-spine protection.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is exsanguinating limb haemorrhage controlled?</summary>

**Answer:** Packs and direct pressure on the wound and artery; if this fails, a tourniquet proximal to the wound, with the time recorded, followed by urgent surgical control to reperfuse the limb.

**Why:** Slides 6-7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the time of tourniquet application recorded?</summary>

**Answer:** The limb becomes ischaemic once the tourniquet is on, so the duration must be known and the patient needs urgent surgery to reperfuse the limb.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Outline the stepwise management of a compromised airway.</summary>

**Answer:** Suction secretions or blood; jaw thrust or chin lift; oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway; cuffed endotracheal tube, with the cervical spine immobilised throughout.

**Why:** Slide 8.
</details>

## 3. B: Breathing and C: Circulation and haemorrhage control

- **Breathing:** **high-flow oxygen** for all. **Life-threatening chest pathologies:** **tension pneumothorax, haemothorax, flail segment** → diagnose and manage immediately; **intercostal chest drains** must be available.
- **Circulation:** **at least two large-bore IV cannulae**; **central or intraosseous** access if peripheral access is difficult; blood for **cross-match, lab tests, Hb and venous lactate.**
- **Shock signs:** skin **pale, cool, sweaty**; **pulse > 100/min**; **low BP.**
- **Pelvic binder:** all **haemodynamically unstable patients with blunt trauma**; **not removed until a pelvic fracture is excluded.**
- **Hypotensive trauma patients are hypovolaemic until proven otherwise;** priority = **simultaneous fluid resuscitation and identification of the bleeding source.**
- **Aim:** keep blood supply to **brain, heart and kidneys.** **Avoid excess crystalloid or colloid** (haemodilution, ↑ coagulopathy, ↑ risk of **ARDS**); use **warmed blood and blood products.**
- ⭐ **Massive transfusion protocol: 1 PRBC : 1 FFP : 1 platelets.**
- **Tranexamic acid** (antifibrinolytic): **1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 h; within 3 hours of injury;** for suspected significant haemorrhage, including SBP < 110 mmHg or PR > 110/min (per slide).

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Chest threats** | Tension pneumothorax, haemothorax, flail segment |
| **IV access** | ≥ 2 large-bore cannulae; central or intraosseous if needed |
| **Transfusion ratio** | 1 PRBC : 1 FFP : 1 platelets |
| **Tranexamic acid** | 1 g over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 h, within 3 h |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- High-flow O2; treat tension pneumothorax, haemothorax and flail segment at once.
- Two large-bore IV lines, bloods incl. Hb and lactate, pelvic binder if unstable blunt trauma.
- Blood products 1:1:1, not crystalloid; tranexamic acid within 3 hours.

</div>

## 4. Finding the bleeding, D: Disability, E: Exposure and secondary survey

- **Major haemorrhage sites:** **chest, abdomen, pelvis, long bones, external.** Examination and investigations rapidly **confirm or exclude** bleeding at each.
- ⭐ **Whole body CT (head to pelvis, IV contrast)** = **gold standard** in multiple injury or deranged physiology; early chest and pelvis X-rays are increasingly omitted in favour of CT.
- **FAST** (Focused Abdominal Sonography for Trauma) helps locate the major bleeding source; views: **subxiphoid, suprapubic, left upper quadrant, right upper quadrant.**
- **Disability:** calculate **GCS**, assess **pupils** (size, light reaction) and whether **all four limbs move.**

| GCS score | Eye (E) | Verbal (V) | Motor (M) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **6** | | | Obeys commands |
| **5** | | Oriented | Localises to pain |
| **4** | Spontaneous | Confused | Withdraws from pain |
| **3** | To speech | Inappropriate words | Flexion to pain |
| **2** | To pain | Incomprehensible sounds | Extension to pain |
| **1** | None | None | None |

- **Exposure:** completely undress the patient for systematic examination **but prevent hypothermia** (trauma patients are often hypothermic, which **increases coagulopathy**).
- **Secondary survey:** detailed **top-to-toe examination** after life-threatening injuries have been managed.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five bleeding sites; WBCT is the gold standard, FAST helps locate the source.
- GCS = E (1-4) + V (1-5) + M (1-6), range 3 to 15, plus pupils and limb movement.
- Expose but keep warm; secondary survey after the primary survey.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the major sites of haemorrhage in trauma and the best investigation?</summary>

**Answer:** Chest, abdomen, pelvis, long bones and external bleeding; whole body CT with IV contrast is the gold standard, with FAST to locate the source.

**Why:** Slides 22-24.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is assessed under Disability?</summary>

**Answer:** GCS (eye, verbal, motor), pupil size and reaction to light, and whether the patient moves all four limbs.

**Why:** Slides 25-26.
</details>

**Answer:** Trauma patients are frequently hypothermic and hypothermia further increases coagulopathy.

</details>

## 5. Damage control surgery versus early total care

- **Why DCS:** severely traumatised patients with **impaired physiology** do poorly after **lengthy, complex reconstruction** soon after trauma; prolonged procedures add trauma and immune and physiological derangement.
- ⭐ **Triad of death: acidosis, coagulopathy, hypothermia** → multiorgan failure and death.
- **DCS allows only rapid life- and limb-saving procedures:** 1) **control of haemorrhage**; 2) **decompression of cavities** (e.g. craniotomy); 3) **revascularisation** of ischaemic organs and limbs; 4) **removal of contamination.** Definitive reconstruction is **deferred** until resuscitated and optimised.
- **Abdominal DCS:** packing and control of haemorrhage; debridement and resection of devitalised tissue; removal of contamination (foreign bodies, faeces).
- **Orthopaedic DCS:** debridement of severe open fractures; rapid temporary splintage or stabilisation of long bone fractures; decompression of compartment syndrome.
- **Good response to resuscitation:** PR < 100/min, normal BP, normal RR, **urine output > 30 mL/h**, no hypothermia, no acidosis on ABG, normal coagulation screen, normal lactate → **safe to proceed to definitive repair.**
- **Venous lactate:** **< 2 mmol/L** = probably resuscitated, suitable for **early total care**; **> 3 mmol/L** = under-resuscitated → **further resuscitation or DCS** if surgery is urgent.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- DCS = brief life-saving surgery when physiology is impaired; reconstruct later.
- Triad of death: acidosis, coagulopathy, hypothermia.
- Lactate < 2 mmol/L: early total care; lactate > 3 mmol/L: keep resuscitating or DCS.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why was damage control surgery developed?</summary>

**Answer:** Patients with impaired physiology do poorly after lengthy reconstruction; prolonged surgery adds trauma and derangement, leading to the triad of death (acidosis, coagulopathy, hypothermia).

**Why:** Slide 31.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which procedures are permitted in DCS?</summary>

**Answer:** Control of haemorrhage, decompression of cavities (e.g. craniotomy), revascularisation of ischaemic organs and limbs, and removal of contamination.

**Why:** Slide 32.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do the indices and lactate guide early total care versus DCS?</summary>

**Answer:** Normal PR, BP, RR, urine output > 30 mL/h, temperature, ABG, coagulation and lactate < 2 mmol/L mean it is safe for definitive repair; lactate > 3 mmol/L means under-resuscitation, so more resuscitation or DCS if urgent.

**Why:** Slides 34-35.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **The small c (massive external bleeding) comes before Airway.**
- **Tourniquet goes proximal to the wound, and the time must be recorded.**
- **Resuscitate with blood products 1:1:1, not crystalloid or colloid.**
- **Tranexamic acid: 1 g over 10 min then 1 g over 8 h, within 3 hours.**
- **Pelvic binder stays on until a pelvic fracture is excluded.**
- **Triad of death = acidosis, coagulopathy, hypothermia.**
- **Lactate < 2 = early total care; lactate > 3 = under-resuscitated.**
