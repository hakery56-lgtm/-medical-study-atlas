## 1. Blood samples and skin puncture

- **Peripheral (capillary) smear:** direct examination of parasites (**malaria, filariasis**). **Venous blood:** **serology, biochemistry, culture**; **better for hematology**.
- **Capillary sites:** **ring finger tip, ear lobe; heel or toe in infants/children.**
- **Technique:** sterilize with **spirit or 70% alcohol**, let **dry**, puncture with **sterile lancet** deeply, **wipe off the first drop**, **never press out blood.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Capillary for parasites; venous for serology/culture.
- Wipe the first drop; never squeeze.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare capillary and venous samples.</summary>

**Answer:** Capillary smears are for direct parasite examination (malaria, filariasis); venous blood is for serology, biochemistry and culture and is better for hematology.

**Why:** Slide 2.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the capillary puncture sites.</summary>

**Answer:** Ring finger tip and ear lobe in adults; heel or toe in infants and children.

**Why:** Slides 2 and 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the puncture technique.</summary>

**Answer:** Sterilize with alcohol, dry, puncture deeply with a sterile lancet, wipe off the first drop and never press out blood.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

## 2. Thin and thick films

| Feature | Thin film | Thick film |
|---|---|---|
| **Made by** | **Small drop spread with a smooth-edged spreader at an angle** | **4 drops mixed on the center with a wooden stick/slide corner** |
| **Layers** | **Nearly one layer of RBCs** | **10–20 layers over about 0.5 cm²** |
| **Fixation** | **Methyl alcohol** | **Not fixed with alcohol; dried at room temperature (no dust, insects, heat)** |
| **Use** | **Describe blood cells; identify hemoparasites (Plasmodium species, microfilaria)** | **Mainly detect malaria or filariasis** |

- **Thin film steps:** drop → spread → air dry → **fix** → stain (**Giemsa/Leishman**) → air dry → **100×**.
- **Thick film steps:** 4 drops → mix → air dry → stain → air dry → 100×.
- **Parasites in peripheral blood:** **microfilaria (W. bancrofti), malaria ring stage, Leishmania amastigote, trypanosome trypomastigote.** **Ring stage:** A = P. vivax, ovale, malariae; B = P. falciparum.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Thick film = detection; thin film = identification.
- Thick film not fixed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare thin and thick films.</summary>

**Answer:** Thin: single layer, fixed with methyl alcohol, for cell description and parasite identification; thick: 10–20 layers, not alcohol-fixed, mainly for detecting malaria or filariasis.

**Why:** Slides 9, 12, 14, 16.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the steps of the thin film.</summary>

**Answer:** Drop of blood, spread, air dry, methyl alcohol fixation, Giemsa/Leishman staining, air dry, examine at 100×.

**Why:** Slide 12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List parasites in peripheral blood.</summary>

**Answer:** Microfilaria (W. bancrofti), Plasmodium ring stage, Leishmania amastigote and trypanosome trypomastigote.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

## 3. Fixation and stains

- **Fixation** prevents **hemolysis** (alcohol denatures proteins and hardens cell contents) with **acetone-free methyl alcohol (1–2 min)**.
- **Leishman:** **no pre-fixation** (contains acetone-free methyl alcohol). **Giemsa:** **pre-fixation a must** (alcohol only 5% in ready-to-use stain).
- **Important stains:** **Leishman, Giemsa, hematoxylin.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Leishman needs no pre-fixation; Giemsa does.
- Fixation prevents hemolysis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why are films fixed?</summary>

**Answer:** To prevent hemolysis and harden cell contents.

**Why:** Slide 21.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which stain needs pre-fixation and why?</summary>

**Answer:** Giemsa, because its ready-to-use alcohol content is only 5%; Leishman already contains acetone-free methyl alcohol.

**Why:** Slide 21.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the stains for blood parasites.</summary>

**Answer:** Leishman, Giemsa and hematoxylin.

**Why:** Slide 22.
</details>

## 4. Staining methods

- **Leishman:** powder in **acetone-free methyl alcohol**, glass-stoppered bottle, **do not filter**; **buffer pH 6.8**. **Method:** **about 8 drops, wait 2 min → add 16 drops buffered water, mix, wait 7–10 min → flood off with distilled water in 2–3 s** (longer washing removes stain) → drain, dry.
- **Giemsa:** stock **diluted 1 ml stain : 9 ml buffered distilled water**; **fix 3–5 min**, stain **15 min or longer**, wash with tap/neutral distilled water, dry.
- **Hematoxylin:** blood and tissue parasites (hemoparasites, microfilaria); **boil 800 ml distilled water + potash alum; 4 g hematoxylin in 60 ml ethanol; combine.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Leishman: 8 drops, 2 min; 16 drops, 7–10 min; wash 2–3 s.
- Giemsa: 1:9, 15 min.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe Leishman's staining method.</summary>

**Answer:** About 8 drops for 2 minutes, add 16 drops buffered water and mix, wait 7–10 minutes, then flood off with distilled water within 2–3 seconds and dry.

**Why:** Slide 23.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe Giemsa staining.</summary>

**Answer:** Dilute the stock 1:9 with buffered water; fix with methyl alcohol for 3–5 minutes; stain 15 minutes or longer; wash with tap or neutral distilled water and dry.

**Why:** Slide 24.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is hematoxylin used for?</summary>

**Answer:** Mainly for blood and tissue parasitic diseases such as hemoparasites and microfilaria.

**Why:** Slide 25.
</details>
