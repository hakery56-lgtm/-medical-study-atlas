## 1. Facts about the cell & cell composition

### Interesting facts
- The body contains about **75–100 trillion cells**.
- A typical cell is about **10 square micrometers** in size and **1 nanogram** in mass.
- More than **10,000 cell membranes** stacked equal the **thickness of a sheet of paper**; yet the total membrane surface area in the body is more than **27,871 m²** (≈ **4 football fields**).
- The **phospholipids** act like the insulation of a **double-glazed window**, selectively letting substances through.

### Cell composition
| Component | Percentage |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Water** | **70–85%** |
| **Proteins** | **10–20%** |
| **Lipids** | **2%** (mainly **phospholipids and cholesterol**) |
| **Carbohydrates** | **1%** |
| **Electrolytes** | **K⁺, Mg²⁺, phosphate, sulfate, bicarbonate**, and small amounts of **Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Body ≈ 75–100 trillion cells; a membrane is extremely thin but its total surface area is huge.
- Cell: water 70–85%, proteins 10–20%, lipids 2%, carbohydrates 1%, electrolytes (mainly K⁺, Mg²⁺, phosphate, sulfate, bicarbonate).

</div>

**Answer:** Water 70–85%, proteins 10–20%, lipids about 2% (mainly phospholipids and cholesterol), carbohydrates about 1%, and electrolytes — mainly potassium, magnesium, phosphate, sulfate and bicarbonate, with small amounts of sodium, chloride and calcium.

**Why:** A percentage table.
</details>

**Why:** Opening fact of the lecture.

## 2. The plasma membrane

### Composition
- **Lipids 40%** and **protein 60%** of the membrane.
- **Lipid-soluble substances** cross easily: **O₂, CO₂, N₂, anesthetic gases, steroid hormones, alcohol**.
- **Water-soluble substances** need help: **electrolytes, glucose, urea, H₂O**.
- **Cholesterol** has a **rigid** structure.
- **Phospholipid:** **polar head (hydrophilic)** + **nonpolar tails (hydrophobic)**.
- Membrane proteins: **integral** (membrane-spanning) and **peripheral**; plus **glycoproteins, glycolipids and the glycocalyx**.

### Functions of the cell membrane
1. **Maintenance of cell shape and structure.**
2. ⭐ **Transport** — selective permeability maintains the **ionic composition, osmotic pressure, pH and contents** of the cell.
3. **Intercellular communication** — signal transduction (nerve transmission, hormone release, muscle contraction).
4. **Intercellular adhesion** by specialized junctions.
5. **Directed cell movement.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Membrane = 40% lipid + 60% protein; lipid-soluble substances (O₂, CO₂, steroids, alcohol) cross directly; water-soluble ones need channels/carriers.
- Five functions: shape, transport, communication, adhesion, directed movement.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the composition of the plasma membrane.</summary>

**Answer:** The membrane is about 40% lipids (phospholipids with polar hydrophilic heads and nonpolar hydrophobic tails, plus rigid cholesterol) and 60% proteins (integral and peripheral, including glycoproteins and glycolipids that form the glycocalyx).

**Why:** Percentages + parts.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give examples of lipid-soluble and water-soluble substances.</summary>

**Answer:** Lipid-soluble: oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen, anesthetic gases, steroid hormones and alcohol. Water-soluble: electrolytes, glucose, urea and water.

**Why:** Which can cross the lipid bilayer directly.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the functions of the cell membrane.</summary>

**Answer:** Maintaining cell shape and structure; transport through selective permeability (maintaining ionic composition, osmotic pressure, pH and contents); intercellular communication through signal transduction; intercellular adhesion by specialized junctions; and directed cell movement.

**Why:** Five functions.
</details>

## 3. Ion channels, receptors & the glycocalyx

### Transmembrane ion channel proteins
1. **Specificity** — highly selective for one or more ions or molecules.
2. They are **open channels** or **gated channels**:
   - ⭐ **Voltage-gated** — e.g. **Na⁺ and K⁺ channels**.
   - ⭐ **Chemical (ligand)-gated** — e.g. **Ca²⁺-sensitive K⁺ channel**.
   - ⭐ **Physical-gated** — in the membrane of **pressure receptors**.

### Receptors
- **Proteins or glycoproteins**, located on the **cell surface**, or within the **cytoplasm or nucleus**.

### Glycocalyx (carbohydrate coat)
A. **Repels** other negative objects. B. **Attaches cells** to each other. C. Acts as a **receptor**. D. Takes part in **immune reactions** — **antigen markers** identifying the cells of an individual as **"self"**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Ion channels are specific and either open or gated: voltage-gated (Na⁺, K⁺), ligand-gated (Ca²⁺-sensitive K⁺), physically gated (pressure receptors).
- Receptors are proteins/glycoproteins on the surface, in the cytoplasm or in the nucleus.
- Glycocalyx: repels negative objects, adhesion, receptor, "self" markers.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify ion channels according to gating, with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Channels are open or gated. Gated channels may be voltage-gated (Na⁺ and K⁺ channels), chemically (ligand) gated (Ca²⁺-sensitive K⁺ channel) or physically gated (in the membrane of pressure receptors).

**Why:** Three gates + one example each.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the functions of the glycocalyx?</summary>

**Answer:** It repels other negative objects, attaches cells to each other, acts as a receptor and, as antigen markers, enters immune reactions by identifying the cells of an individual as "self".

**Why:** Four functions A–D.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Where are receptors located and what are they made of?</summary>

**Answer:** Receptors are proteins or glycoproteins found on the cell surface or inside the cell, in the cytoplasm or in the nucleus.

**Why:** Location decides which signals they bind (lipophilic vs lipophobic).
</details>

## 4. Cilia and flagella

- **Cilia** = **thread-like projections** through the cell membrane, each **anchored by a basal body** just inside the membrane; **non-motile (more widespread)** or **motile (less widespread)**.

| Site | Function |
|---|---|
| **Inner ear** | sense of **balance** (non-motile) |
| **Retina** | **light-absorbing part** of receptor cells (non-motile) |
| **Kidney tubules** | **monitor fluid flow** (non-motile) |
| **Nose** | **bind odor molecules** (non-motile) |
| **Respiratory tract and uterine (fallopian) tubes** | **sweeping** (motile) |

- **Cilia vs flagellum (figure):** cilia beat in **power stroke and recovery stroke**; flagellum shows propeller-like back-and-forth motion.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cilia are anchored by a basal body; most are non-motile (sensory), a few are motile (respiratory tract, uterine tubes).
- Sensory cilia: balance (inner ear), light absorption (retina), fluid-flow sensing (kidney tubules), odor binding (nose).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are cilia and how are they anchored?</summary>

**Answer:** Cilia are thread-like projections through the cell membrane, anchored inside the cell by a basal body just beneath the membrane; they may be non-motile (more widespread) or motile (less widespread).

**Why:** Definition + anchor.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the functions of non-motile cilia.</summary>

**Answer:** In the inner ear they contribute to balance; in the retina they form the light-absorbing part of photoreceptors; in kidney tubules they monitor fluid flow; in the nose they bind odor molecules.

**Why:** Four sites.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Where are motile cilia found and what do they do?</summary>

**Answer:** In the respiratory tract and the uterine (fallopian) tubes, where they sweep (e.g. mucus, the ovum) with a power stroke followed by a recovery stroke.

**Why:** The two motile sites.
</details>

## 5. Cell-to-cell adhesion

### A. Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs)
**Integral membrane proteins** with **cytoplasmic, transmembrane and extracellular domains**: **immunoglobulin-like adhesion molecules, integrins, cadherins, selectins**.

### B. Extracellular matrix (ECM) — the biological "glue"
**Gel (ground substance) + network of fibrous proteins**: **collagen** (flexible, **non-elastic** fibers) and **elastin** (flexible, **elastic** fibers).

### C. Specialized membrane junctions
| Junction | Features | Where |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Tight junction** | **A. impermeable (non-leaky):** separates compartments of different chemical composition; **B. permeable (leaky):** lets substances pass depending on size, charge and characteristics; **occludin** proteins | epithelia |
| ⭐ **Gap junction** | **channel between two adjacent cells** (**connexin** proteins); **cytoplasm of the two cells connected**; **ions transfer without entering the ECF** | **cardiac and smooth muscle** |
| ⭐ **Desmosome (adherens)** | **cadherin** proteins, plaque, actin/intermediate filaments; hold cells together | **skin and heart muscle** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Three means of adhesion: CAMs (immunoglobulin-like, integrins, cadherins, selectins), the ECM (collagen, elastin), specialized junctions.
- Tight junction (occludin): non-leaky or leaky; gap junction (connexin): cytoplasmic continuity in cardiac and smooth muscle; desmosome: mechanical adhesion in skin and heart.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the means of cell-to-cell adhesion.</summary>

**Answer:** (A) Cell adhesion molecules — integral membrane proteins such as immunoglobulin-like molecules, integrins, cadherins and selectins; (B) the extracellular matrix — ground substance plus collagen (non-elastic) and elastin (elastic) fibers; (C) specialized membrane junctions — tight junctions, gap junctions and adherens junctions/desmosomes.

**Why:** A, B, C.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare tight, gap and desmosome junctions.</summary>

**Answer:** Tight junctions (occludin) seal neighbouring cells and can be impermeable (separating compartments of different composition) or leaky (permeable by size and charge). Gap junctions (connexin) form channels connecting the cytoplasm of two cells so ions pass without entering the ECF (cardiac and smooth muscle). Desmosomes (cadherin, with plaque and filaments) anchor cells mechanically, especially in skin and heart muscle.

**Why:** Protein + function + site.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the extracellular matrix?</summary>

**Answer:** The biological "glue" made of a gel (ground substance) and a network of fibrous proteins — collagen (flexible, non-elastic fibers) and elastin (flexible, elastic fibers).

**Why:** Gel + two fibers.
</details>

## 6. Intercellular communication (signal transduction)

- **Signal transduction** = the process by which a cell **converts an input signal into a response**.
- **Input:** electrical, physical or chemical signal → cell → **change in chemical composition** → **response**.
- **Responses:** change in **metabolic activity**, **contraction/relaxation**, **secretion**, **release of another signaling substance**, **cell growth and division**, **cell death (apoptosis)**.
- Analogy: a **radio** — external signal → receptor, transducer, amplifier → response (sound).

### A. Electrical communication
**Ion flow → change in membrane potential → activation of voltage-gated channels → influx/efflux of ions → change in chemical composition → response.**

### B. Chemical communication
| Pathway | Example |
|---|---|
| **1. Endocrine** | **ACTH** from the anterior pituitary → **cortisol** (remote target via blood) |
| **2. Paracrine** | **somatostatin** → decreases secretin (adjacent target) |
| **3. Autocrine** | the cell acts on **itself** |
| **4. Contact-dependent** | membrane-attached signals on adjacent cells |
| **5. Intracrine** | **steroids** acting inside the cell |

Chemical communication = binding of the **1st messenger (ligand)** to a receptor.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Signal transduction = converting an input signal into a response (metabolic change, contraction, secretion, growth, apoptosis).
- Electrical communication uses voltage-gated channels; chemical communication uses the 1st messenger binding a receptor.
- Chemical pathways: endocrine, paracrine, autocrine, contact-dependent, intracrine.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define signal transduction and list the possible cell responses.</summary>

**Answer:** It is the process by which a cell converts an input (electrical, physical or chemical) signal into a response. Responses include change in metabolic activity, contraction or relaxation, secretion, release of another signaling substance, cell growth and division, and cell death (apoptosis).

**Why:** Definition + six responses.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe electrical communication.</summary>

**Answer:** Ion flow changes the cell membrane potential, which activates voltage-gated ion channels, causing influx or efflux of ions; this changes the cell's chemical composition and produces a response.

**Why:** A chain of five steps.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Classify chemical communication with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Endocrine (e.g. ACTH from the anterior pituitary acting on the adrenal cortex to release cortisol), paracrine (e.g. somatostatin decreasing secretin), autocrine (the cell acts on itself), contact-dependent (membrane-attached signals) and intracrine (e.g. steroids).

**Why:** Five pathways.
</details>

## 7. The five receptor mechanisms (what the 1st messenger does)

### [1] Opening or closing of ion channel-linked receptors
Conformational change of the **ion-gated channel protein attached to the receptor** → gate opens/closes → ↑ or ↓ **ion diffusion**.

### [2] Activation or inhibition of **G proteins**
- **G protein** = α, β, γ subunits with GDP; on activation **GDP → GTP exchange** and the **α subunit** moves laterally in the membrane to modulate:
  - **[A] G protein-linked ion channels** → gate opens, ions move, electrical properties change.
  - **[B] G protein-linked adenylate cyclase** → **ATP → cAMP** → **protein kinase** → phosphorylates proteins → response.
  - **[C] G protein-linked phospholipase C** → **PIP₂ → diacylglycerol (DAG) + IP₃**; **IP₃** releases **Ca²⁺ from the ER**; **DAG + Ca²⁺ → protein kinase C** → phosphorylation → response.

### [3] **Receptor-linked guanylyl cyclase** (catalysis-linked)
**GTP → cGMP → protein kinase G**; activated by **ANP/BNP** (membrane-bound) or **nitric oxide (NO)** (soluble guanylyl cyclase); **phosphodiesterase** breaks down cGMP.

### [4] **Receptor-linked tyrosine kinase** (catalysis-linked)
Hormone binds the receptor → **tyrosine kinase domain** activated.

### [5] **Gene modification**
Messenger (lipophilic) binds an intracellular receptor → **receptor conformation change → gene activation → more of a specific protein/enzyme**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Five mechanisms: ion channel-linked receptors, G proteins, guanylyl cyclase, tyrosine kinase, gene modification.
- G protein: GDP→GTP exchange; α subunit → channels, adenylate cyclase (cAMP → protein kinase) or phospholipase C (IP₃ → Ca²⁺ release; DAG → protein kinase C).
- Guanylyl cyclase: GTP → cGMP → protein kinase G (ANP, BNP, NO).
- Gene modification: intracellular receptor → ↑ protein synthesis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe how a G protein-coupled receptor transmits a signal.</summary>

**Answer:** The messenger binds the receptor, which activates the G protein (α, β, γ subunits with GDP): GDP is exchanged for GTP and the α-GTP subunit migrates in the membrane to modulate ion channels, adenylate cyclase or phospholipase C. With adenylate cyclase, ATP is converted to cAMP, which activates protein kinase and phosphorylates proteins; with phospholipase C, PIP₂ is split into DAG and IP₃, IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from the ER and DAG with Ca²⁺ activates protein kinase C.

**Why:** Three effector branches.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the guanylyl cyclase pathway.</summary>

**Answer:** Receptor-linked (membrane-bound, ANP/BNP) or soluble (activated by nitric oxide) guanylyl cyclase converts GTP into cGMP, which activates protein kinase G with many downstream effects; phosphodiesterase terminates the signal by degrading cGMP.

**Why:** GTP → cGMP → PKG.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does a lipophilic messenger alter cell function?</summary>

**Answer:** It enters the cell and binds a specific receptor in the cytosol or nucleus; the receptor changes conformation and modifies a gene, increasing the formation of a specific protein with enzymatic activity (gene modification).

**Why:** Slow, protein-synthesis-based response.
</details>

## 8. Regulation of intracellular Ca²⁺, H⁺, cell volume & cell death

### Intracellular Ca²⁺
- **Entry:** **voltage-gated**, **chemical-gated** and **physical-gated (stretch)** Ca²⁺ channels.
- Ca²⁺ binds **Ca-binding proteins (CaBP)** → effects.
- **Removal:** **uptake by mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum**; **extrusion** across the plasma membrane by **Ca²⁺-ATPase (ATP) pumps** and **3Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange**.

### Intracellular [H⁺]
- **To decrease [H⁺]ᵢ:** (1) **Na⁺-H⁺ exchange**; (2) **Na⁺-HCO₃⁻ cotransport**.
- **To increase [H⁺]ᵢ:** (3) **Cl⁻-HCO₃⁻ exchange**.
- **Intracellular buffering:** H₂O + CO₂ ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; CO₂ diffuses out.

### Cell volume regulation
- Depends on the **total intracellular solute** (Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients made by the **Na⁺-K⁺ pump**).
- **After shrinkage** → solute-increasing mechanisms: synthesis of **osmotically active molecules (sorbitol, taurine)**; ion uptake by **Na⁺-H⁺ exchanger** or **Na⁺-H⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter**.
- **After swelling** → solute-extruding mechanisms: **K⁺ or Cl⁻ channels**, **K⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter**.

### Apoptosis vs necrosis
- **Apoptosis** = **cell suicide** — **genetically determined**.
- **Necrosis** = **cell murder** — **external cause**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Ca²⁺ in via voltage-, chemical- and stretch-gated channels; out via Ca²⁺-ATPase and 3Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange; stored in mitochondria and ER.
- [H⁺]ᵢ ↓ by Na⁺-H⁺ exchange and Na⁺-HCO₃⁻ cotransport; ↑ by Cl⁻-HCO₃⁻ exchange.
- Shrinkage → sorbitol/taurine, Na⁺-H⁺ exchanger; swelling → K⁺/Cl⁻ channels, K⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter.
- Apoptosis = genetically programmed suicide; necrosis = killing by an external cause.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does the cell regulate its intracellular Ca²⁺?</summary>

**Answer:** Ca²⁺ enters through voltage-gated, chemical-gated and physical (stretch)-gated channels and binds Ca-binding proteins to produce effects. It is removed by uptake into mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum and by extrusion across the plasma membrane via Ca²⁺-ATPase pumps and the 3Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger.

**Why:** Entry routes + storage + extrusion.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is intracellular [H⁺] regulated?</summary>

**Answer:** It is decreased by Na⁺-H⁺ exchange and Na⁺-HCO₃⁻ cotransport and increased by Cl⁻-HCO₃⁻ exchange; the cell also buffers with the CO₂/H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ system (H₂O + CO₂ ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻) and CO₂ diffuses out.

**Why:** Two ways down, one way up, plus buffering.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate between apoptosis and necrosis; how does a cell regulate its volume after shrinkage and swelling?</summary>

**Answer:** Apoptosis is genetically determined cell suicide, necrosis is cell death caused by an external agent ("murder"). After shrinkage the cell increases intracellular solutes by synthesizing osmotically active molecules (sorbitol, taurine) and by Na⁺-H⁺ exchange or Na⁺-H⁺-Cl⁻ cotransport; after swelling it extrudes solutes through K⁺ or Cl⁻ channels or the K⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter.

**Why:** Two short comparisons.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- Membrane = **40% lipid, 60% protein** (not the reverse); cell **lipids 2%**.
- **Most cilia are non-motile** (sensory); motile cilia: respiratory tract + uterine tubes.
- **Gap junctions** = cytoplasmic channels (**connexin**, cardiac and smooth muscle); **desmosomes** = **cadherin**, skin and heart; **tight junctions** = **occludin**.
- **Collagen = non-elastic; elastin = elastic.**
- **Autocrine = acts on itself; paracrine = neighbouring cell; endocrine = distant via blood.**
- G protein activation = **GDP → GTP** exchange.
- **IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from the ER; DAG activates protein kinase C.**
- **Apoptosis = suicide (genetic); necrosis = murder (external).**
- **Na⁺-H⁺ exchange decreases [H⁺]ᵢ; Cl⁻-HCO₃⁻ exchange increases it.**

## 🔥 Final review — memorize before the exam

1. **Cell:** water 70–85%, proteins 10–20%, lipids 2%, carbohydrates 1%; body = 75–100 trillion cells.
2. **Membrane:** 40% lipid + 60% protein; five functions (shape, transport, communication, adhesion, directed movement).
3. **Channels:** specific; voltage-, ligand- and physically-gated.
4. **Glycocalyx:** repels negatives, adhesion, receptor, "self" markers.
5. **Cilia:** basal body; non-motile (ear, retina, kidney, nose) vs motile (airways, fallopian tubes).
6. **Adhesion:** CAMs, ECM (collagen, elastin), junctions (tight, gap, desmosome).
7. **Signaling:** electrical (voltage-gated channels) and chemical (endocrine, paracrine, autocrine, contact-dependent, intracrine).
8. **Receptor mechanisms:** ion channel-linked, G proteins (channels, adenylate cyclase/cAMP, PLC/IP₃-DAG), guanylyl cyclase/cGMP, tyrosine kinase, gene modification.
9. **Ca²⁺:** in via 3 channel types; out via Ca²⁺-ATPase + 3Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange; **H⁺:** Na⁺-H⁺ exchange, Na⁺-HCO₃⁻ cotransport (↓), Cl⁻-HCO₃⁻ exchange (↑).
10. **Apoptosis = suicide; necrosis = murder.**
