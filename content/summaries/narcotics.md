## 1. Introduction, receptors & classification

- **Analgesic** = a drug that relieves pain:
  - **Narcotic** — acts **centrally** (opioids).
  - **Non-narcotic** — acts **peripherally** (e.g. **aspirin**).
- **Narcotics** relieve pain and also cause **drowsiness and sedation**. **Prototype: morphine**, from the **opium poppy**.
- **Opioids** = all narcotic analgesics. **Opiates** = the **natural** opioids: **morphine and codeine**.

### Opioid receptors
- **Four families**: **mu (μ), kappa (κ), delta (δ), sigma (σ)** — each has different drug specificity.
- **Distribution**: CNS (**brain stem, thalamus, hypothalamus, limbic system, spinal cord**), the periphery (e.g. **GIT**) and **immune cells**.
- **Mechanism**: all are coupled to the **inhibitory G-protein (Gi)** → **↓ cAMP** and **cellular hyperpolarization**.

### Endogenous opioids (peptide neurotransmitters of the pain-inhibitory system)
1. **Enkephalins**
2. **Dynorphins A & B**
3. **Endorphins** (e.g. **β-endorphin**)

### Classification
| Class | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Strong agonists** | **morphine**, **pethidine (meperidine)**, **methadone**, **fentanyl**, **heroin (diamorphine)** |
| **Moderate agonists** | **codeine**, dextropropoxyphene, **diphenoxylate**, **loperamide**, **dextromethorphan** |
| **Partial agonists** | **pentazocine**, phenazocine |
| **Antagonists** | **naloxone**, **naltrexone**, **nalorphine**, **nalmefene** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Narcotic = central (opioids); non-narcotic = peripheral (aspirin). Morphine = prototype, from opium poppy.
- Opiates = natural opioids (morphine, codeine).
- Receptors μ, κ, δ, σ; all Gi-coupled → ↓cAMP, hyperpolarization; in CNS, GIT and immune cells.
- Endogenous: enkephalins, dynorphins A & B, endorphins (β-endorphin).
- Strong / moderate / partial agonists / antagonists — learn the table.

</div>

</details>

## 2. Morphine — effects on the CNS

### A. Depressant effects
1. **Analgesia** — raises the **spinal and supraspinal pain threshold** and **alters the brain's perception** of pain: the pain is **felt but no longer unpleasant**.
2. **Drowsiness** — hypnotic and tranquillizer effects.
3. **Respiratory depression** — ↓ rate and depth by **reducing the respiratory centre's sensitivity to CO₂** (overdose → **respiratory arrest**). ⚠️ Dangerous in **COPD and asthma**, where it also causes **bronchospasm by histamine release**.
4. **Cough-centre inhibition** — **antitussive**.
5. **Raised intracranial pressure** — from **CO₂ retention → cerebral vasodilation → ↑ CSF pressure** → **contraindicated in severe head injury**.

### B. Stimulant effects
1. **Miosis (pin-point pupil)** — via **μ and κ** stimulation of the **3rd nerve (oculomotor) nucleus** → ↑ parasympathetic drive to the eye.
   - ⭐ **Little tolerance** develops (even addicts have pin-point pupils).
   - ⭐ An important **diagnostic feature of coma** — **most other causes of coma dilate the pupil**.
2. **Emesis** — **40% nausea, 10% vomiting**, by **direct stimulation of the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ)**.

Also: **euphoria** and **dependence**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Depressant: analgesia (pain felt but not unpleasant), drowsiness, respiratory depression (↓ CO₂ sensitivity), antitussive, ↑ICP.
- Stimulant: miosis (μ, κ → 3rd nerve nucleus; little tolerance; diagnostic in coma), emesis (CTZ: 40% nausea, 10% vomiting).
- Dangerous in asthma/COPD (respiratory depression + histamine bronchospasm) and head injury (↑ICP).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the mechanism of morphine-induced respiratory depression and its clinical significance.</summary>

**Answer:** Morphine decreases the rate and depth of respiration by reducing the sensitivity of the respiratory centre to raised CO₂ tension; overdose causes respiratory arrest. It is dangerous in COPD and asthma, where it also causes bronchospasm through histamine release.

**Why:** Mechanism + danger groups.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is morphine contraindicated in head injury?</summary>

**Answer:** Because it raises intracranial pressure: respiratory depression causes CO₂ retention, which dilates cerebral vessels and increases CSF pressure. (It also causes miosis, which masks pupillary signs.)

**Why:** The CO₂ → vasodilation → ICP chain.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain morphine-induced miosis and its diagnostic importance.</summary>

**Answer:** Stimulation of μ and κ receptors excites the oculomotor (3rd nerve) nucleus, enhancing parasympathetic stimulation of the eye and producing pin-point pupils. Little tolerance develops, so even addicts show it. It is an important diagnostic feature in coma, because most other causes of coma dilate the pupil.

**Why:** Mechanism + tolerance + diagnostic value.
</details>

## 3. Morphine — smooth muscle & cardiovascular effects

### Smooth muscle (mostly spasm)
| Organ | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Stomach** | delays **gastric emptying**, ↓ motility and **HCl** |
| **Small intestine** | ↓ motility, **spasm of sphincters** |
| **Large intestine** | ↓ **peristalsis**, ↑ **tone**, ↓ secretions → **constipation** and ↑ **intrasigmoidal pressure** (contraindicated in **diverticular disease** and **intestinal obstruction**) |
| **Biliary** | **spasm of the sphincter of Oddi**, ↑ biliary pressure — may **worsen biliary colic**; reversed by **naloxone or GTN**, **not effectively by atropine** |
| **Bronchi** | **bronchospasm** |
| **Ureter** | spasm |
| **Bladder** | **sphincter spasm** → **urinary retention in BPH** |
| **Uterus** | **prolongs labour** (slower effective contractions) |

### Cardiovascular system
Unimportant in normal people, but ⭐ **useful in acute left ventricular failure (acute pulmonary oedema)** because it:
- relieves **mental distress** (tranquillizer),
- relieves **cardiac distress** (↓ **sympathetic drive**),
- relieves **respiratory distress** (makes the respiratory centre **insensitive to stimuli from the congested lungs**).

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Morphine-induced biliary spasm is reversed by **naloxone or GTN (nitroglycerin)** — **not** by atropine.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- GIT: delayed gastric emptying, constipation, ↑ intrasigmoidal pressure (avoid in diverticular disease, obstruction).
- Biliary: sphincter of Oddi spasm (reverse with naloxone/GTN, not atropine).
- Bronchospasm, ureteric spasm, urinary retention (BPH), prolonged labour.
- CVS: beneficial in acute LVF — mental, cardiac and respiratory distress relieved.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the effects of morphine on the gastrointestinal tract.</summary>

**Answer:** In the stomach it delays gastric emptying and decreases motility and HCl secretion. In the small intestine it decreases motility and causes spasm of sphincters. In the large intestine it decreases peristalsis, increases tone and decreases secretions. The result is constipation and raised intrasigmoidal pressure, so it is contraindicated in diverticular disease and intestinal obstruction.

**Why:** Organ by organ, then the consequence.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is morphine useful in acute left ventricular failure?</summary>

**Answer:** It relieves mental distress through its tranquillizer effect, relieves cardiac distress by decreasing sympathetic drive, and relieves respiratory distress by rendering the respiratory centre insensitive to stimuli from the congested lungs.

**Why:** Three types of distress relieved.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the effect of morphine on the biliary system and how is it reversed?</summary>

**Answer:** It causes spasm of the sphincter of Oddi and raises intrabiliary pressure, which may worsen biliary colic. This is reversed by naloxone or GTN but not effectively by atropine.

**Why:** The reversal agents are the tested detail.
</details>

## 4. Morphine — pharmacokinetics, uses, adverse effects & contraindications

### Pharmacokinetics
- **Slow oral absorption** + **significant first-pass elimination** → usually given **IM, IV or SC**; also **buccal/sublingual** (dose equal to IM) and **transdermal patch**; crude opioid is inhaled for non-medical use (rapid onset).
- Enters all tissues and **crosses the placenta** → **respiratory depression in the newborn**.
- **t½ = 2 h**; **duration 4–6 h**.
- Metabolized (liver, kidney) to **morphine-6-glucuronide (active)** and **morphine-3-glucuronide (inactive)**; excreted in **urine** (small amount in bile).

### Clinical uses
1. **Severe pain** — **visceral (e.g. MI)** or **somatic (wounds, fractures)**. (Aspirin only for mild–moderate somatic pain.)
2. **Anxiety** in serious, frightening conditions (accidents, haemorrhage) — give **after fluid replacement**.
3. **Dyspnoea of acute LVF** (pulmonary oedema).
4. **Euphoria and pain relief in the dying**.
5. **Premedication** for surgery (GA & LA).
6. **Non-serious diarrhoea** (e.g. travellers') — **codeine and loperamide preferred**.
7. **Cough suppression** — **codeine preferred**.

### Adverse effects
**Respiratory depression**, **vomiting**, **dysphoria** (withdrawal), **hypotension**, **urinary retention (BPH)**, **pruritus** (itching around the nose).

### Contraindications
1. **CNS**: **head injury** (↑ICP), **acute alcoholism**, **convulsive disorders**.
2. **GIT**: **diverticulitis**, **pancreatitis**, **biliary infections**, **intestinal obstruction**.
3. **Respiratory**: **asthma, COPD**.
4. **Severe liver disease** (risk of **encephalopathy**).
5. **Labour** (avoid).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- High first-pass → parenteral; crosses placenta; t½ 2 h, duration 4–6 h; M6G active, M3G inactive.
- Uses: severe visceral/somatic pain, anxiety (after fluids), acute LVF, dying patients, premedication; codeine/loperamide preferred for diarrhoea and cough.
- Adverse: respiratory depression, vomiting, dysphoria, hypotension, urinary retention, pruritus.
- Contraindications: head injury, alcoholism, convulsions, diverticulitis, pancreatitis, biliary infection, obstruction, asthma/COPD, severe liver disease, labour.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the pharmacokinetics of morphine.</summary>

**Answer:** Oral absorption is slow and there is significant first-pass elimination, so it is usually given IM, IV or SC (also buccal/sublingual at the IM dose, and transdermal). It enters all tissues and crosses the placenta, causing neonatal respiratory depression. Its half-life is 2 hours and duration of action 4–6 hours. It is metabolized in the liver and kidney to active morphine-6-glucuronide and inactive morphine-3-glucuronide, which are excreted mainly in urine.

**Why:** Absorption, distribution, half-life, metabolism, excretion.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the clinical uses of morphine.</summary>

**Answer:** Relief of severe visceral (MI) or somatic pain; relief of anxiety in serious conditions such as accidents or haemorrhage (after fluid replacement); relief of dyspnoea in acute left ventricular failure; euphoria and pain relief in the dying; premedication for surgery; symptomatic control of non-serious diarrhoea and cough suppression (codeine and loperamide preferred).

**Why:** Seven uses.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Enumerate the contraindications of morphine.</summary>

**Answer:** CNS: head injury, acute alcoholism, convulsive disorders. GIT: diverticulitis, pancreatitis, biliary infections, intestinal obstruction. Respiratory: asthma and COPD. Severe liver disease (encephalopathy). It should be avoided during labour.

**Why:** Group by system — each links to an effect you already learned.
</details>

## 5. Tolerance, addiction & poisoning

### Tolerance and dependence
- Tolerance develops mainly to **respiratory depression, analgesia, euphoria, sedation** — and to **nausea and vomiting**.
- ⭐ **No (little) tolerance to the stimulant actions** such as **miosis** (and constipation).
- **Cross-tolerance** with other CNS depressants: **barbiturates and alcohol**.

### Treatment of addiction
1. **Withdraw morphine gradually**.
2. **Substitute with methadone** — its **long t½ (48 h)** gives a **mild withdrawal syndrome**.
3. **Clonidine** reduces withdrawal severity by damping **noradrenaline hyperactivity**; sometimes **β-blockers (propranolol)**.

### Morphine poisoning (overdose)
- ⭐ Triad: **coma, slow shallow respiration, pin-point pupils**; circulation is maintained until hypoxia is advanced and cyanosis appears.
- **Treatment**: support respiration and circulation, and give **naloxone IV** — a **pure antagonist** that is also a **diagnostic test** for opioid-induced coma.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Tolerance to respiratory depression, analgesia, euphoria, sedation, nausea — not to miosis.
- Cross-tolerance with barbiturates and alcohol.
- Addiction: gradual withdrawal, methadone (t½ 48 h), clonidine ± propranolol.
- Poisoning: coma + slow shallow breathing + pin-point pupils → support + IV naloxone (also diagnostic).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe tolerance to morphine.</summary>

**Answer:** Tolerance develops mainly to respiratory depression, analgesia, euphoria and sedation, and also to nausea and vomiting, but not to the stimulant actions such as miosis. There is cross-tolerance between opioids and other CNS depressants such as barbiturates and alcohol.

**Why:** Which effects develop tolerance and which don't.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is morphine addiction treated?</summary>

**Answer:** By gradual withdrawal of morphine; substitution with methadone, whose long half-life (48 h) gives only a mild withdrawal syndrome; and clonidine (sometimes with propranolol) to reduce withdrawal severity by decreasing noradrenaline hyperactivity.

**Why:** Three measures.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the features and treatment of morphine poisoning.</summary>

**Answer:** Coma, slow and shallow respiration and pin-point pupils; circulation is maintained until hypoxia is advanced and cyanosis occurs. Treatment is support of respiration and circulation plus IV naloxone, a pure opioid antagonist that also serves as a diagnostic test for opioid-induced coma.

**Why:** Classic triad + antidote.
</details>

## 6. Individual opioids

| Drug | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Pethidine (meperidine)** | less efficacy than morphine but beyond codeine; **structurally unrelated** to morphine but shares its effects and is **reversed by naloxone**; **t½ 3 h**, given by injection; metabolite **norpethidine** → **excitement/confusion** in poisoning. Uses: **post-operative pain**, **pre-operative medication**, **1st stage of labour** |
| **Methadone** | structurally related to morphine; **oral**; less sedation and euphoria; ⭐ **t½ 48 h**, analgesia **24 h**; milder dependence/withdrawal → **used to treat morphine and heroin dependence**; also analgesia and cough |
| **Heroin (diamorphine)** | **semi-synthetic**, made by **acetylation of morphine**; ⭐ **the most powerful dependence-producing drug** |
| **Fentanyl** | used in **general anaesthesia** |
| **Codeine** | **methyl-morphine**; natural; **t½ 3 h**; only **10% converted to morphine** → **1/10 of its action**; **no efficacy in severe pain**; used for **cough** and short-term **acute diarrhoea**; combined with aspirin/paracetamol |
| **Dextropropoxyphene** | **t½ 12 h**; analgesia **equal to codeine**; combined with aspirin/paracetamol |
| **Diphenoxylate, loperamide** | **antidiarrhoeals** |
| **Dextromethorphan** | **cough suppressant** |
| **Pentazocine** (partial agonist) | analgesia **equal to morphine** but **less euphoria**; ⭐ **precipitates withdrawal in morphine addicts**; causes psychological and physical dependence; reversed by naloxone |
| **Tramadol** | **t½ 6 h**; binds **μ** receptors + **inhibits noradrenaline uptake & serotonin release**; as effective as **pethidine** for post-op pain; **less constipation, addiction and respiratory depression**; may cause **convulsions, hallucinations, anaphylaxis** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pethidine: t½ 3 h, norpethidine excitement; post-op pain, premedication, labour.
- Methadone: oral, t½ 48 h, treats opioid dependence.
- Heroin = acetylated morphine, most addictive. Fentanyl = general anaesthesia.
- Codeine: 10% → morphine (1/10 action), cough/diarrhoea, useless in severe pain.
- Pentazocine: partial agonist, precipitates withdrawal in addicts. Tramadol: μ + NA/5-HT, convulsions.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare pethidine and methadone.</summary>

**Answer:** Pethidine is structurally unrelated to morphine, less effective than morphine, with t½ 3 h, given by injection; its metabolite norpethidine causes excitement and confusion. It is used for post-operative pain, premedication and the first stage of labour. Methadone is structurally related to morphine, given orally, causes less sedation and euphoria, has t½ 48 h with 24-hour analgesia, and because its withdrawal is mild it is used to treat morphine and heroin dependence.

**Why:** A popular comparison.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is codeine weaker than morphine and what is it used for?</summary>

**Answer:** Codeine (methyl-morphine) is only about 10% converted to morphine in the body, so its action is about one-tenth of morphine's and it lacks efficacy in severe pain. It is used for cough suppression and short-term control of acute diarrhoea, usually combined with aspirin or paracetamol.

**Why:** The 10% conversion explains everything.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe tramadol.</summary>

**Answer:** Tramadol (t½ 6 h) binds μ receptors to cause analgesia and also inhibits neuronal noradrenaline uptake and serotonin release. It is as effective as pethidine for post-operative pain and less likely to cause constipation, addiction and respiratory depression, but may cause convulsions, hallucinations and probably anaphylaxis.

**Why:** Dual mechanism + advantages + adverse effects.
</details>

## 7. Opioid antagonists

| Drug | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Naloxone** | **pure competitive antagonist** (reverses agonists and partial agonists); ⭐ **IV only** (high pre-systemic metabolism); reverses respiratory depression in **1–2 min**, lasts only **1 h** → **repeated doses or infusion** needed; **precipitates withdrawal** in addicts. Uses: **opioid overdose**, **diagnosis** of opioid coma, reversing opioids after **surgery** and in the **newborn after childbirth**, **septic shock** |
| **Naltrexone** | like naloxone but **longer** (t½ 4 h; active metabolites t½ 13 h → **2–3 days**); ⭐ **given orally**; also **reduces alcohol craving** |
| **Nalorphine** | antagonist with **partial agonist** effect — a weak agonist in normal people, an antagonist in addicts |
| **Nalmefene** | **newest**; derivative of naltrexone; **IV only**; **longer t½ than naloxone** |

> ⚠️ **Exam trap:** Naloxone acts for only **1 hour**, shorter than most opioids → the patient can **re-narcotize**, so give **repeated doses or an infusion**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Naloxone: pure antagonist, IV only, 1–2 min onset, 1 h duration → repeat; overdose, diagnosis, post-op, newborn, septic shock.
- Naltrexone: oral, long-acting (2–3 days), reduces alcohol craving.
- Nalorphine: partial agonist/antagonist. Nalmefene: newest, IV, longer than naloxone.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe naloxone and its uses.</summary>

**Answer:** Naloxone is a pure competitive opioid antagonist that antagonizes agonists and partial agonists. It is given IV only because of high pre-systemic metabolism; it reverses respiratory depression within 1–2 minutes but lasts only 1 hour, so repeated doses or an infusion are needed. It precipitates withdrawal in addicts. Uses: opioid overdose, diagnosis of opioid-induced coma, countering opioid effects after anaesthesia and in the newborn after childbirth, and septic shock.

**Why:** Mechanism, route, timing, uses.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare naloxone and naltrexone.</summary>

**Answer:** Both are opioid antagonists. Naloxone is given IV only and acts for about 1 hour. Naltrexone has a longer half-life (4 h, with active metabolites of 13 h giving 2–3 days' action), can be given orally, and is also used to reduce craving for alcohol in chronic alcoholics.

**Why:** Route + duration + extra use.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are repeated doses of naloxone needed in morphine overdose?</summary>

**Answer:** Because naloxone's duration of action (about 1 hour) is much shorter than that of the opioid, so the patient may relapse into respiratory depression; repeated IV doses or an IV infusion are therefore required.

**Why:** A classic "why" question.
</details>
