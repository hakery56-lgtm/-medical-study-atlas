## 1. Practical 1 (A): expiry date, names and tablets

### Practical task
- Students fill a table for each formulation: **trade name, generic name, dosage form, strength, expiry date, indications**.

### Q1 — Expiry date and shelf-life
- ⭐ **Expiry date** = the date until which the manufacturer **guarantees full potency and safety**, **if unopened and stored correctly**.
- **Opened formulations** (eye drops, insulin vials) have **reduced stability** → **discard earlier** (e.g. **28 days for many eye drops**).
- ⭐ **Shelf-life** = the period during which the drug remains **stable, safe and effective under recommended storage** (= time between **manufacturing date and expiry date**).

### Q2 — Trade vs generic name
| **Brand (trade/proprietary)** | **Generic (official/non-proprietary)** |
|---|---|
| used for **marketing** | scientific description of the **API** |
| given by the **manufacturing company** | given by official bodies (**WHO, USAN**) |
| carries **TM or ®** (Panadol) | written in **lowercase** (paracetamol) |
| **short, easy to remember** (Voltaren) | often **long, based on chemical name** (diclofenac sodium) |
| **multiple names** for one drug | **one only** per drug, **same worldwide** |
| **owned by the company** | **public property** |
| **more expensive** | **lower cost** |

### Q3 — Tablet splitting
- **No — only SCORED tablets (with a line)** are designed to be split; **unscored, coated or modified-release tablets must not be broken** (alters absorption and safety).

### Q4 — Release types
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Extended/prolonged** | general term: **slow release over time** |
| ⭐ **Sustained release (SR)** | **nearly constant rate** for a prolonged period (**not predictable**) |
| ⭐ **Controlled release (CR)** | **precise, predictable constant rate** |
| ⭐ **Modified release** | **broad term**: any formulation altering **time/place/rate** of release (delayed, extended…) |

### Q5 — Tablet coats
- **Sugar coat** (**taste masking, appearance**), **film coat** (**thin polymer layer; improves swallowing and stability**), **enteric coat** (**resists stomach acid, dissolves in the intestine**).

### Q6 — Enteric-coated tablets
- **Must NOT be chewed** — chewing destroys the coating → **drug inactivation or stomach irritation**. Example: **enteric-coated aspirin**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Expiry date = guaranteed potency/safety if unopened; opened eye drops/insulin expire sooner (28 days). Shelf-life = stability period between manufacture and expiry.
- Brand = marketing, TM/®, expensive; generic = WHO/USAN name, lowercase, same worldwide, cheaper.
- Only scored tablets can be split. SR = nearly constant; CR = predictable; modified = any altered release.
- Coats: sugar, film, enteric. Don't chew enteric-coated aspirin.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define expiry date and shelf-life and the effect of opening a formulation.</summary>

**Answer:** The expiry date is the date until which the manufacturer guarantees potency and safety if the product is unopened and properly stored; shelf-life is the period (from manufacture to expiry) during which the drug stays stable, safe and effective. Opened products such as eye drops or insulin vials have reduced stability and must be discarded earlier (e.g. 28 days for many eye drops).

**Why:** Practical 1, Q1.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate trade (brand) from generic names.</summary>

**Answer:** The brand name is given by the manufacturing company for marketing, carries ™ or ®, is short and memorable (Panadol, Voltaren), is proprietary and costs more, with several brands per drug; the generic name is the official non-proprietary name given by WHO/USAN, written in lowercase (paracetamol, diclofenac sodium), unique for each drug and the same worldwide, public property and cheaper.

**Why:** Comparison table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Can all tablets be split and what are the tablet coats?</summary>

**Answer:** No; only scored tablets can be split, while unscored, coated or modified-release tablets must not be broken because absorption and safety change. Coats: sugar (taste, appearance), film (swallowing, stability) and enteric (acid-resistant, dissolves in the intestine); enteric-coated tablets such as aspirin must not be chewed.

**Why:** Q3, Q5, Q6.
</details>

## 2. Practical 1 (B): other dosage forms

### Q7 — Pessaries vs suppositories
- ⭐ **Pessaries** = solid dosage forms inserted into the **vagina** (e.g. antifungal pessaries for candidiasis).
- ⭐ **Suppositories** = solid dosage forms inserted into the **rectum** (e.g. glycerin suppositories for constipation).

### Q8 — Ointment, cream, gel, lotion
| Form | Features |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Ointment** | **oil-based, greasy, occlusive**, good for **dry skin** |
| ⭐ **Cream** | **water + oil emulsion**, **less greasy**, good for **moist lesions** |
| ⭐ **Gel** | **water/alcohol base, transparent, cooling, quick absorption** |
| ⭐ **Lotion** | **liquid**, easy to apply on **large or hairy areas** |

### Q9–Q10 — Ampule vs vial; breaking an ampule
- ⭐ **Ampule:** **sealed glass container, single dose; must be broken to open.**
- ⭐ **Vial:** **glass/plastic container with a rubber stopper; single- or multi-dose.**
- **Breaking an ampule:** **tap the top** to bring the liquid down from the neck → **wrap the neck** with an alcohol swab/gauze → **snap the neck away from your body** at the scored line or marked dot.

### Q11 — Why does a 500 mg paracetamol tablet weigh 600–650 mg?
- Because tablets contain **excipients** (**binders, fillers, disintegrants, lubricants, coatings**) in addition to the active drug.

### Q12 — Syrup vs elixir
- ⭐ **Syrup:** **sweet, viscous, water-based solution**, often for **children**, **no or very little alcohol**.
- ⭐ **Elixir:** **clear hydro-alcoholic solution**, **less viscous**, a **better solvent** for some drugs.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pessary = vaginal; suppository = rectal.
- Ointment (greasy, dry skin), cream (less greasy, moist lesions), gel (transparent, cooling), lotion (liquid, large/hairy areas).
- Ampule = sealed glass, single dose, snap neck; vial = rubber stopper, single or multi-dose.
- Tablet weight > active dose because of excipients. Syrup (sweet, water-based) vs elixir (hydro-alcoholic, clear).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate pessaries from suppositories and ampules from vials.</summary>

**Answer:** Pessaries are solid forms inserted into the vagina (antifungal pessaries); suppositories are inserted into the rectum (glycerin for constipation). An ampule is a sealed single-dose glass container that must be broken to open; a vial is a glass or plastic container with a rubber stopper that can be single- or multi-dose.

**Why:** Q7 and Q9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare ointment, cream, gel and lotion.</summary>

**Answer:** Ointment: oil-based, greasy and occlusive, for dry skin. Cream: water–oil emulsion, less greasy, for moist lesions. Gel: water/alcohol base, transparent, cooling and quickly absorbed. Lotion: liquid, easy to apply over large or hairy areas.

**Why:** Q8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do you break an ampule, why does paracetamol 500 mg weigh 600–650 mg, and what is the difference between syrup and elixir?</summary>

**Answer:** Tap the top to bring the liquid down, wrap the neck with a swab or gauze and snap it away from the body at the scored line or dot. The tablet contains excipients (binders, fillers, disintegrants, lubricants, coatings). Syrup is a sweet viscous water-based solution (often for children, little or no alcohol); elixir is a clear hydro-alcoholic, less viscous solution that dissolves some drugs better.

**Why:** Q10–Q12.
</details>

## 3. Practical 2 (A): oral powders, inhalers, nebulizers, vials

### 1 — Dissolving a powder for oral use
- Use the **recommended solvent** (clean, preferably **boiled and cooled** water; milk/juice only if permitted); **add gradually while stirring/shaking** until dissolved/suspended; **use immediately and keep in the fridge** (reconstituted powders are often unstable).

### 2 — Proper inhaler technique
1. **Shake** the inhaler well. 2. **Exhale fully.** 3. Mouthpiece in the mouth, **lips sealed**. 4. **Press the canister once while breathing in slowly and deeply.** 5. **Hold your breath ~10 seconds.** 6. **Exhale slowly.** 7. **Rinse the mouth after steroid inhalers** (prevents fungal infection).

### 3 — Spacer
- A **tube/chamber attached to the inhaler**; **improves delivery to the lungs**, **reduces mouth/throat deposition**, helps **children and elderly** who can't coordinate pressing and inhaling.

### 4 — Nebulizer
- A device that **converts liquid medication into a fine mist** for inhalation via a **mask or mouthpiece**; for patients unable to use inhalers (**children, severe asthma/COPD attacks, hospitalized patients**).

### 5 & 6 — Ampoule and vial powder
- **Break an ampoule** (tap, wrap, snap away).
- **Dissolve powder in a vial:** **clean the rubber stopper with alcohol**, **withdraw sterile water or saline into a syringe**, **inject the diluent slowly**, **swirl gently (don't shake vigorously)**, **withdraw the reconstituted solution**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Oral powder: recommended solvent, add gradually, use immediately/refrigerate.
- Inhaler: shake, exhale, seal lips, press once while inhaling slowly, hold ~10 s, exhale slowly; rinse mouth after steroids.
- Spacer improves lung delivery (children, elderly); nebulizer = fine mist via mask for severe asthma/COPD, children.
- Vial: clean stopper, inject diluent slowly, swirl gently.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the proper use of an inhaler.</summary>

**Answer:** Shake it, exhale fully, seal the lips around the mouthpiece, press the canister once while breathing in slowly and deeply, hold the breath for about 10 seconds, exhale slowly, and rinse the mouth after steroid inhalers to prevent fungal infection.

**Why:** Practical 2, Q2.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are a spacer and a nebulizer and when are they used?</summary>

**Answer:** A spacer is a chamber attached to an inhaler that improves lung delivery, reduces mouth and throat deposition and helps children and the elderly who can't coordinate pressing and inhaling. A nebulizer converts liquid medication into a fine mist for inhalation by mask or mouthpiece in patients who cannot use inhalers (children, severe asthma/COPD attacks, hospitalized patients).

**Why:** Q3 and Q4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do you dissolve a drug powder in a vial?</summary>

**Answer:** Clean the rubber stopper with an alcohol swab, draw sterile water or saline into a syringe, inject it slowly into the vial, swirl gently (do not shake vigorously unless instructed) and withdraw the reconstituted solution.

**Why:** Q6.
</details>

## 4. Practical 2 (B): irritants, depot, suppositories, insulin, drops

- ⭐ **7 — Irritant drug orally:** give **after meals**, with **plenty of water or milk (if appropriate)**, some need **enteric coating**, patient **upright before and after swallowing**.
- ⭐ **8 — Irritant drug IM:** **deep IM into a large muscle (gluteus, vastus lateralis)** using the **Z-track technique** (pull skin sideways, inject, release) to **prevent leakage into subcutaneous tissue and irritation**.
- ⭐ **9 — Depot preparation:** formulation for **slow release over days to weeks**; **injected IM or SC**; **slow absorption, long duration, fewer doses** (**antipsychotics, contraceptives**).
- ⭐ **10 — Storing a suppository at home:** **cool place or refrigerator (not freezer)**, **tip kept down**, **protect from heat** (melting).
- ⭐ **11 — Insulin:** **subcutaneous injection (abdomen, thigh, upper arm), rotate sites**; **yes, patients can self-inject** after training (syringes or pens).
- ⭐ **12 — Drops:**
  - **Eye:** tilt head back, **pull lower eyelid**, instill a drop, **close the eye gently**, **avoid touching the dropper tip**.
  - **Nasal:** tilt head back slightly, instill into the nostril, **sniff gently**.
  - **Ear:** **adults: pull the pinna UP and BACK; children: pull DOWN and BACK**; instill and keep the head tilted for a few minutes.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Irritant orally: after meals, plenty of water, upright; irritant IM: deep, large muscle, Z-track.
- Depot: slow release for days–weeks, IM/SC (antipsychotics, contraceptives).
- Suppository: cool/fridge (not freezer), tip down. Insulin: SC, rotate sites, self-injection possible.
- Ear drops: adults up and back; children down and back.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How do you give an irritant drug orally and intramuscularly?</summary>

**Answer:** Orally: after meals with plenty of water or milk, sometimes in an enteric-coated form, with the patient upright before and after swallowing. IM: deep injection into a large muscle (gluteus or vastus lateralis) using the Z-track technique to prevent leakage into subcutaneous tissue and irritation.

**Why:** Q7 and Q8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is a depot preparation?</summary>

**Answer:** A formulation designed for slow release over a prolonged period (days to weeks), given IM or SC, with slow absorption, long duration of action and less frequent dosing (e.g. antipsychotics, contraceptives).

**Why:** Q9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do you store suppositories, give insulin and instill ear drops?</summary>

**Answer:** Suppositories: in a cool place or the refrigerator (not the freezer), tip down, away from heat. Insulin: subcutaneously in the abdomen, thigh or upper arm, rotating sites; patients can self-inject after training. Ear drops: pull the pinna up and back in adults and down and back in children, instill and keep the head tilted for a few minutes.

**Why:** Q10–Q12.
</details>
