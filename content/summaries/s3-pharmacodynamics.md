## 2. Pharmacodynamics and the targets of drug action

- **Pharmacodynamics** = the actions of a drug on the body.
- **Drugs act as signals; their targets (e.g. receptors) act as signal detectors.**
- **Drug + receptor ⇄ drug–receptor complex → biological effect.**

### Targets for drug action
| Target | Example |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Receptors** (most common and most important) | adrenoceptors, nicotinic receptor |
| **Enzymes** | **cyclooxygenase** inhibited by **aspirin** |
| **Transport (carrier) proteins** | **hemicholinium** blocks **choline re-uptake** |
| **Ion channels** | **lidocaine** blocks **Na⁺ channels** |

### What is a receptor?
- Any **cellular macromolecule (usually a protein)** to which a drug binds to initiate its effects.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pharmacodynamics = drug → body. Drugs = signals, targets = detectors.
- Targets: receptors (most important), enzymes (aspirin/COX), carrier proteins (hemicholinium), ion channels (lidocaine/Na⁺).
- Receptor = usually a protein to which the drug binds.

</div>

## 3. The four types of receptors

| Type | Example | Intracellular effect | Speed / duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Ligand-gated ion channels** | **cholinergic nicotinic**, **GABA** receptors | change in **membrane potential / ionic concentration** | **very rapid, milliseconds** |
| **B. G protein-coupled receptors (GPCR)** | **α and β adrenoceptors** | **protein phosphorylation** (via second messengers) | **seconds to minutes**; **most abundant** type |
| **C. Enzyme-linked receptors** | **insulin** receptor (also EGF, PDGF, ANP) | protein and receptor **phosphorylation** (e.g. **tyrosine kinase**) | **minutes to hours** |
| **D. Intracellular (nuclear) receptors** | **steroid** receptors (thyroid hormone, vitamins D and A) | altered **gene expression** | **30 min or more**; effect lasts **hours to days** |

### Details
- **Ligand-gated ion channels:** regulate the **flow of ions across the membrane**.
- **GPCR:** a **single α-helical peptide with 7 membrane-spanning regions**; **extracellular domain = ligand-binding area**; linked intracellularly to a **G protein effector** (second messenger).
- **Enzyme-linked receptors:** span the membrane **once**, may form **dimers or multi-subunit complexes**, and have **cytosolic enzyme activity** (e.g. tyrosine kinase) as part of their structure.
- **Intracellular receptors:** the receptor is **entirely intracellular**, so the ligand must be **highly lipid-soluble**; targets are **transcription factors** (DNA/RNA).

### Second messengers of GPCRs
| G protein | Enzyme effect | Second messenger |
|---|---|---|
| **Gs** | **activates adenylyl cyclase** | **↑ cAMP** (regulates protein phosphorylation) |
| **Gi** | **inhibits adenylyl cyclase** | **↓ cAMP** |
| **Gq** | **activates phospholipase C** | **IP₃** (regulates intracellular free Ca²⁺) + **DAG** (activates **protein kinase C**) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 4 families: ligand-gated channel (ms), GPCR (sec–min, most abundant), enzyme-linked (min–hours), nuclear (≥30 min, hours–days).
- GPCR: 7 transmembrane α-helices; Gs ↑cAMP, Gi ↓cAMP, Gq → PLC → IP₃ + DAG.
- Nuclear receptor ligands must be lipid-soluble (steroids, thyroid hormone, vitamins A and D).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify receptors and give an example and the time course of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Ligand-gated ion channels (nicotinic, GABA; milliseconds); G protein-coupled receptors (α and β adrenoceptors; seconds to minutes); enzyme-linked receptors (insulin; minutes to hours); intracellular/nuclear receptors (steroid hormones; ≥30 minutes with effects lasting hours to days).

**Why:** The receptor table of the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the G protein second-messenger systems.</summary>

**Answer:** Gs activates adenylyl cyclase and increases cAMP; Gi inhibits adenylyl cyclase and decreases cAMP; Gq activates phospholipase C, producing IP₃ (which regulates intracellular free calcium) and DAG (which activates protein kinase C).

**Why:** Second messengers amplify signals from GPCRs.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why must the ligand of an intracellular receptor be lipid-soluble, and why is the response slow?</summary>

**Answer:** The receptor is inside the cell, so the ligand must diffuse across the membrane; the ligand–receptor complex acts on transcription factors (DNA/RNA), so a response is seen only after 30 minutes or more and lasts hours to days.

**Why:** Gene expression takes time.
</details>

## 4. Signal transduction, potency and efficacy

### Characteristics of signal transduction
1. **Amplification of small signals** — only a **fraction of receptors** may need to be activated to give a maximal response. Such systems have **spare receptors** (≈ **99 % of insulin receptors are spare**).
2. **Protection of the cell from excessive stimulation** (**desensitization** and **down-regulation**). Repeated dosing with a diminishing effect = ⭐ **tachyphylaxis**; the receptor needs a **rest (refractory) period** before it can be activated again.

### Potency vs efficacy
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Potency** | the **amount of drug needed to produce an effect of a given strength**; measured by the **EC₅₀** (concentration giving **50 % of the maximum effect**) |
| ⭐ **Efficacy** | the **ability of a drug to give a response when it binds the receptor**; the **maximal response = Emax** |

- **Efficacy (Emax) is more important than potency.**
- Figure: **Drug A is more potent than Drug B** (lower EC₅₀) but **both have the same efficacy**; **Drug C has lower potency and lower efficacy** than A and B.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Spare receptors → amplification (99 % of insulin receptors).
- Tachyphylaxis = diminished effect with repeated doses (desensitization); receptor is refractory during recovery.
- Potency = EC₅₀; efficacy = Emax; efficacy matters more than potency.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are spare receptors? Give an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Receptors in excess of those needed for a maximal response; only a fraction of them has to be occupied. About 99 % of insulin receptors are spare.

**Why:** This is how cells amplify small signals.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define tachyphylaxis.</summary>

**Answer:** A diminished response after repeated administration of a drug because the receptor becomes desensitized; it needs a recovery (refractory) period before it can be activated again.

**Why:** A protective mechanism against over-stimulation.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate between potency and efficacy using Drugs A, B and C.</summary>

**Answer:** Potency is the dose needed for an effect of a given strength (EC₅₀); efficacy is the maximal response (Emax). A is more potent than B (lower EC₅₀) but they have the same efficacy; C has both lower potency and lower efficacy. Efficacy is clinically more important.

**Why:** A potent drug is not necessarily a more effective one.
</details>

## 5. Agonists, antagonists and the therapeutic index

### Agonists (bind and produce a biologic response)
| Type | Features | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Full agonist** | **maximal response**, strong affinity + maximal efficacy | **phenylephrine** (α₁) |
| **Partial agonist** | efficacy **> 0 but < full agonist**; affinity may be greater, less or equal; may act as an **antagonist of a full agonist** | **aripiprazole** (atypical neuroleptic) |
| **Inverse agonist** | binds the same receptor but gives the **opposite response**; **negative efficacy (< 0 %)** | **β-carbolines** on **BDZ** receptors |

### Antagonists (decrease or oppose the action of another drug or ligand; **no effect if no agonist is present**)
1. **Pharmacological antagonism**
   - **Competitive:** agonist and antagonist compete for the **same site** (e.g. **histamine and loratadine**); can be overcome by more agonist.
   - **Irreversible:** **cannot be overcome by adding agonist**; by **covalent binding** or binding to an **allosteric site**.
2. **Functional (physiologic) antagonism** — acts at a **separate receptor** producing the opposite effect (e.g. **histamine H₁** causes bronchoconstriction, **epinephrine at β₂** causes bronchial relaxation).
3. **Chemical antagonism** — modifies or **sequesters the agonist** so it cannot bind (e.g. **protamine sulfate** vs **heparin**).

### Therapeutic index
- A **measure of drug safety**: a **larger value = wide margin** between effective and toxic doses.
- **Warfarin** = **small** therapeutic index; **Penicillin** = **large** therapeutic index.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Full agonist (phenylephrine), partial agonist (aripiprazole; can act as antagonist), inverse agonist (negative efficacy; β-carbolines on BDZ).
- Antagonists: competitive (loratadine), irreversible (covalent/allosteric), functional (epinephrine vs histamine in bronchi), chemical (protamine vs heparin).
- Therapeutic index = safety margin; warfarin small, penicillin large.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare full, partial and inverse agonists.</summary>

**Answer:** A full agonist produces the maximal response with high affinity and maximal efficacy (phenylephrine). A partial agonist has efficacy above zero but below the full agonist and may act as its antagonist (aripiprazole). An inverse agonist binds the same receptor but produces the opposite effect, with negative efficacy (β-carbolines on BDZ receptors).

**Why:** They differ in intrinsic activity.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Classify antagonists with an example of each.</summary>

**Answer:** Competitive (loratadine vs histamine); irreversible (covalent or allosteric binding, not overcome by more agonist); functional/physiologic (epinephrine at β₂ relaxes bronchi against histamine H₁ bronchoconstriction); chemical (protamine sulfate sequesters heparin).

**Why:** Each differs in the mechanism by which it opposes the agonist.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the therapeutic index and which drug has a small one?</summary>

**Answer:** A measure of safety; a large index means a wide gap between effective and toxic doses. Warfarin has a small therapeutic index; penicillin has a large one.

**Why:** Warfarin needs careful monitoring.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Potency = EC₅₀ (dose); efficacy = Emax (maximal response)** — A can be more potent than B yet equally efficacious.
- **Gs ↑cAMP; Gi ↓cAMP; Gq → IP₃ + DAG.**
- **Speed:** ligand-gated (ms) < GPCR (sec–min) < enzyme-linked (min–hours) < nuclear (≥30 min; hours–days).
- **Competitive antagonist can be overcome by more agonist; irreversible cannot.**
- **Functional antagonist acts on a different receptor; chemical antagonist binds the agonist itself.**
- **Inverse agonist ≠ antagonist** (it gives an opposite effect; an antagonist has no effect alone).
- **Partial agonists can behave as antagonists** of full agonists.
- **Warfarin = small TI; penicillin = large TI.**
