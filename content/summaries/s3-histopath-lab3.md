## 1. Definition, cardinal signs, acute vs chronic

- ⭐ **Inflammation** = a **response of vascularized tissues to infections and tissue damage** that **brings cells and molecules from the circulation to the sites where they are needed, to eliminate the offending agents**.
- **Acute:** **initial, rapid** response, **minutes or hours**, lasts **hours to a few days**; **edema (exudation of fluid and plasma proteins)** + **emigration of leukocytes, mainly neutrophils**.
- **Chronic:** **follows acute inflammation or arises de novo**; **longer duration**, **more tissue destruction**, **lymphocytes and macrophages**, **proliferation of blood vessels**, **fibrosis**.

### Table 3.1 — Acute vs chronic
| Feature | **Acute** | **Chronic** |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | **fast: minutes or hours** | **slow: days** |
| Cellular infiltrate | **mainly neutrophils** | **monocytes/macrophages and lymphocytes** |
| Tissue injury, fibrosis | **usually mild and self-limited** | **may be severe and progressive** |
| Local and systemic signs | **prominent** | **less** |

### Cardinal signs
| English | Latin | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Heat** | Calor | **vasodilation** |
| **Redness** | Rubor | **vasodilation** |
| **Swelling** | Tumor | **↑ vascular permeability, ↑ granulation tissue** |
| **Pain** | Dolor | **physical and chemical stimulation of nociceptors** |
| **Loss of function** | Functio laesa | **pain, reflex muscle inhibition, disruption of tissue structure, fibroplasia and metaplasia** |
- Acronym **PRISH = Pain, Redness, Immobility, Swelling, Heat**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Inflammation brings cells and molecules to the injury site to eliminate the agent.
- Acute: minutes–hours, neutrophils, edema; chronic: slow, macrophages/lymphocytes, fibrosis, angiogenesis.
- Cardinal signs: calor, rubor, tumor, dolor, functio laesa (PRISH).

</div>

## 2. Acute inflammation: components, exudate vs transudate, appendicitis

### Three major components
1. **Dilation of small vessels** → **↑ blood flow**.
2. **↑ vascular permeability** → **plasma proteins and leukocytes leave the circulation**.
3. **Emigration of leukocytes** from the microcirculation → **accumulation** at the focus and **activation**.

| | **Exudate** | **Transudate** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | **escape of fluid, proteins and blood cells into tissues or cavities** | **low-protein fluid, little or no cells; ultrafiltrate of plasma** |
| Permeability | **↑ (increased permeability of small vessels)** | **normal** (osmotic/hydrostatic imbalance) |

### Acute appendicitis
- **Gross:** **yellow-tan exudate and hyperemia** (including the periappendiceal fat) instead of a **smooth, glistening, pale tan serosa**.
- **Microscopic:** **mucosal inflammation and necrosis**, **ulceration and undermining of the mucosa by an extensive neutrophilic exudate**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Components: vasodilation, ↑ permeability, leukocyte emigration.
- Exudate = ↑ permeability, protein-rich, cells; transudate = normal permeability, low protein, ultrafiltrate.
- Appendicitis: neutrophilic exudate, mucosal ulceration and necrosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Mention the three major components of acute inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** Dilation of small vessels increasing blood flow; increased vascular permeability allowing plasma proteins and leukocytes to leave the circulation; and emigration of leukocytes from the microcirculation with accumulation and activation at the injury site.

**Why:** Slide "Acute inflammation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate exudate from transudate.</summary>

**Answer:** An exudate is the escape of fluid, proteins and blood cells into tissues or cavities and implies increased permeability of small vessels. A transudate is a low-protein, cell-poor ultrafiltrate of plasma produced by osmotic or hydrostatic imbalance across vessels with normal permeability.

**Why:** Definitions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the gross and microscopic features of acute appendicitis.</summary>

**Answer:** Grossly a yellow-tan exudate with hyperemia of the appendix and periappendiceal fat; microscopically mucosal inflammation and necrosis with ulceration and undermining by an extensive neutrophilic exudate.

**Why:** Slides "Acute appendicitis".
</details>

## 3. Morphologic patterns of acute inflammation

| Pattern | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Serous** | **watery, protein-poor fluid, not infected, few leukocytes**; **skin blister of a burn or viral infection**; **contact dermatitis blisters**; **effusion within or beneath the epidermis** |
| **Fibrinous** | **severe injury → ↑ permeability → fibrinogen leaks**; ends in **resolution or organization/scarring**; **lining of body cavities: meninges, pericardium**; pericardium **dry, coarse, granular**; **fronds of fibrin + neutrophils + plump reactive mesothelial cells** |
| **Suppurative / abscess** | **pus = neutrophils, necrotic cells, edema fluid**; **pyogenic (staphylococci)**; **abscess = localized pus buried in a tissue/organ/confined space** from **seeding of pyogenic bacteria**; examples: **infected wound, suppurative inflammation of the lung, skin abscess in the deep dermis with ulceration** |
| **Ulcer** | **local defect/excavation of a surface from necrosis and sloughing**; only when **necrosis + inflammation are on or near a surface**; **peptic ulcer (gastric antrum), hemorrhaging gastric ulcer, mouth ulcer, venous leg ulcer (between knee and ankle; shallow with uneven weeping edges)**; gastric ulcer microscopically: **no epithelium in the crater, submucosa exposed** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Serous = watery, protein-poor, sterile; fibrinous = fibrin, meninges/pericardium; suppurative = pus/abscess (pyogenic); ulcer = surface defect from necrosis.
- Fibrinous exudate resolves or organizes/scars.
- Gastric ulcer: loss of epithelium, exposed submucosa.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe serous and fibrinous inflammation with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Serous inflammation is the outpouring of a watery, protein-poor fluid that is usually not infected and has few leukocytes (burn or viral blister, contact dermatitis). Fibrinous inflammation follows more severe injury with greater permeability allowing fibrinogen to leak; the exudate resolves or is organized and scarred, and it typically affects the meninges and pericardium (dry coarse granular pericardium; fronds of fibrin with neutrophils and reactive mesothelial cells).

**Why:** Patterns I and II.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define abscess and pus.</summary>

**Answer:** Pus is a purulent exudate of neutrophils, necrotic cells and edema fluid; an abscess is a localized collection of pus buried in a tissue, organ or confined space, produced by seeding of pyogenic bacteria (e.g. staphylococci).

**Why:** Pattern III.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define ulcer and give examples.</summary>

**Answer:** An ulcer is a local defect or excavation of the surface of an organ or tissue from necrosis and sloughing of necrotic and inflammatory tissue; it needs necrosis and inflammation on or near a surface. Examples: peptic (gastric antral) ulcer, hemorrhaging gastric ulcer, mouth ulcer and venous leg ulcer.

**Why:** Pattern IV.
</details>

## 4. Chronic inflammation

- **Causes tissue damage in:** **rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, tuberculosis, chronic lung diseases**; **infiltration with macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells**.
- Starts **ab initio (insidious, low-grade)** or **complicates acute (usually suppurative) inflammation**.
- **Rheumatoid arthritis:** **chronic autoimmune** disease with **systemic inflammation destroying joints** (lining, cartilage, bone) → **swelling, stiffness, loss of function, deformity**; **chronic synovitis with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate and lymphoid aggregates; prominent fibrosis**.
- **Lung (chronic bronchial inflammation):** **bronchial dilation and scarring**, **increased collagen**, **chronic inflammatory cells in the submucosa**. **Three features:** **(1) chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate, (2) destruction of normal alveoli, (3) fibrosis** (vs **acute bronchopneumonia: neutrophils in alveoli**).
- **Chronic cervicitis:** chronic inflammation in the cervical **squamous epithelium**.
- **Cholecystitis:** **chronic calculous cholecystitis** = **mixed cholesterol stones**, **irregularly thickened wall (inflammation + fibrosis)**, focal transmural necrosis; **hallmarks:** **mononuclear infiltrate in the lamina propria, fibrosis, mucosal metaplasia**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Chronic: macrophages/lymphocytes/plasma cells, tissue destruction, fibrosis.
- RA: autoimmune synovitis with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate and fibrosis.
- Lung: infiltrate + alveolar destruction + fibrosis; cholecystitis: stones + thick fibrotic wall.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the three characteristic features of chronic inflammation in the lung?</summary>

**Answer:** (1) Infiltration by chronic inflammatory cells, (2) destruction of normal tissue (alveoli replaced by spaces lined by cuboidal cells) and (3) replacement by fibrosis.

**Why:** Slide "Features of chronic inflammation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe rheumatoid arthritis as chronic inflammation.</summary>

**Answer:** A chronic autoimmune disease with systemic inflammation that destroys joint lining, cartilage and bone causing painful swelling, stiffness, loss of function and deformity; histology shows chronic synovitis with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, lymphoid aggregates and prominent fibrosis.

**Why:** Slides on rheumatoid arthritis.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe chronic calculous cholecystitis.</summary>

**Answer:** The gallbladder contains many mixed cholesterol stones and has an irregularly thickened wall due to inflammation and fibrosis with focal necrosis; histologic hallmarks are a mononuclear infiltrate in the lamina propria, fibrosis and metaplastic mucosal change.

**Why:** Cholecystitis slides.
</details>

## 5. Granulomatous inflammation

- **Granulomatous inflammation** = chronic reaction with **focal accumulations of activated macrophages with epithelioid appearance**.
- ⭐ **Granuloma** = **microscopic aggregation of macrophages transformed into epithelioid cells**, surrounded by a **collar of lymphocytes (± plasma cells)**; **older granulomas have a fibroblast/connective tissue rim**.
- **Epithelioid cells:** **pale pink granular cytoplasm, indistinct borders, vesicular oval/elongate nucleus**. **Giant cells** = fused epithelioid cells with **≥ 20 nuclei**: **Langhans = peripheral nuclei; foreign body = haphazard nuclei**.
- **TB (M. tuberculosis):** **central caseous necrosis** (grossly **cheese-like**; microscopically **irregular, amorphous, pink**); **active pulmonary TB: numerous confluent granulomas in the upper lobes**; typical granuloma mix: **epithelioid macrophages, Langhans cells, lymphocytes, plasma cells, fibroblasts (± neutrophils)**.
- **Foreign body granulomas:** material **not phagocytosable by a single macrophage — talc, sutures, fibers**; giant cells **apposed to or surrounding the material**, **identified in the center**; examples: **suture strand, aspirated vegetable material (thick walls), silk implants**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Granuloma = epithelioid macrophages + lymphocyte collar (+ giant cells, fibroblast rim in older ones).
- TB = caseating (central cheesy necrosis); foreign body = talc/sutures/fibers, material in the center.
- Langhans = peripheral nuclei; foreign body type = haphazard.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define a granuloma.</summary>

**Answer:** A focus of chronic inflammation made of a microscopic aggregation of macrophages transformed into epithelioid cells surrounded by a collar of mononuclear leukocytes, mainly lymphocytes and occasional plasma cells; older granulomas get a rim of fibroblasts and connective tissue and may contain giant cells.

**Why:** Slides "Granuloma".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe a caseating (TB) granuloma.</summary>

**Answer:** Epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, lymphocytes and fibroblasts surround a central irregular, amorphous pink zone of caseous necrosis, which grossly looks cheese-like; confluent granulomas occur in the upper lobes in active pulmonary TB.

**Why:** TB granuloma slides.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a foreign body granuloma?</summary>

**Answer:** A granuloma formed around material that cannot be phagocytosed by a single macrophage (talc, sutures, fibers); epithelioid cells and giant cells (nuclei haphazard) lie against or around the material, which is found in the center.

**Why:** Foreign body slides.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Transudate = normal permeability; exudate = ↑ permeability.**
- **Serous fluid is protein-poor and sterile; pus = neutrophils + necrotic cells + edema.**
- **Fibrinous: meninges, pericardium; resolves or scars.**
- **Ulcer needs necrosis on/near a surface; abscess is buried pus.**
- **Chronic inflammation: macrophages + lymphocytes + plasma cells; fibrosis and angiogenesis.**
- **Langhans giant cell = peripheral nuclei; foreign body giant cell = haphazard nuclei.**
- **Caseation = TB (cheesy, amorphous pink); talc/sutures/fibers = foreign body granuloma.**
- **PRISH: Pain, Redness, Immobility, Swelling, Heat.**
