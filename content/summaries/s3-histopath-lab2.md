## 1. Objectives and cell injury flow

### Learning objectives
1. **Define cell injury** and list the **patterns of tissue necrosis**: **coagulative, liquefactive, caseous, gangrenous, fat, fibrinoid**.
2. Explain **reversible vs irreversible injury** and **necrosis vs apoptosis**.
3. Describe **cellular adaptations**: **hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy, metaplasia**.
4. Examine **intracellular accumulations** (**fatty change/steatosis**) and **pigments** (**lipofuscin, bilirubin, hemosiderin**).
5. Differentiate **dystrophic vs metastatic calcification**.

### Cell injury — introduction
- **Homeostasis** = tightly regulated, constant intracellular environment.
- Under **physiologic stress** (↑ workload of the heart) or **injurious conditions** (nutrient deprivation) cells **adapt** → new steady state.
- If **adaptive capability is exceeded** or the stress is harmful/excessive → **cell injury**: **reversible** (within limits) or **irreversible** (severe, persistent, rapid onset) → **cell death**.
- Causes of death: **ischemia, infections, toxins, immune reactions**; but **cell death is also normal** (embryogenesis, organ development, tissue homeostasis).

### Flow of events (figure)
| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| **Normal cell** | homeostasis |
| ⭐ **Reversible injury** | **membrane blebbing, ribosomal detachment, ER swelling, mitochondrial swelling, cellular swelling, nuclear chromatin clumping** |
| ⭐ **Irreversible injury** | **lysosomal rupture, phospholipid membrane damage, ↑ mitochondrial permeability** |
| **Nuclear changes** | **pyknosis (condensation), karyorrhexis (fragmentation), karyolysis (nuclear pallor/fading)** |
| **Death** | **necrosis or apoptosis** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Injury develops when adaptation is exceeded; reversible vs irreversible depends on severity, persistence and speed of onset.
- Reversible: blebbing, ER/mitochondrial/cell swelling, chromatin clumping. Irreversible: lysosomal rupture, membrane damage, ↑ mitochondrial permeability.
- Nuclear changes: pyknosis → karyorrhexis → karyolysis.

</div>

## 2. Apoptosis and necrosis

### Apoptosis
- **Eliminates cells with intrinsic abnormalities** and **clears fragments without inflammation**; **also occurs in healthy tissue** (eliminates unwanted cells in development, maintains cell numbers) = **programmed cell death**.
- **A regulated suicide program**; the dead cell is **rapidly cleared before contents leak**; **no inflammation**; **physiologic and pathologic types**.
- **Morphology:** **fragmented nuclei with condensed chromatin and shrunken cell bodies, some with pieces falling off** (e.g. **colonic epithelium**).

### Necrosis
- **Membranes fall apart, enzymes leak and digest the cell** → **inflammation** (induced by substances from dead cells) removes debris and starts repair.
- **Morphology:** **↑ cytoplasmic eosinophilia (deeper pink)**; **nuclear changes** (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis).

### Table 2.1 — Necrosis vs apoptosis
| Feature | **Necrosis** | **Apoptosis** |
|---|---|---|
| Cell size | **enlarged** | **reduced** |
| Nucleus | **pyknosis → karyorrhexis → karyolysis** | **fragmentation into nucleosome-sized pieces** |
| Plasma membrane | **disrupted** | **intact** (altered lipid orientation) |
| Contents | **enzymatic digestion; leak out** | **intact; in apoptotic bodies** |
| Inflammation | **frequent** | **none** |
| Role | **invariably pathologic** | **often physiologic**; may be pathologic after DNA/protein damage |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Apoptosis: programmed, clean, no inflammation, shrinkage, intact membrane, apoptotic bodies.
- Necrosis: swelling, membrane disruption, inflammation, ↑ eosinophilia, always pathologic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define apoptosis and describe its morphology.</summary>

**Answer:** A regulated cell-suicide program that eliminates abnormal or unwanted cells and clears fragments without inflammation; morphologically, shrunken cells with condensed chromatin and fragmented nuclei, with pieces breaking off (apoptotic bodies), as in colonic epithelium.

**Why:** Programmed cell death.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define necrosis and describe its morphology.</summary>

**Answer:** Cell death in which membranes disintegrate and leaked enzymes digest the cell, eliciting inflammation; the cytoplasm becomes deeper pink (eosinophilic) and the nucleus shows pyknosis, karyorrhexis and karyolysis.

**Why:** Always pathologic.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare necrosis and apoptosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Necrosis: enlarged cells, disrupted membrane, enzymatic digestion, frequent inflammation, always pathologic. Apoptosis: shrunken cells, intact membrane, apoptotic bodies, no inflammation, often physiologic.

**Why:** Table 2.1.
</details>

## 3. Patterns of necrosis — specimens

| Pattern | Gross/microscopic specimen features |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Coagulative** | **myocardial infarction** (dark mottling at 12–24 h; yellow softened lesion with red-tan borders at 10–14 days); micro: **eosinophilic cytoplasm, no cross-striations, pyknotic or absent nuclei (H&E, 40×)**; **splenic infarcts** — **wedge-shaped, base on the capsule, pale (ischemic) infarct, subcapsular rim spared** (capsular arteries); usually **vascular loss of blood supply** |
| ⭐ **Liquefactive** | **cerebral infarction** (hypoxia): **melted cells as yellowish mucus masses**; over weeks monocytes→macrophages → **cystic space**; **liver abscess**: many **neutrophils**, yellow-tan **pus** |
| ⭐ **Caseous** | **tuberculosis lung**: **yellow-white cheesy debris**, **granulomas (tubercles)** of macrophages/inflammatory cells, confluent **cheesy tan granulomas** in the upper lung |
| ⭐ **Gangrenous** | **dry gangrene**: atherosclerosis-induced foot; **wet gangrene**: infected deep irregular ulcers (back of foot, toe after auto-amputation) = **coagulative necrosis + liquefactive component from superimposed infection**; typical in **diabetes with severe peripheral vascular disease** |
| ⭐ **Fat necrosis** | injury of **pancreatic acini releases powerful enzymes** → **soaps**, **soft chalky-white areas**; micro: **vague cell outlines, lost peripheral nuclei, pink amorphous cytoplasm**; also **breast fat necrosis at a prior biopsy/lumpectomy site** (vacuolization, adipocyte dropout, lymphocytes + plasma cells) |
| ⭐ **Fibrinoid** | **intensely pink** necrosis of **blood vessel wall in vasculitis**; **polyarteritis nodosa** (fibrinoid necrosis + intimal proliferation) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Coagulative: infarcts (heart, spleen—wedge-shaped, base on capsule); micro: eosinophilic, anucleate, no striations.
- Liquefactive: brain infarct → cystic space; abscess = pus with neutrophils.
- Caseous: TB, cheesy granulomas. Gangrene: dry (atherosclerosis) vs wet (infection, diabetes).
- Fat necrosis: pancreatic enzymes → chalky soaps; breast after biopsy. Fibrinoid: vasculitis, polyarteritis nodosa.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the appearance of coagulative necrosis in the heart and spleen.</summary>

**Answer:** In the heart, myocardial infarction appears grossly as dark mottling (12–24 h) then a yellow softened lesion with red-tan borders (10–14 days); microscopically the fibers have distinct eosinophilic cytoplasm, lost cross-striations and pyknotic or absent nuclei. In the spleen, infarcts are wedge-shaped, pale, with their base on the capsule and a spared subcapsular rim; the etiology is loss of blood supply.

**Why:** Typical ischemic infarcts.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate liquefactive from caseous necrosis in specimens.</summary>

**Answer:** Liquefactive necrosis: cerebral infarction with melted yellowish tissue that becomes a cystic space, or a liver abscess filled with neutrophils and pus. Caseous necrosis: tuberculous lung with yellow-white cheese-like debris and confluent tan granulomas (tubercles).

**Why:** Different etiology and appearance.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate dry from wet gangrene and describe fat and fibrinoid necrosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Dry gangrene is ischemic coagulative necrosis (e.g. atherosclerotic foot); wet gangrene adds a liquefactive component from superimposed infection (diabetic foot ulcers). Fat necrosis: pancreatic enzymes make soaps, giving chalky-white areas with ghost-like fat cells (also in the breast after biopsy). Fibrinoid necrosis: intensely pink necrosis of vessel walls in vasculitis such as polyarteritis nodosa.

**Why:** Remaining patterns.
</details>

## 4. Cellular adaptations (specimens)

- **Adaptations** = reversible changes in **number, size, phenotype, metabolic activity or function** in response to environmental change (**physiologic** and **pathologic**).

| Adaptation | Definition | Specimens |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Hypertrophy** | **↑ cell size → ↑ organ size**; no new cells; physiologic or pathologic | **pregnant uterus** (hypertrophy + hyperplasia of smooth muscle; plump cells vs small spindle cells); **LV thickening in hypertension** (bigger fibers, **thicker fibers with larger darker nuclei**, no increase in number) |
| ⭐ **Hyperplasia** | **↑ cell number**; needs replicating cells; may coexist with hypertrophy | **endometrial hyperplasia** (glands + stroma ↑); **benign prostatic (nodular) hyperplasia**: obstructive urinary symptoms, tissue projecting into the bladder neck; micro: glandular proliferation with cystic dilatation and **corpora amylacea** |
| ⭐ **Atrophy** | **shrinkage of cells by loss of cell substance**; organ becomes smaller | **muscle atrophy** from prolonged disuse |
| ⭐ **Metaplasia** | **reversible replacement of one adult cell type by another** under chronic stress (smoke, stomach acid); persistent → **dysplasia → cancer** | **bronchus: columnar → squamous**; **cervix transformation zone: columnar → stratified squamous** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hypertrophy (size): pregnant uterus, hypertensive LV. Hyperplasia (number): endometrium, BPH (corpora amylacea).
- Atrophy: loss of cell substance (disuse). Metaplasia: columnar → squamous (bronchus, cervix); may become dysplastic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate hypertrophy from hyperplasia with specimen examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Hypertrophy is an increase in cell size without new cells (pregnant uterus, left ventricle in hypertension with thicker fibers and larger nuclei); hyperplasia is an increase in cell number (endometrial glands and stroma, benign prostatic hyperplasia with nodular growth and corpora amylacea).

**Why:** Size vs number.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define atrophy and give an example.</summary>

**Answer:** Shrinkage of cell size by loss of cell substance, making the tissue or organ smaller, e.g. muscle atrophy after prolonged disuse.

**Why:** Slide "Atrophy".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Define metaplasia and its significance.</summary>

**Answer:** Reversible replacement of one adult cell type by another, usually under chronic stress such as smoke or stomach acid (bronchial and cervical columnar epithelium becoming squamous); if the stress persists it may become dysplastic, a precancerous condition.

**Why:** Adaptive but risky.
</details>

## 5. Intracellular accumulations, pigments and calcification

### Fatty change (steatosis)
- **Triglycerides in parenchymal cells** (liver); mild = no gross change; progressive = **enlarged, yellow, soft, greasy**; micro: **small perinuclear vacuoles → large clear space pushing the nucleus to the periphery (macrovesicular steatosis)**.

### Pigments (exogenous vs endogenous)
| Pigment | Features |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Lipofuscin** | **insoluble brownish-yellow granules**, **protein–lipid complex**; in **cardiac myocytes, hepatocytes** |
| ⭐ **Bilirubin** | **normal bile pigment from Hb, contains no iron**; **jaundice** (skin, sclera); **yellow-green globules in bile ductules = cholestasis/obstruction** |
| ⭐ **Hemosiderin** | **iron-containing ferritin aggregates**, **golden-brown**, **blue with Prussian blue**; **hemosiderosis** = in macrophages, **no damage**; **hemochromatosis** = parenchymal cells, **tissue damage/scarring/dysfunction**; e.g. **renal tubules after chronic hematuria**, liver |

### Pathologic calcification
| | **Dystrophic** | **Metastatic** |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue | **dead or dying** | **normal** |
| Occurs in | **necrosis (coagulative, caseous), advanced atherosclerosis (aorta, coronaries), aging/damaged heart valves** → **calcific aortic stenosis** (thickened, fibrotic cusps with irregular calcified masses behind them) | **interstitium of vessels, kidneys, lungs, gastric mucosa**, e.g. **lungs in chronic renal failure** |
| Serum calcium | normal | **hypercalcemia** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Steatosis: macrovesicular, nucleus pushed to the periphery; liver yellow, soft, greasy.
- Lipofuscin (brown-yellow, protein–lipid), bilirubin (no iron; cholestasis), hemosiderin (iron, Prussian blue; hemosiderosis vs hemochromatosis).
- Dystrophic (dead tissue; aortic stenosis) vs metastatic (normal tissue; CRF lungs).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe fatty change in the liver.</summary>

**Answer:** Abnormal triglyceride accumulation in hepatocytes; early small perinuclear vacuoles coalesce into one large clear space displacing the nucleus to the periphery (macrovesicular steatosis); the liver becomes enlarged, yellow, soft and greasy.

**Why:** Slide "Fatty change".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare lipofuscin, bilirubin and hemosiderin.</summary>

**Answer:** Lipofuscin: insoluble brownish-yellow protein–lipid granules in cardiac myocytes and hepatocytes. Bilirubin: bile pigment derived from hemoglobin without iron, causing jaundice and yellow-green globules in cholestasis. Hemosiderin: golden-brown iron-containing ferritin aggregates, stained blue by Prussian blue; hemosiderosis (macrophages, no damage) vs hemochromatosis (parenchymal cells, with damage).

**Why:** Pigment slides.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate dystrophic from metastatic calcification with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Dystrophic calcification occurs in dead or dying tissue (necrosis, advanced atherosclerosis, damaged valves causing calcific aortic stenosis) with normal calcium; metastatic calcification occurs in normal tissue because of hypercalcemia, affecting vessels, kidneys, lungs and gastric mucosa (e.g. lung calcification in chronic renal failure).

**Why:** Tissue condition and calcium level.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Splenic infarct = wedge-shaped, base on the capsule, pale; subcapsular rim spared.**
- **Brain infarct = liquefactive; heart/spleen = coagulative.**
- **Caseous = TB with granulomas.**
- **Wet gangrene = coagulative + liquefactive (infection).**
- **Fat necrosis: pancreatic enzymes → soaps (chalky white).**
- **Fibrinoid necrosis = vessel wall in vasculitis (polyarteritis nodosa).**
- **Bilirubin has no iron; hemosiderin does (Prussian blue).**
- **Hemosiderosis = no damage; hemochromatosis = damage.**
- **Dystrophic = dead tissue, normal Ca²⁺; metastatic = normal tissue, ↑ Ca²⁺.**
- **Corpora amylacea in benign prostatic hyperplasia.**
