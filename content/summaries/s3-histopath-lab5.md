## 1. Hyperemia and congestion

| | **Hyperemia** | **Congestion** |
|---|---|---|
| **Process** | **Active: arteriolar dilation, ↑ inflow** | **Passive: impaired venous outflow** |
| **Gross** | **Redder than normal (oxygenated blood)** | **Blue-red / cyanosis (deoxygenated hemoglobin)** |

- **Pulmonary venous congestion:** **↑ left atrial pressure** from **LV failure** (coronary artery disease, systemic hypertension) or **mitral stenosis.**
- **Acute:** **alveoli filled with pink edema material, congested alveolar capillaries.** **Chronic:** **dilated capillaries, blood leaking into alveoli → hemosiderin-laden macrophages (heart failure cells).**
- ⭐ **Acute hepatic congestion:** **central vein and sinusoids distended; centrilobular necrosis; peri-portal hepatocytes (better oxygenated) may show only reversible fatty change.**
- ⭐ **Chronic passive congestion = nutmeg liver:** **red-brown depressed centrilobular zones against tan-fatty uncongested zones.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Hyperemia = active and red; congestion = passive and blue-red.
- Heart failure cells (hemosiderin macrophages) = chronic pulmonary congestion; nutmeg liver = chronic hepatic congestion.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate hyperemia from congestion.</summary>

**Answer:** Hyperemia is active (arteriolar dilation, increased inflow) with red oxygenated tissue; congestion is passive (impaired venous outflow) with blue-red cyanotic tissue from deoxygenated hemoglobin.

**Why:** Slides "Hyperemia" and "Congestion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe acute and chronic pulmonary congestion.</summary>

**Answer:** Acute: alveoli filled with pink edema and congested capillaries; chronic: dilated capillaries with leakage of blood and hemosiderin-laden macrophages (heart failure cells); caused by raised left atrial pressure (LV failure, mitral stenosis).

**Why:** Slides "Pulmonary venous congestion".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe hepatic congestion and nutmeg liver.</summary>

**Answer:** Acute: distended central vein and sinusoids with centrilobular necrosis; chronic: red-brown, slightly depressed central zones against tan-fatty uncongested zones (nutmeg liver).

**Why:** Slides "Hepatic congestion".
</details>

## 2. Edema

- ⭐ **Edema = accumulation of interstitial fluid;** in cavities it is an **effusion.**
- **Subcutaneous:** **pitting edema, periorbital edema.**
- **Pulmonary:** **fluid in alveoli, congested alveolar capillaries (RBCs).**
- **Cerebral:** **widened gyri with flattened surface, narrowed sulci;** histology of the basal ganglia vessel: edema (± siderophages, inflammatory cells).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Edema = interstitial fluid; effusion = fluid in a body cavity.
- Brain edema: wide gyri, narrow sulci.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define edema and give its three morphologic sites.</summary>

**Answer:** Accumulation of interstitial fluid; seen subcutaneously (pitting, periorbital), in the lungs (alveolar fluid) and the brain.

**Why:** Slides "Edema".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe cerebral edema.</summary>

**Answer:** Widened gyri with a flattened surface and narrowed sulci; microscopically edema around vessels, sometimes with inflammatory cells and siderophages.

**Why:** Slide "Brain edema".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe pulmonary edema histology.</summary>

**Answer:** Edema fluid within alveoli with congested alveolar capillaries containing RBCs.

**Why:** Slide "Pulmonary edema".
</details>

## 3. Thrombosis

- ⭐ **Thrombus = solid or semisolid mass from blood constituents formed within vessels during life** (postmortem clot is different).
- ⭐ **Virchow's triad:** **endothelial injury; abnormal flow (stasis or turbulence); hypercoagulability.**

| | **Arterial** | **Venous** |
|---|---|---|
| **Site / cause** | **Endothelial injury or turbulence; ruptured plaque, vasculitis, trauma** | **Stasis or hypercoagulability; lower-extremity veins** |
| **Example** | **MI** | **DVT (leg pain and swelling)** |

- ⭐ **Lines of Zahn:** **alternating pale layers (platelets, fibrin) and dark layers (RBCs); only in thrombi formed in flowing blood → distinguish antemortem thrombi from bland, non-laminated postmortem clots.**
- **Mural thrombi** (heart chambers, aortic lumen): **abnormal myocardial contraction, endomyocardial injury, ulcerated plaques, aortic aneurysm.** Examples: **after MI (LV wall, apex).**
- ⭐ **Valve thrombi = vegetations:** **infective endocarditis (non-sterile; IV drug users: tricuspid or pulmonary valve; S. aureus with fever, chills, fatigue, dyspnea; colonies and neutrophils in fibrin)** vs **nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis (sterile platelet-fibrin vegetations in hypercoagulable states).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Lines of Zahn = antemortem thrombus in flowing blood.
- Arterial = injury/turbulence; venous = stasis/hypercoagulability.
- Infective endocarditis = non-sterile; NBTE = sterile.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List Virchow's triad and compare arterial and venous thrombi.</summary>

**Answer:** Endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow and hypercoagulability; arterial thrombi form at injury or turbulence (e.g. MI), venous at stasis or hypercoagulability (e.g. DVT of leg veins).

**Why:** Slides "Arterial thrombus" and "Venous thrombosis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the lines of Zahn and their significance?</summary>

**Answer:** Alternating layers of platelets and fibrin with red cells in thrombi formed in flowing blood; they distinguish ante mortem thrombi from non-laminated postmortem clots.

**Why:** Slides "Lines of Zahn".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare infective endocarditis and nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis.</summary>

**Answer:** Infective: non-sterile vegetations from bacterial or fungal infection (e.g. S. aureus, IV drug users) with colonies and neutrophils in fibrin; nonbacterial: sterile platelet-fibrin vegetations on non-infected valves in hypercoagulable states.

**Why:** Slides "Thrombi on heart valves".
</details>

## 4. Hemorrhage

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Hemorrhage** | **Extravasation of blood from vessels** (vessel, platelet, coagulation defects, others) |
| **Petechiae** | **Pinpoint, non-blanching red/brown/purple spots in skin and mucosa (capillary bleeding)** |
| **Ecchymosis** | **Bruise: skin discoloration from ruptured subcutaneous vessels** |
| **Hematoma** | **Blood collection outside vessels with pain and swelling; trauma (accidents, falls, fractures, aneurysms)** |
| **Hemarthrosis** | **Bleeding into a joint: pain, swelling, pressure, restricted motion** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Petechiae are non-blanching.
- Hemarthrosis = blood in a joint.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define hemorrhage and list its causes.</summary>

**Answer:** Extravasation of blood from vessels due to inherited or acquired defects of vessels, platelets or coagulation factors, or other causes.

**Why:** Slide "Hemorrhage".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate petechiae, ecchymosis and hematoma.</summary>

**Answer:** Petechiae: pinpoint non-blanching spots; ecchymosis: a bruise; hematoma: a collection of blood outside vessels causing pain and swelling.

**Why:** Slides on hemorrhage types.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is hemarthrosis?</summary>

**Answer:** Bleeding into a joint space causing pain, swelling, pressure and restricted range of motion.

**Why:** Slide "Hemarthrosis".
</details>

## 5. Embolism

- ⭐ **Pulmonary thromboembolism:** **> 95% from DVT** (deep leg veins proximal to the popliteal fossa) → right heart → pulmonary vasculature.
- ⭐ **Systemic thromboembolism:** **mostly intracardiac mural thrombi (LV infarcts);** also **aortic aneurysms, atherosclerotic plaques, valvular vegetations, venous system (paradoxical).** **Venous emboli lodge in the lung; arterial emboli can go anywhere.**
- **Fat embolism:** **fat globules from long bone/pelvic fractures, severe skeletal injury, soft tissue injury, burns;** pulmonary arterioles occluded by adipose islands.
- **Amniotic fluid embolism:** **uncommon, high mortality; during labor or immediately postpartum; entry via placental membrane tears or uterine vein rupture; fetal squamous cells in pulmonary arterioles.**
- **Air embolism:** **bubbles coalesce, obstruct flow, distal ischemia; small venous gas emboli generally harmless.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pulmonary emboli: DVT > 95%.
- Systemic emboli: mural thrombi after LV infarct.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the origin of pulmonary and systemic emboli?</summary>

**Answer:** Pulmonary: DVT of deep leg veins (> 95%); systemic: mostly intracardiac mural thrombi from LV infarcts, plus aortic aneurysms, plaques, valvular vegetations and paradoxical emboli.

**Why:** Slides "Embolism".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe fat embolism and amniotic fluid embolism.</summary>

**Answer:** Fat embolism: fat globules from fractures and soft tissue injury lodge in pulmonary arterioles; amniotic fluid embolism: rare, highly fatal, during labor or postpartum with fetal squamous cells in pulmonary arterioles.

**Why:** Slides "Fat embolism" and "Amniotic fluid embolism".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When is an air embolism harmful?</summary>

**Answer:** When bubbles coalesce and obstruct flow causing distal ischemia; small venous gas emboli are usually harmless.

**Why:** Slide "Air embolism".
</details>

## 6. Infarction

- ⭐ **Infarct = ischemic necrosis from vascular occlusion.** Classified by **color (red/hemorrhagic vs white/anemic)** and **infection (septic vs bland).**

| | **Red (hemorrhagic)** | **White (anemic)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | **Dual blood supply or collateral flow (lung via bronchial arteries; bowel)** | **Solid organs with end-arterial supply (kidney, spleen)** |
| **Gross** | **Dark red-brown, wedge-shaped, demarcated; alveolar necrosis and hemorrhage in lung** | **Pale, wedge-shaped coagulative necrosis (kidney cortex, spleen subcapsular)** |

- **Myocardial infarction:** **coronary artery blockage/narrowing (plaque).** **Healed MI = dense collagenous scar; age cannot be determined once completely healed.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Lung and bowel: red infarcts; kidney and spleen: white infarcts.
- Infarcts are wedge-shaped (branching vascular supply).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare red and white infarcts.</summary>

**Answer:** Red infarcts occur with dual supply or collateral flow (lung, bowel) and are dark red-brown wedges; white infarcts occur in end-artery organs (kidney, spleen) and are pale wedge-shaped zones of coagulative necrosis.

**Why:** Slides "Red infarcts" and "White infarcts".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is a pulmonary infarct hemorrhagic?</summary>

**Answer:** Because of the dual blood supply: blood still flows from non-occluded bronchial arteries, which does not prevent infarction but causes alveolar hemorrhage.

**Why:** Slide "Red infarcts".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does a healed myocardial infarct look like?</summary>

**Answer:** Dense collagenous scar between viable myocytes; its age can no longer be determined from morphology.

**Why:** Slide "Myocardial infarction".
</details>
