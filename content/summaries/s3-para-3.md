## 1. Introduction, summary and structure

### Summary table
| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Disease** | **amoebic dysentery** (intestinal and extra-intestinal amoebiasis) |
| **Infective stage** | **mature cyst** from the patient's stool; **route: orally** |
| **Habitat** | **large intestine, mainly caecum and colon** |
| **Host** | **only the human** |
| **Diagnostic stage** | **cyst and trophozoite in stool** |
| **Source of infection** | **only humans** (patient's stool) |
| **Movement** | **pseudopodia** |
| **Reproduction** | **asexually by binary fission** |

### Introduction
- Discovered by **Lösch in 1875**; in **1890 William Osler** reported a young person with dysenteric feces who died of a **liver abscess**.
- The **third leading cause of mortality after malaria and schistosomiasis**.
- **All intestinal amoebae are non-pathogenic EXCEPT *E. histolytica*** (amoebic dysentery) — and ***Acanthamoeba*** (**keratitis and granulomatous encephalitis**).

### Structure
- **No fixed shape**, irregular, actively motile via **pseudopodia from the ectoplasm** (locomotion + phagocytosis).
- **Ectoplasm** (outer, clear): **locomotion, excretion, defense**. **Endoplasm** (inner, granular): **metabolism**; contains **one true nucleus** (spherical, **4–6 µm**, **central karyosome**), **food vacuoles, excretory vacuoles**; stored food = carbohydrate/protein.
- **Stages:** **precystic** (trophozoite encysts in the intestinal cavity), **cyst**, **metacystic** (excystation by **alkaline medium and trypsin** → **4 nuclei** → **4 small metacystic trophozoites**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Infective = mature (4-nucleate) cyst orally; habitat caecum/colon; human only; diagnosis = cyst + trophozoite in stool.
- Only E. histolytica (and Acanthamoeba) are pathogenic amoebae.
- Ectoplasm = locomotion/defense; endoplasm = metabolism, one nucleus with central karyosome.
- Excystation: alkaline medium + trypsin → 4 metacystic trophozoites.

</div>

## 2. Life cycle (one host: the human)

1. **Ingestion:** swallowing food/water contaminated with the **mature cyst** from patients/carriers. **Trophozoites are killed by gastric juice**; the **cyst wall resists gastric acid**.
2. **Excystation:** cysts pass the stomach; in the **small intestine** (alkaline medium + **trypsin**) the wall ruptures and **four-nucleated amoeba** are released; each surrounded by cytoplasm → **4 small trophozoites** → binary division → **8 metacystic trophozoites**. They **do not infect the small intestine** — they pass to the **large intestine (caecum)**.
3. **Invasion:** trophozoites invade the **columnar epithelium** of the caecum/colon using **motility, the tissue-lytic enzyme histolysin, cysteine proteinase and lectin**, forming **mucosal (superficial) ulcers → deep flask-shaped ulcers** (narrow mouth/neck, rounded base; "beehive"-like) that **extend to the submucosa and muscular layer but NOT to the subserosa**. **Virulence factors:** amoebic **cysteine proteinase, lectin**; **host factors: malnutrition, alcoholism, immunodeficiency**.
   - Trophozoites spread laterally (undermining, patchy mucosal loss); may cause **perforation and peritonitis** or **hemorrhage**; carried by blood to **liver, lungs, brain, spleen**.
4. **Encystment:** some trophozoites are passed in stool; others form **precystic forms → cysts** (1 nucleus → **2 nuclei** → **mature 4-nuclei = quadrinucleate (infective)**) in the stool/soil.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cyst resists gastric acid; excystation in the small intestine (alkaline pH + trypsin) → 4 → 8 metacystic trophozoites → large intestine.
- Invasion with histolysin, cysteine proteinase, lectin → flask-shaped ulcers (not beyond submucosa/muscularis).
- Cyst maturation: 1 → 2 → 4 nuclei (infective).
- Trophozoites ingested are killed by gastric juice.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the life cycle of E. histolytica.</summary>

**Answer:** The human swallows food or water contaminated with mature cysts; the cyst resists gastric acid and excysts in the small intestine under alkaline pH and trypsin, releasing a four-nucleated amoeba that gives 4 then 8 metacystic trophozoites which migrate to the large intestine, invade the colonic wall forming ulcers, and multiply by binary fission; some trophozoites encyst and are passed in stool as mature four-nucleated cysts.

**Why:** Direct life cycle with one host.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does E. histolytica invade the colon?</summary>

**Answer:** Metacystic trophozoites penetrate the columnar epithelium by their motility and the lytic enzyme histolysin (with cysteine proteinase and lectin), forming superficial ulcers that develop into deep flask-shaped ulcers extending to the submucosa and muscular layer; host malnutrition, alcoholism and immunodeficiency increase virulence.

**Why:** Invasion step.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why are ingested trophozoites not infective?</summary>

**Answer:** They are killed by gastric juice, whereas the cyst wall is resistant to gastric acidity.

**Why:** Only cysts transmit infection.
</details>

## 3. Pathogenesis and clinical features of intestinal amoebiasis

- **Incubation: 1–4 months (weeks to months).**
- **Forms:** **asymptomatic colonization (carrier / non-dysenteric amoebiasis)** — cysts/trophozoites remain as **commensals in the lumen**; **symptomatic infection** with invasion.
- **Two ulcer types:** **small muco-ulceration** (superficial) and **deep flask-shaped ulceration**; **mucosa between ulcers is usually normal**.
- **Amoeboma:** a **localized granuloma** forming a **palpable mass in the rectal wall**; **may be mistaken for a tumor**.

### Dysenteric (intestinal) amoebiasis = amoebic colitis
- **Abdominal cramps/pain (lower abdomen, especially right)**, **no high fever or severe hemorrhage**, **mild intermittent bloody diarrhea with pus cells, RBCs and offensive odor**; **alternating diarrhea and constipation**.
- Confused with **bacillary dysentery** (which has **high fever and no blood**).

### Amoebic vs bacillary dysentery
| Feature | **Amoebic** | **Bacillary** |
|---|---|---|
| Course | **slow, localized** | **acute, generalized** |
| Fever / toxicity | **absent** | **present** |
| Tenesmus | **present** | **absent** |
| Stool | **6–8/day, blood and mucus NOT adherent to container** | **> 10/day, little/no blood, mucus adherent to container** |
| Odor | **offensive** | **nil** |
| Color | **dark red (RBCs)** | **bright red** |
| Reaction | **acid** | **alkaline** |
| Motile bacteria | **absent** | **present** |
| Motile amoeba (trophozoite with ingested RBCs) | **present** | **absent** |
| Charcot–Leyden crystals | **present** | **absent** |

### Complications
1. **Fulminant amoebic colitis:** **toxic megacolon, perianal ulceration, amoeboma**; ulcer may reach muscular/serous coats → **perforation and peritonitis**; vessel erosion → **hemorrhage**.
2. **Extra-intestinal (metastatic) amoebiasis:** spread by blood and lymphatics → abscesses in the liver, lung, kidney, brain, spleen, adrenals.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Incubation 1–4 months; carriers (non-dysenteric) vs invasive disease; flask-shaped ulcers; amoeboma mimics a tumor.
- Amoebic colitis: lower right abdominal pain, no high fever, bloody stool with offensive odor, alternating constipation.
- Amoebic: no fever, tenesmus present, stool 6–8/day, acid, motile trophozoite with RBCs, Charcot–Leyden crystals. Bacillary: fever, > 10/day, alkaline, motile bacteria.

</div>

## 4. Extra-intestinal amoebiasis

| Site | Features |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **1. Liver abscess** | **commonest extra-intestinal complication**; often **without recent diarrhea**; **right lobe > left**; **right upper (epigastric) pain, malaise, fever, sweats, anorexia, weight loss, right shoulder pain**; **aspirate = chocolate-brown (blood + necrotic liver)**; damage is probably due to **lysosomal enzymes and cytokines of inflammatory cells**, not directly the trophozoite; untreated abscess may **rupture** into lung/pleura, pericardium, peritoneum, stomach, intestine, IVC or skin |
| ⭐ **2. Lung abscess** | **usually secondary to liver abscess** rupturing through the **right diaphragm** → **broncho-hepatic fistula**; **fever, pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, non-productive cough, chocolate-brown sputum** |
| ⭐ **3. Brain abscess** | **hematogenous/lymphatic spread**; usually a **single lesion in the cerebral cortex**; **meningoencephalitis**; **fatal**; most have a coexistent liver abscess |
| **4. Cutaneous abscess** | **perianal**, direct extension; **discharging sinuses**; may be mistaken for **condyloma or epithelioma**; may be **venereal (oral–anal) in homosexual males** |
| **5. Genitourinary** | **renal** (rare); **penile** (prepuce, glans) through **anal intercourse**; vulva/vagina/cervix lesions; **ulcerative lesions resemble carcinoma** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Liver abscess = commonest extra-intestinal; right lobe; chocolate-brown pus; right shoulder pain.
- Lung abscess secondary to liver rupture through the diaphragm: chocolate sputum.
- Brain abscess: fatal single cortical lesion with meningoencephalitis. Cutaneous: perianal. Genitourinary: penile/vulval ulcers resembling carcinoma.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe amoebic liver abscess.</summary>

**Answer:** The commonest extra-intestinal complication, usually in the right hepatic lobe, often without recent diarrhea; presents with right upper abdominal pain, fever, sweats, anorexia, weight loss and right shoulder pain; aspiration gives chocolate-brown pus; untreated abscess may rupture into the lung, pleura, pericardium, peritoneum, stomach, intestine, IVC or skin.

**Why:** Most important extra-intestinal form.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does amoebic lung abscess develop and present?</summary>

**Answer:** Usually secondary to rupture of a hepatic abscess through the right diaphragm into the chest and lung/bronchus (broncho-hepatic fistula), presenting with fever, severe pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, non-productive cough and chocolate-brown sputum.

**Why:** Direct extension from the liver.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Mention the other extra-intestinal sites.</summary>

**Answer:** Brain (single cerebral cortex abscess with meningoencephalitis; fatal), cutaneous perianal abscess (may mimic condyloma) and genitourinary amoebiasis (renal, penile, vulval; resembling carcinoma), as well as spleen, kidney and adrenals.

**Why:** Metastatic amoebiasis.
</details>

## 5. Laboratory diagnosis, treatment and prophylaxis

### Diagnosis of intestinal amoebiasis
- **A. Macroscopic:** stool **semi-liquid, foul-smelling, brownish-black, with blood and mucus; not adherent to the container**.
- **B. Microscopic:** **trophozoite and cyst** with **RBCs and pus cells**.
- **C. Stool culture:** **sensitive for chronic and asymptomatic (carrier) cases**; media: **Diamond's (casein hydrolysate)**, **Dobell & Laidlaw**.
- **D. Serology:** **IHA (indirect hemagglutination), ELISA, latex agglutination**.
- **E. Molecular:** **DNA/antigen detection by probes and PCR** — **more sensitive and specific than microscopy**.

### Diagnosis of extra-intestinal amoebiasis
- **Serological tests are the most important:** **complement fixation test and IHAT**.

### Treatment
| Situation | Drug |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Cyst stage in stool (carriers)** | **furamide (diloxanide furoate) 500 mg orally TID for 10 days**; **metronidazole (Flagyl) = drug of choice**, **800 mg orally q8h for 5 days** or **2 g once daily for 3 days** |
| ⭐ **Trophozoites (dysenteric amoebiasis)** | **dehydroemetine 1–1.5 mg/kg IM once daily for 10 days** |
| ⭐ **Extra-intestinal** | **dehydroemetine + metronidazole (or furamide or chloroquine 300 mg q12h for 2 days)**; add **oxytetracycline** to prevent secondary bacterial infection |

### Prophylaxis
- **As for all fecal–oral infections:** protect **food, water and fingers**; **no fresh uncooked vegetables or unsterilized water**; **treat carriers**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Stool: brownish-black, blood + mucus, non-adherent; microscopy cyst + trophozoite with RBCs/pus; culture (Diamond's, Dobell & Laidlaw); serology (IHA, ELISA, latex); PCR most sensitive.
- Extra-intestinal: serology (complement fixation, IHAT).
- Cysts → diloxanide furoate/metronidazole; trophozoites → dehydroemetine IM; extra-intestinal → dehydroemetine + metronidazole/furamide/chloroquine + oxytetracycline.
- Prevention: hygiene, safe food/water, treat carriers.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Mention the laboratory diagnosis of intestinal amoebiasis.</summary>

**Answer:** Macroscopic stool (semi-liquid, foul, brownish-black, with blood and mucus not adherent to the container); microscopy for trophozoites and cysts with RBCs and pus cells; stool culture (Diamond's or Dobell & Laidlaw media) for chronic and carrier cases; serology (IHA, ELISA, latex agglutination); and PCR/DNA or antigen detection, which is the most sensitive and specific.

</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is E. histolytica infection prevented?</summary>

**Answer:** As for fecal–oral infections: protect food, water and fingers from contamination, avoid raw vegetables and unsterilized water, and treat carriers.

**Why:** Transmission is by cysts in stool.
</details>

- **Cyst (4 nuclei) = infective; trophozoite = invasive but killed by gastric juice.**
- **Excystation in the small intestine; colonization in the large intestine (caecum).**
- **Flask-shaped ulcers stop at the submucosa/muscularis — not the subserosa.**
- **Amoebic: NO fever, tenesmus PRESENT, acid; bacillary: fever, tenesmus absent, alkaline.**

- **Lung abscess is secondary to liver abscess.**
- **Metronidazole = drug of choice; dehydroemetine = IM for trophozoites; diloxanide furoate for carriers.**
- **Only E. histolytica (and Acanthamoeba) are pathogenic amoebae.**
- **Third leading cause of parasitic mortality after malaria and schistosomiasis; discovered by Lösch (1875).**
