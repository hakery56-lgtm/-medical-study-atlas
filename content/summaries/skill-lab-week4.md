## 1. Elbow anatomy and examination sequence

- **Objectives:** **surface anatomy of the elbow, focused history of elbow pain, physical examination of the elbow, ulnar nerve examination, clinical reasoning**.
- **Elbow joint:** **humeroulnar, radiocapitellar, superior radioulnar** articulations.
- **Medial epicondyle = common flexor origin; lateral epicondyle = common extensor origin.**
- **Medial epicondyle, lateral epicondyle and olecranon tip:** **equilateral triangle at 90° flexion; straight line in full extension.**

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Look** | alignment of the extended elbow; **carrying angle = valgus 11–13° in full extension** (compare both sides) |
| **Feel** | **synovitis swelling between the lateral epicondyle and olecranon (blocks full extension)**; **psoriasis skin changes**; **rheumatoid nodules on the proximal extensor forearm / olecranon bursa**; bony contours of the epicondyles and olecranon; **olecranon bursa swelling, nodules, tophi** |
| **Move** | **extension–flexion arc:** touch the same-side shoulder then straighten the elbow; **supination/pronation:** elbows at the sides flexed 90°, palms up then down |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Three articulations; epicondyles + olecranon = triangle (90°) / line (extension).
- Carrying angle 11–13° valgus; Look → Feel → Move.
- Synovitis blocks extension; nodules/tophi/bursa on the extensor side.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the surface anatomy of the elbow.</summary>

**Answer:** The elbow has humeroulnar, radiocapitellar and superior radioulnar articulations; the medial and lateral epicondyles are the common flexor and extensor origins, and together with the olecranon tip they form an equilateral triangle at 90° of flexion and a straight line in full extension.

**Why:** Slide "The physical examination".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the carrying angle and how is it examined?</summary>

**Answer:** The normal valgus angle of 11–13° with the elbow fully extended; the patient extends both elbows and the two sides are compared so that small differences are obvious.

**Why:** "Look" step.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the "Move" part of the elbow examination.</summary>

**Answer:** Assess the extension–flexion arc by asking the patient to touch the same-side shoulder and then straighten the elbow as far as possible, and assess supination and pronation with the elbows at the sides flexed to 90°, turning the palms up then down.

**Why:** "Move" step.
</details>

## 2. Testing the nerves

| Nerve | Motor | Sensory |
|---|---|---|
| **Median** | wrist flexion and abduction; thumb and index finger flexion; thumb opposition | palmar thumb, index and middle finger, radial half of ring finger |
| **Ulnar** | wrist flexion and adduction; **finger spread**; flexion of the distal phalanx of the fifth digit | ulnar side of ring finger + little finger and associated dorsal hand |
| **Radial** | wrist extension; supination; thumb extension | dorsal web space between thumb and index finger + dorsal hand (excluding ulnar territory) |

- **Quick motor tests:** **"OK" sign → anterior interosseous nerve (branch of median) and radial nerve**; **ulnar nerve → finger spread against resistance or holding paper between middle and ring fingers**; **radial nerve → thumb's up sign**.
- **Sensory test sites:** **dorsal web space (radial), palmar index finger (median), little finger (ulnar).**
- **OK sign:** tests **DIP flexion and the strength of flexor pollicis longus and flexor digitorum profundus**; **AIN deficit → flattening at the DIP joint and weak apposition**.
- **Paper test:** examiner pulls the paper; tests the **lumbricals supplied by the ulnar nerve**.
- ⚠️ **Suspected displaced supracondylar fracture of the humerus → do not move the elbow without stabilization.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- OK sign = anterior interosseous (median) nerve; finger spread/paper = ulnar; thumb's up = radial.
- Sensory sites: dorsal web (radial), index pulp (median), little finger (ulnar).
- Don't move a suspected supracondylar fracture unstabilized.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give the motor and sensory functions of the median, ulnar and radial nerves.</summary>

**Answer:** Median: wrist flexion/abduction, thumb and index flexion, thumb opposition; sensation of the palmar thumb, index, middle and radial half of the ring finger. Ulnar: wrist flexion/adduction, finger spread, flexion of the fifth distal phalanx; sensation of the ulnar ring finger and little finger. Radial: wrist extension, supination, thumb extension; sensation of the dorsal web space and dorsal hand.

**Why:** Nerve table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do you test the anterior interosseous nerve?</summary>

**Answer:** With the OK sign: the patient forms the sign and keeps it against resistance; in a deficit the DIP joints of the thumb and index finger flatten (weak flexor pollicis longus and flexor digitorum profundus) and apposition is weak.

**Why:** OK sign slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do you test the ulnar nerve motor function quickly?</summary>

**Answer:** Finger spread against resistance, or holding a piece of paper firmly between the middle and ring fingers while the examiner pulls it (lumbricals supplied by the ulnar nerve).

**Why:** Ulnar nerve test.
</details>

## 3. Case scenario 2: child with a painful elbow

- **Patient:** **6-year-old girl, left elbow pain for 1 hour**, **fell off a trampoline onto an outstretched hand**; **constant, sharp, severe (9/10), progressive**; **swelling and bluish discoloration**; no past history.
- **Examination:** **S-shaped deformity, marked swelling and ecchymosis; marked tenderness over the medial and lateral epicondyles; reluctant to move the elbow; weak brachial and radial pulses; sensory loss over the palmar index finger; flattening of the thumb and index DIP flexion (abnormal OK sign).**
- **Tasks:** **history, examination of the left elbow, differential diagnosis.**
- **Interpretation (clinical reasoning):** FOOSH in a child with an S-shaped elbow deformity and X-ray of a displaced distal humerus fracture → **displaced supracondylar fracture of the humerus** with **brachial artery compromise (weak pulses)** and **anterior interosseous (median) nerve injury** (*diagnosis inferred from the findings; the slide leaves it as a task*).
- **Structures at risk:** anteromedial fractures — **brachial artery and median nerve**; anterolateral fractures — **radial nerve** (with brachial artery and median nerve nearby).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- FOOSH in a child + S-shaped elbow + weak pulses + abnormal OK sign = displaced supracondylar fracture with neurovascular compromise.
- Median (AIN) nerve: abnormal OK sign, index pulp sensory loss; brachial artery: weak pulses.
- Stabilize the elbow; urgent assessment.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the findings of case scenario 2 and the nerve and vessel involved.</summary>

**Answer:** A 6-year-old girl with an S-shaped, swollen, ecchymotic elbow after a fall on an outstretched hand, tender over the epicondyles, weak brachial and radial pulses (brachial artery compromise), sensory loss over the palmar index finger and an abnormal OK sign with flattened thumb and index DIP flexion (anterior interosseous branch of the median nerve).

**Why:** SP instructions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the probable diagnosis and key precaution?</summary>

**Answer:** A displaced supracondylar fracture of the humerus with neurovascular compromise; do not move the elbow without stabilization.

**Why:** Clinical reasoning and the red warning on the nerve-testing slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which structures are at risk in anteromedial and anterolateral humeral fractures?</summary>

**Answer:** Anteromedial: brachial artery and median nerve; anterolateral: radial nerve (with the brachial artery and median nerve nearby).

**Why:** Figure of humeral fractures.
</details>
