## 1. The circulatory system & the vascular wall

- The **circulatory system** pumps and directs blood cells and substances carried in the blood to all tissues; it includes **the blood and lymphatic vascular systems**.
- In an adult the total length of its vessels is estimated at **100,000–150,000 km**.
- The **blood vascular (cardiovascular) system** = **heart + blood vessels**.
- At rest: **≈ 70% of the blood is in the systemic circulation**, **≈ 18% in the pulmonary circulation** and **12% in the heart**.

### Layers of the vessel wall (all vessels except capillaries)
| Layer | Position | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Tunica intima** | **inner** | endothelium + subendothelium (+ internal elastic lamina) |
| ⭐ **Tunica media** | **middle** | **structure related directly to the function** of the vessel |
| ⭐ **Tunica adventitia (externa)** | **outer** | connective tissue |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Circulatory system = blood + lymphatic vascular systems; adult vessels 100,000–150,000 km.
- At rest: systemic ≈ 70%, pulmonary ≈ 18%, heart 12%.
- Wall of all vessels except capillaries: intima (inner), media (middle), adventitia/externa (outer).

</div>

## 2. Tunica intima, endothelium & thrombosis

### Components
- **A. Endothelium:** cells are **squamous, polygonal and elongated, with the long axis in the direction of blood flow**; **rest on a basal lamina**; **joined by occluding junctions**.
- **B. Subendothelium:** **loose fibro-elastic connective tissue**; in **muscular arteries** the intima has a thin **internal elastic lamina (elastin)** with **holes** that allow **better diffusion** of substances from the blood into the wall.

### Functions of the endothelium (a special simple squamous epithelium)
1. **Semipermeable barrier** between **blood plasma** and **interstitial tissue fluid**.
2. Production of **vasoactive factors** affecting vascular tone: **nitric oxide, endothelins, vasoconstrictive agents**.
3. **Conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II.**
4. **Weibel–Palade bodies** — unique very small elongated vesicles containing **selectin and von Willebrand factor** (blood coagulation).
5. ⭐ **Antithrombogenic action** (prevents coagulation).

### Thrombosis & embolism (medical application)
1. When the endothelium is **damaged** (e.g. by **atherosclerosis**), the **uncovered subendothelial connective tissue** induces **platelet aggregation**.
2. This produces **fibrin from circulating fibrinogen**.
3. An intravascular clot = **thrombus**, which may grow until it **completely obstructs the flow**.
4. **Emboli** = solid masses that **detach** from the thrombus and travel to obstruct **distant vessels** — potentially life-threatening.
- **Artificial heart valves** lack a complete endothelial covering → patients need **exogenous anticoagulants**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Endothelium: squamous cells elongated along the flow, on a basal lamina, joined by occluding junctions.
- Functions: semipermeable barrier, vasoactive factors (NO, endothelins), angiotensin I → II, Weibel–Palade bodies (selectin, vWF), antithrombogenic.
- Damaged endothelium → platelets → fibrin → thrombus → embolus; artificial valves need anticoagulants.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the tunica intima.</summary>

**Answer:** It consists of the endothelium — squamous, polygonal, elongated cells with their long axis in the direction of blood flow, resting on a basal lamina and joined by occluding junctions — and the subendothelium of loose fibro-elastic connective tissue. In muscular arteries the intima also includes an internal elastic lamina of elastin with holes that improve diffusion into the wall.

**Why:** Endothelium + subendothelium + internal elastic lamina.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. List the functions of the vascular endothelium.</summary>

**Answer:** It is a semipermeable barrier between plasma and interstitial fluid; it produces vasoactive factors (nitric oxide, endothelins and vasoconstrictors); it converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II; its Weibel–Palade bodies store selectin and von Willebrand factor for coagulation; and it has an antithrombogenic action preventing blood coagulation.

**Why:** Five functions.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Explain how thrombosis and embolism occur and why patients with artificial valves need anticoagulants.</summary>

**Answer:** Damage to the endothelium (e.g. by atherosclerotic lesions) exposes subendothelial connective tissue, which causes platelet aggregation and conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin, forming a thrombus that can obstruct the vessel; pieces (emboli) may detach and block distant vessels. Artificial valves lack a complete endothelial covering, so the patient needs exogenous anticoagulants to prevent thrombus formation.

**Why:** A clinical chain of events.
</details>

## 3. Tunica media & tunica adventitia

### Tunica media
- **A. Concentric layers of helically arranged smooth muscle cells**; **B. elastic fibers**; **C. reticular fibers**; **D. extracellular matrix of proteoglycans** — **all produced by the smooth muscle cells**.
- In arteries the media may have an **external elastic lamina** separating it from the adventitia.

### Tunica adventitia
- **A. Fibroelastic connective tissue** — mainly **type I collagen** and **elastic fibers arranged longitudinally**.
- **B. Fibroblasts.**
- **C. Vasa vasorum ("vessels of the vessel")** — arterioles, capillaries, venules in the adventitia and outer media; **nourish the vessel wall**.
- **D. Nervi vasorum** — **vasomotor autonomic nerves**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Media: helical smooth muscle + elastic fibers + reticular fibers + proteoglycans (all made by smooth muscle cells); external elastic lamina in arteries.
- Adventitia: type I collagen and longitudinal elastic fibers, fibroblasts, vasa vasorum (nutrition), nervi vasorum (autonomic vasomotor nerves).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the tunica media.</summary>

**Answer:** It is formed of concentric layers of helically arranged smooth muscle cells, elastic fibers, reticular fibers and an extracellular matrix of proteoglycans, all produced by the smooth muscle cells; in arteries it may have an external elastic lamina separating it from the adventitia.

**Why:** Four components, one cell source.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the tunica adventitia.</summary>

**Answer:** Fibroelastic connective tissue made mainly of type I collagen and longitudinally arranged elastic fibers, with fibroblasts, vasa vasorum and nervi vasorum.

**Why:** Four components.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are vasa vasorum and nervi vasorum?</summary>

**Answer:** Vasa vasorum ("vessels of the vessel") are arterioles, capillaries and venules in the adventitia and outer media that nourish the wall of large vessels; nervi vasorum are the autonomic vasomotor nerves.

**Why:** Nutrition + innervation of the vessel wall.
</details>

## 4. Arteries

### 1) Large elastic arteries (aorta and its large branches; pulmonary arteries)
| Layer | % of wall | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Intima** | **10%** | thicker than in muscular arteries; subendothelium also has **a few smooth muscle cells and macrophages** |
| ⭐ **Media** | **70%** | **elastic fibers + concentric, perforated elastic laminae** — number ↑ with age (**≈ 40 in the newborn, ≈ 70 in the adult**); between laminae: smooth muscle cells and reticular fibers |
| **Adventitia** | **20%** | **numerous vasa vasorum** |

### Atherosclerosis
- A disease of **elastic arteries and large muscular arteries** that may play a role in **nearly half of all deaths in developed countries**.
- **Initiated by damaged endothelial cells oxidizing LDL** in the tunica intima → **adhesion and intimal entry of macrophages**.
- **Lipid-filled macrophages = foam cells** → with free LDL form **fatty streaks** (earliest sign).
- Progress to **fibro-fatty atheromas**: gruel-like mix of smooth muscle cells, collagen, lymphocytes + necrotic lipid, debris and foam cells.
- **Elastic arteries:** localized wall destruction → weakening → **aneurysms** (may rupture).
- **Muscular arteries (e.g. coronary):** atheromas **occlude** flow → **ischemic heart disease**.

### 2) Muscular arteries (e.g. femoral, ulnar)
- **Intima:** very thin subendothelium; **prominent internal elastic lamina**.
- **Media:** up to **40 layers** of prominent smooth muscle cells; **external elastic lamina only in the larger ones**; **50% of the wall**.
- **Adventitia:** connective tissue, lymphatic capillaries, vasa vasorum, nervi vasorum; **50% of the wall**.

### 3) Arterioles
- **Control blood flow into the capillaries**; **< 0.1 mm** in diameter, **lumen ≈ as wide as the wall is thick**.
- Very thin subendothelium, **no elastic laminae**; **media = 1–3 layers of circular smooth muscle**; very thin adventitia.
- **Blood pressure = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance**, which is mostly due to the **arterioles**; **essential hypertension** results from mechanisms increasing arteriolar constriction.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Elastic artery: intima 10%, media 70% (elastic laminae 40 → 70), adventitia 20% with many vasa vasorum.
- Muscular artery: media (up to 40 smooth muscle layers) ≈ 50%, adventitia ≈ 50%; prominent internal elastic lamina.
- Arteriole: < 0.1 mm, 1–3 smooth muscle layers, no elastic laminae; main site of peripheral resistance.
- Atherosclerosis: endothelial injury → oxidized LDL → macrophages → foam cells → fatty streak → atheroma → aneurysm (elastic) or ischemia (muscular).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of a large elastic artery.</summary>

**Answer:** The intima forms 10% of the wall and has a few smooth muscle cells and macrophages in the subendothelium. The media forms 70% and consists of elastic fibers and concentrically arranged perforated elastic laminae (about 40 in the newborn, 70 in the adult) with smooth muscle cells and reticular fibers between them. The adventitia forms 20% with numerous vasa vasorum.

**Why:** 10 / 70 / 20 rule.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the pathogenesis and consequences of atherosclerosis.</summary>

**Answer:** Damaged endothelial cells oxidize LDL in the intima, attracting macrophages that engulf it and become foam cells; foam cells and free LDL form fatty streaks, which progress to fibro-fatty atheromas (smooth muscle cells, collagen, lymphocytes and a necrotic core). In elastic arteries atheromas destroy the wall, causing aneurysms that can rupture; in muscular arteries such as the coronaries they occlude blood flow, causing ischemic heart disease.

**Why:** Steps + two outcomes.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare muscular arteries and arterioles.</summary>

**Answer:** Muscular arteries (e.g. femoral, ulnar) have a prominent internal elastic lamina, a media with up to 40 smooth muscle layers (and an external elastic lamina in larger ones) and a thick adventitia. Arterioles (< 0.1 mm) have a very thin subendothelium, no elastic laminae, a media of 1–3 circular smooth muscle layers and a very thin adventitia; they control flow into the capillaries and provide most of the peripheral resistance (hypertension).

**Why:** Layers of smooth muscle: 40 vs 1–3.
</details>

## 5. Capillaries & pericytes

### Capillaries
- **Smallest blood vessels** — allow metabolic exchange between blood and tissues.
- **A single layer of endothelial cells rolled into a tube**, resting on a **basal lamina that splits to enclose the pericytes**.
- **Diameter 4–10 µm**; **individual length usually ≤ 50 µm**; **> 90% of all blood vessels**, **total length > 100,000 km**, **total surface area ≈ 5000 m²**.
- **Total cross-sectional diameter ≈ 800 × that of the aorta**.
- **Blood velocity:** **aorta ≈ 320 mm/s** vs **capillaries ≈ 0.3 mm/s** → favourable for exchange.
- **Diabetic microangiopathy:** high blood sugar → **diffuse thickening of capillary basal laminae** → ↓ metabolic exchange, especially in the **kidneys, retina, skeletal muscle and skin**.

### Pericytes
- **Perivascular cells of mesenchymal origin** with long processes partly surrounding the endothelium of **capillaries and postcapillary venules**, enclosed in the **basal lamina**.
- Functions: (a) mainly **contractile**; (b) **support** of capillaries and postcapillary venules; (c) after injury they **proliferate and differentiate to form new vessels**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Capillary: endothelial tube on a basal lamina enclosing pericytes; diameter 4–10 µm; > 90% of vessels; surface ≈ 5000 m²; flow 0.3 mm/s (aorta 320 mm/s).
- Diabetic microangiopathy: thickened capillary basal lamina → ↓ exchange (kidney, retina, muscle, skin).
- Pericytes: contractile, supportive, can form new vessels.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure and dimensions of capillaries.</summary>

**Answer:** Capillaries are the smallest vessels, formed of a single layer of endothelial cells rolled into a tube resting on a basal lamina that splits to enclose pericytes. Their diameter is 4–10 µm and their individual length usually under 50 µm; they make up over 90% of all vessels with a total length of more than 100,000 km and a surface area of about 5000 m².

**Why:** Structure + numbers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why are capillaries a favourable site for exchange?</summary>

**Answer:** Because of their thin walls, slow blood flow (about 0.3 mm/s compared with 320 mm/s in the aorta, as their total diameter is about 800 times that of the aorta), extensive surface area and slow pulsatile flow.

**Why:** Four reasons.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are pericytes and diabetic microangiopathy?</summary>

**Answer:** Pericytes are perivascular cells of mesenchymal origin with long processes around capillaries and postcapillary venules, enclosed in the basal lamina; they are contractile, support the vessels and form new vessels after injury. Diabetic microangiopathy is a diffuse thickening of capillary basal laminae due to high blood sugar, with decreased metabolic exchange especially in the kidneys, retina, skeletal muscle and skin.

**Why:** Two definitions.
</details>

## 6. Types of capillaries

| Type | Features | Locations |
|---|---|---|
| ⭐ **Continuous** (most common) | **tight (occluding) junctions with overlapping marginal folds** seal the clefts → minimal leakage; **continuous basal lamina**; exchange by **diffusion or transcytosis**; **many pinocytotic vesicles**; take part in the **blood barriers (blood–brain, blood–air, blood–thymus)** | **muscle, connective tissue, fat, exocrine glands, lung, nervous tissue** |
| ⭐ **Fenestrated** | tight junctions and **continuous basal lamina** but **fenestrations (perforations)** through the endothelial cells → **sieve-like** → more exchange; some fenestrae covered by thin **diaphragms of proteoglycans** | **intestinal villi/wall, endocrine glands, kidney glomeruli, choroid plexus** |
| ⭐ **Sinusoids (discontinuous)** | **large diameter (30–40 µm)**, irregular outline; **large fenestrations without diaphragms**, **wide intercellular gaps**; **discontinuous basal lamina**; **macrophages** associated; allow **maximal exchange of macromolecules and cells** | **liver, bone marrow, spleen** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Continuous: most common; tight junctions, continuous basal lamina, pinocytotic vesicles; muscle, nervous system, lung; blood barriers.
- Fenestrated: fenestrations with diaphragms, continuous basal lamina; kidney glomeruli, intestine, endocrine glands, choroid plexus.
- Sinusoid: 30–40 µm, large fenestrations without diaphragms, discontinuous basal lamina; liver, bone marrow, spleen.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify capillaries with the location of each type.</summary>

**Answer:** Continuous capillaries (muscle, connective tissue, fat, exocrine glands, lung, nervous tissue); fenestrated capillaries (intestinal villi, endocrine glands, kidney glomeruli, choroid plexus); sinusoids or discontinuous capillaries (liver, bone marrow, spleen).

**Why:** Three types × sites.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe continuous capillaries.</summary>

**Answer:** The most common type: endothelial cells are sealed by tight occluding junctions with overlapping marginal folds, producing minimal leakage; the basal lamina is continuous; molecules cross the cells by diffusion or transcytosis, and the cytoplasm contains many pinocytotic vesicles. They provide metabolic exchange and contribute to blood barriers (blood–brain, blood–air, blood–thymus).

**Why:** Structure + exchange + barriers.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare fenestrated capillaries and sinusoids.</summary>

**Answer:** Fenestrated capillaries have tight junctions and a continuous basal lamina, with small circular fenestrations (some with diaphragms) allowing more exchange (kidney, intestine, endocrine glands). Sinusoids are wider (30–40 µm) with large fenestrations without diaphragms, wide intercellular gaps and a discontinuous basal lamina, with associated macrophages, allowing maximal exchange of macromolecules and cells (liver, bone marrow, spleen).

**Why:** Diaphragm + basal lamina differences.
</details>

- **Foam cells = lipid-filled macrophages** (not smooth muscle cells).
- **Weibel–Palade bodies** store **selectin and von Willebrand factor**.
- **Atherosclerosis → aneurysm in elastic arteries; occlusion in muscular arteries.**
