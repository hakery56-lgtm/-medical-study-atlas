## 1. Dysphagia

- ⭐ **Dysphagia** = **difficulty swallowing**; may coexist with heartburn or vomiting.
- **Globus sensation** = anxious people feel a **lump in the throat** without organic cause. **Odynophagia** = **pain on swallowing**, usually **esophagitis** (reflux or candidiasis).
- **Oropharyngeal:** **neuromuscular dysfunction** of initiation of swallowing (pharynx, upper esophageal sphincter); signs: **drooling, dysarthria, hoarseness, cranial nerve or neurological signs.**
- **Esophageal:** **structural** (benign or malignant **strictures**) and **dysmotility.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Dysphagia ≠ globus ≠ odynophagia.
- Oropharyngeal = neuromuscular; esophageal = stricture or dysmotility.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Differentiate dysphagia, odynophagia and globus.</summary>

**Answer:** Dysphagia is difficulty swallowing; odynophagia is pain on swallowing (usually esophagitis); globus is a lump in the throat felt by anxious people without organic cause.

**Why:** Slide "Dysphagia".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe oropharyngeal dysphagia.</summary>

**Answer:** Neuromuscular dysfunction affecting the initiation of swallowing by the pharynx and upper esophageal sphincter, with drooling, dysarthria, hoarseness and cranial nerve or neurological signs.

**Why:** Slide 2.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are esophageal causes?</summary>

**Answer:** Structural disease (benign or malignant strictures) and dysmotility.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

## 2. Dyspepsia, vomiting, heartburn and regurgitation

- ⭐ **Dyspepsia** = collective term for symptoms from the **upper GI tract** (bloating, nausea), including disorders outside the digestive system. A careful history **elicits classic disorders (peptic ulcer), detects alarm features, and detects atypical symptoms (myocardial ischemia).**
- **Vomiting:** with nausea, retching, salivation, anorexia, dyspepsia; **distinguish from regurgitation**; acute vs chronic; seek abdominal pain, fever, diarrhea, food relation, drugs, headache, vertigo, weight loss.
- **Heartburn:** **retrosternal burning** rising to the chest, ± regurgitation of acid or bitter fluid; **after meals, lying down, bending, straining, heavy lifting**; up to **50%** present atypically (chest pain, belching, halitosis, **chronic cough**, sore throat).
- **Management:** **young + typical + good response** → **no investigation**; **> 55 years or alarm or atypical features** → **urgent endoscopy.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Alarm features and age > 55 need urgent endoscopy.
- Heartburn can present as chronic cough or chest pain.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why take a careful history in dyspepsia?</summary>

**Answer:** To elicit classical symptoms of disorders such as peptic ulcer, to detect alarm features needing urgent investigation, and to detect atypical symptoms suggesting other disorders such as myocardial ischemia.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define heartburn and its triggers.</summary>

**Answer:** Retrosternal burning discomfort, sometimes with acid or bitter regurgitation, occurring after meals, on lying down or with bending, straining or heavy lifting.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When is endoscopy required?</summary>

**Answer:** Urgently in patients over 55 and in those with alarm symptoms or atypical features; not in young patients with typical symptoms responding to diet, antacids or acid suppression.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

## 3. Diarrhea

- ⭐ **Diarrhea** = **> 200 g stool/day**; urgency is the most severe symptom; fecal incontinence is common.
- **Acute:** **fecal-oral** bacteria, toxins, viruses, parasites; short-lived; **> 10 days rarely infective**; drugs (**antibiotics, cytotoxics, PPIs, NSAIDs**).
- **Chronic or relapsing:** commonest = **IBS**: frequent loose, watery or pellety stools; **rarely nocturnal, worst before and after breakfast**; otherwise **constipated**; **mucus but never blood**; **< 200 g/day.**
- **Chronic diarrhea categories:** **colon, small bowel, malabsorption**; many diagnosed by presentation, stool exam, blood tests, imaging; **negative investigations usually mean IBS**, but some have organic disease.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Diarrhea > 200 g/day; > 10 days = rarely infective.
- IBS: mucus, never blood, < 200 g/day, not at night.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define diarrhea and name causes of the acute form.</summary>

**Answer:** More than 200 g of stool daily; acute diarrhea is usually fecal-oral infection by bacteria, toxins, viruses or parasites, or drugs (antibiotics, cytotoxics, PPIs, NSAIDs).

**Why:** Slides "Diarrhea" and "Acute Diarrhea".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the diarrhea of IBS.</summary>

**Answer:** Frequent loose, watery or pellety stools, rare at night and worst before and after breakfast, alternating with constipation, with mucus but never blood and a 24-hour volume under 200 g.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is chronic diarrhea categorized?</summary>

**Answer:** Colon disease, small bowel disease or malabsorption.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

## 4. Weight loss

- ⭐ **Significant weight loss = > 3 kg over 6 months.**
- **Physiological:** dieting, exercise, starvation, old age (dietitian help for older patients).
- **Psychiatric:** anorexia nervosa, bulimia, affective disorders, **alcoholism**, **depression.**
- **Systemic:** chronic infections (**TB**, recurrent UTI or chest infection, parasites), **HIV**, disseminated **malignancy** (late), **rheumatoid arthritis, polymyalgia rheumatica.**
- **GI:** **dysphagia and gastric outlet obstruction** (↓ intake), **malignancy**, **malabsorption** (pancreatic, small bowel), **Crohn's and ulcerative colitis.**
- **Metabolic/others:** metabolic disorders, **end-stage respiratory and cardiac disease.**
- **Investigations:** **urinalysis, blood tests (LFTs, glucose, TFTs), CRP, ESR, fecal calprotectin**; invasive tests (**bone marrow, liver biopsy** for cryptic miliary TB) or **CT** rarely; first **revisit history and reweigh.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- > 3 kg in 6 months is significant.
- Four pathological groups: psychiatric, systemic, GI, metabolic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define significant weight loss and list pathological causes.</summary>

**Answer:** More than 3 kg over 6 months; psychiatric, systemic, gastrointestinal and metabolic or advanced organ disease.

**Why:** Slide "Weight Loss".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does GI disease cause weight loss?</summary>

**Answer:** Dysphagia and outlet obstruction reduce intake; malignancy acts by obstruction, anorexia or cytokines; malabsorption causes profound loss with deficiencies; Crohn's and ulcerative colitis cause anorexia, fear of eating and loss of nutrients.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the investigations.</summary>

**Answer:** Urinalysis, blood tests including LFTs, glucose and thyroid function, CRP, ESR and fecal calprotectin; rarely bone marrow aspiration, liver biopsy or CT.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

## 5. Malabsorption: signs and blood tests

| Sign | Deficiency |
|---|---|
| **Night blindness, follicular hyperkeratosis** | **Vitamin A** |
| **Anemia; angular stomatitis, glossitis** | **Iron, folate, B12** |
| **Bleeding gums** | **Vitamin C** |
| **Purpura and bruising** | **Vitamins C and K** |
| **Poor wound healing** | **Vitamin C, protein, zinc** |
| **Acrodermatitis enteropathica** | **Zinc** |
| **Koilonychia** | **Iron** |
| **Paraesthesia, tetany** | **Calcium, magnesium** |
| **Osteomalacia, rickets** | **Calcium, vitamin D** |
| **Muscle wasting** | **Protein** |
| **Proximal myopathy** | **Vitamin D** |
| **Peripheral neuropathy** | **B12** |
| **Peripheral edema** | **Hypoalbuminemia** |

- **Blood tests:** **microcytic anemia (iron); macrocytic anemia (folate or B12); ↑ prothrombin time (vitamin K); hypoalbuminemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, low zinc.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Match each sign to its vitamin or mineral.
- ↑ PT = vitamin K; macrocytic = folate/B12.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Which sign suggests vitamin A deficiency?</summary>

**Answer:** Night blindness and follicular hyperkeratosis.

**Why:** Malabsorption figure.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the blood test abnormalities of malabsorption.</summary>

**Answer:** Microcytic anemia (iron), macrocytic anemia (folate or B12), increased prothrombin time (vitamin K), hypoalbuminemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia and low zinc.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which deficiencies cause bruising and bleeding gums?</summary>

**Answer:** Vitamins C and K (purpura and bruising); vitamin C (bleeding gums).

**Why:** Malabsorption figure.
</details>

## 6. Constipation

- ⭐ **Constipation** = **infrequent passage of hard stools**, with straining, incomplete evacuation, perianal or abdominal discomfort.
- **Onset matters:** **neonatal → Hirschsprung's disease**; **recent change in middle age → organic disorder, colonic carcinoma.** Ask about **rectal bleeding, pain, weight loss, straining, IBS symptoms, childhood constipation, emotional distress.**
- **Examination:** general medical disorders, **obstruction**, **neurological (spinal cord) lesions**, **perineal and rectal examination** (pelvic floor, anal canal, rectum: masses, impaction, prolapse). **Examination contributes more than extensive investigation.**
- **Management:** **fluids, fiber, exercise, judicious laxatives**; **middle-aged or elderly with a short history or worrying symptoms → barium enema or colonoscopy.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Neonatal onset = Hirschsprung; middle-age change = think carcinoma.
- Not everyone needs investigation.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define constipation and its associated complaints.</summary>

**Answer:** Infrequent passage of hard stools, often with straining, a sense of incomplete evacuation and perianal or abdominal discomfort.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which features raise suspicion of organic disease?</summary>

**Answer:** Neonatal onset (Hirschsprung's), recent change in bowel habit in middle age (colonic carcinoma), rectal bleeding, pain and weight loss.

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is constipation managed?</summary>

**Answer:** Most respond to fluids, fiber, exercise and laxatives; middle-aged or elderly patients with a short history or worrying symptoms need prompt barium enema or colonoscopy.

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

## 7. Abdominal pain: types and acute pain

- ⭐ **Four types:** **visceral** (sensitive to **distension, contraction, twisting, stretching**; unpaired structures → **midline**), **parietal** (somatic nerves; **sharp, well-localized, lateralized**), **referred** (gallbladder → back or shoulder tip), **psychogenic** (depression, somatization).
- **Acute abdominal pain causes:** **inflammation** (appendicitis, diverticulitis, cholecystitis, PID, pancreatitis, pyelonephritis, abscess); **perforation/rupture** (peptic ulcer, diverticular disease, ovarian cyst, **aortic aneurysm**); **obstruction** (intestinal obstruction, biliary colic, ureteric colic).
- **Algorithm:** **peritonitis → resuscitation → laparotomy.** Otherwise: **blood tests (↑ amylase/lipase = pancreatitis) → erect chest X-ray (free air = perforation) → abdominal X-ray (dilated loops = obstruction) → ultrasound (cholecystitis) → contrast radiology → CT**; if inconclusive: **observe; persistent → laparoscopy.**
- **Regional causes:** **RUQ** gallstones, cholangitis, hepatitis; **epigastric** ulcer, pancreatitis; **LUQ** spleen; **umbilical** early appendicitis; **RLQ** appendicitis, Crohn's, ectopic; **LLQ** diverticulitis, colitis; **pelvic** cystitis, fibroid, PID.
- **Exam findings:** **spleen** (can't get above, notch, moves to the RIF) vs **kidney** (ballotable, resonant); **peritonitis** (guarding, rebound, absent bowel sounds, rigidity) vs **obstruction** (distension, tinkling sounds, visible peristalsis); masses by region.
- **Old age:** **blunted pain**, **atypical (delirium, collapse)**, **cancer more common > 70**, non-specific diverticulitis presentations; surgical outcome depends on **frailty**, not age.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Four pain types.
- Peritonitis goes straight to resuscitation and laparotomy.
- Elderly present atypically.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare visceral and parietal pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Visceral pain arises from distension, contraction, twisting or stretching of gut organs and is often midline; parietal pain comes from somatic innervation of the peritoneum and is sharp, well localized and lateralized.

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Outline the algorithm for acute abdominal pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Peritonitis: resuscitation then laparotomy. Otherwise blood tests, erect chest X-ray, abdominal X-ray, ultrasound, contrast radiology and CT in turn; if inconclusive observe, and laparoscopy if symptoms persist.

**Why:** Slide 12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does acute abdominal pain differ in old age?</summary>

**Answer:** Severity and localization are blunted, presentation may be atypical (delirium, collapse), cancer is more common over 70, and diverticulitis may be non-specific; outcome depends on frailty rather than chronological age.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

## 8. Chronic, recurrent and constant abdominal pain

- **Assessment:** demeanor, mood, **weight loss, fever, jaundice, anemia**; **detailed pain history**; if abdominal and rectal exams are normal look at the **vertebral column, spinal cord, lungs, cardiovascular system.**
- **Investigations by pattern:** **endoscopy + ultrasound** (epigastric pain, dyspepsia, gallbladder symptoms); **colonoscopy** (altered bowel habit, rectal bleeding, obstruction); **CT or MR angiography** (food-provoked pain with atherosclerosis = **mesenteric ischemia**); **ultrasound, CT, fecal elastase** (upper abdominal pain radiating to the back); **CT KUB** (loin-to-flank pain with urinary symptoms = stones).
- **IBS:** young, **pain relieved by defecation, bloating, alternating bowel habit**; **blood tests, fecal calprotectin, sigmoidoscopy** suffice without bleeding, weight loss or abnormal findings.
- **Alcohol, weight loss, diarrhea** → **chronic pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer.**
- **Psychological origin** if past psychiatric history, repeated negative tests, vague symptoms; **don't miss acute intermittent porphyria.**
- **Extra-intestinal causes:** **retroperitoneal, psychogenic, locomotor, metabolic/endocrine, drugs/toxins, hematological (sickle cell), neurological.**
- **Constant pain:** some have **chronic functional abdominal pain** (abnormal CNS processing of normal visceral input): aim for **symptom control**; **pain clinics** with psychological support and **tricyclics, gabapentin/pregabalin, ketamine, opioids.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pick the test by pain pattern.
- Functional pain: control symptoms, specialized pain clinic.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Match investigations to presentations of chronic pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Endoscopy and ultrasound for epigastric pain or dyspepsia; colonoscopy for altered bowel habit or bleeding; CT/MR angiography for food-provoked pain with atherosclerosis; ultrasound, CT and fecal elastase for pain radiating to the back; CT KUB for loin pain with urinary symptoms.

**Why:** Slides 14-15.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What suggests IBS and what tests are enough?</summary>

**Answer:** Young patient with pain relieved by defecation, bloating and alternating bowel habit; blood tests, fecal calprotectin and sigmoidoscopy suffice without rectal bleeding, weight loss or abnormal findings.

**Why:** Slide 14.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is chronic functional abdominal pain and how is it managed?</summary>

**Answer:** Constant pain without a cause despite investigation, due to abnormal CNS processing of normal visceral afferent input with psychosocial factors; managed in pain clinics with psychological support and drugs such as tricyclics, gabapentin or pregabalin, ketamine and opioids.

**Why:** Slide 15.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **IBS stool has mucus but NEVER blood.**
- **> 200 g/day = diarrhea; IBS < 200 g/day.**
- **Diarrhea > 10 days rarely infective.**
- **Neonatal constipation = Hirschsprung's.**
- **Peritonitis → laparotomy, not more tests.**
- **> 55 or alarm features → urgent endoscopy.**
- **Vitamin K = ↑ prothrombin time.**
