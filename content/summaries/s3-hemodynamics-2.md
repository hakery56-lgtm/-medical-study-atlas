## 1. Embolism and pulmonary thromboembolism

- ⭐ **Embolus** = detached intravascular **solid, liquid or gaseous mass** carried by blood to a distant site where it often causes **dysfunction or infarction.** Most are **dislodged thrombi**; others: **fat, air/nitrogen, cholesterol (atherosclerotic debris), tumor, bone marrow, amniotic fluid.**
- Systemic embolization → **infarction** downstream; pulmonary embolization → **hypoxia, hypotension, right-sided heart failure.**
- **Pulmonary thromboembolism:** **> 95% from deep leg vein thrombi (DVT) above the popliteal fossa** → right heart → pulmonary vessels. Size decides the outcome: **main artery occlusion, saddle embolus (bifurcation)** or smaller branches. **Paradoxical embolism** crosses an atrial/ventricular defect into the systemic circulation.
- **Pulmonary embolism outcomes:** **60-80% small and silent** (organize into fibrous webs); **large embolus → sudden death**; medium arteries → **hemorrhage**; small end-arterioles → **infarction**; repeated emboli → **pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale.**
- **Systemic thromboembolism:** **80% from intracardiac mural thrombi** (⅔ with left ventricular infarcts, 25% with dilated left atria); others from **aortic aneurysms, ulcerated plaques, valvular vegetations, paradoxical emboli**; 10-15% unknown. **Venous emboli lodge in the lung; arterial emboli travel anywhere**: **lower extremities 75%, CNS 10%.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Most emboli = thrombi; most pulmonary emboli = leg DVT (> 95%).
- Saddle embolus at the pulmonary artery bifurcation; paradoxical embolus crosses a septal defect.
- Systemic emboli: 80% from mural thrombi, 75% to the lower limbs.

</div>

## 2. Fat, amniotic fluid and air embolism

- **Fat embolism:** fat globules from **soft tissue injury or ruptured marrow sinusoids** (long bone fractures); occurs in **~90% of severe skeletal injury** but **< 10% are symptomatic.** Features: **thrombocytopenia**, **anemia**, **petechial rash (20-50%)**; pathogenesis: **mechanical occlusion + fatty acid toxic endothelial injury + platelet/granulocyte activation.** Needs **frozen sections and fat stains.**
- ⭐ **Amniotic fluid embolism:** **1 in 40,000 deliveries; mortality ~80%**; sudden **dyspnea, cyanosis, shock → seizures, coma**; survivors: **pulmonary edema and DIC.** Cause: amniotic fluid enters maternal circulation via **torn membranes/uterine vein rupture**; histology: **fetal squamous cells, lanugo hair, vernix fat, mucin.**
- **Air embolism:** bubbles obstruct flow; **small venous emboli harmless**, **arterial (coronary, cerebral) are dangerous**; enough air in the lung → hypoxia (obstetric/laparoscopic procedures, chest wall injury).
- ⭐ **Decompression sickness:** rapid ascent → **nitrogen bubbles**; lung: edema, hemorrhage, atelectasis/emphysema ("**the chokes**"); CNS: mental impairment, **coma.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Fat embolism: long bone fracture, petechial rash, thrombocytopenia.
- Amniotic fluid embolism: mortality ~80%, DIC; fetal squames and lanugo in lung vessels.
- Decompression sickness: nitrogen bubbles; the chokes.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe fat embolism syndrome.</summary>

**Answer:** Fat/marrow globules released after fractures or soft tissue injury occlude lung and brain microvasculature and cause toxic endothelial injury; features are thrombocytopenia, anemia and a petechial rash; demonstration requires frozen sections and fat stains.

**Why:** Slides "Fat embolism".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the features of amniotic fluid embolism?</summary>

**Answer:** A rare (1 in 40,000), grave (≈ 80% mortality) complication of labor with sudden dyspnea, cyanosis and shock, then seizures and coma, pulmonary edema and DIC; fetal squamous cells, lanugo hair and mucin are seen in the maternal lung vessels.

</details>

**Answer:** Rapid depressurization makes dissolved nitrogen form bubbles in blood and tissues causing ischemia; in lung it causes edema and the "chokes", in CNS confusion and coma.

**Why:** Slide "Decompression sickness".

## 3. Infarction: causes and morphology

- ⭐ **Infarct** = area of **ischemic necrosis** from occlusion of the vascular supply (heart, brain, lung, bowel, gangrene in diabetics).
- **Causes:** mostly **arterial thrombosis or embolism**; also vasospasm, atheroma expansion (intraplaque hemorrhage), extrinsic compression (tumor), dissecting aneurysm, edema in confined space; uncommon: **torsion/volvulus, traumatic rupture, hernia entrapment.** **Venous** infarcts only in organs with **a single efferent vein** (testis, ovary).

| | **Red (hemorrhagic)** | **White (anemic)** |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | **Venous occlusion** (ovarian torsion), **loose tissue**, **dual circulation** (lung, small intestine), previous congestion, reperfusion | **Arterial occlusion in solid organs with end-arterial supply** (**heart, spleen, kidney**) |
| **Organs** | **Lung, brain, gut, liver, ovary, testis** | **Kidney, heart, spleen** |

- **Shape:** **wedge-shaped**, vessel at the **apex**, organ periphery at the **base.**
- **Histology:** **ischemic coagulative necrosis**; inflammation at margins within hours (**well defined in 1-2 days**), then repair; most end as **scar**; the **brain shows liquefactive necrosis.**
- **Septic infarct:** infected vegetation emboli or microbial seeding → **abscess.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Red infarct: venous occlusion, loose or dual-circulation tissue; white: end-arterial solid organs.
- Coagulative necrosis (brain = liquefactive).
- Septic infarct becomes an abscess.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare red and white infarcts.</summary>

**Answer:** Red infarcts follow venous occlusion or occur in loose or dual-circulation tissues (lung, intestine, ovary, testis, brain); white infarcts follow arterial occlusion in solid organs with end-arterial supply (heart, spleen, kidney).

**Why:** Slides "Red infarcts" and "White infarcts".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the shape and histology of an infarct.</summary>

**Answer:** Wedge-shaped with the occluded vessel at the apex; ischemic coagulative necrosis with a margin inflammatory response in 1-2 days and eventual scarring (liquefactive in the brain).

**Why:** Slides "Morphology of infarction".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is a septic infarct?</summary>

**Answer:** An infarct infected by embolized valve vegetations or seeded microbes, becoming an abscess with greater inflammation and healing by organization and fibrosis.

**Why:** Slide "Septic infarct".
</details>

## 4. Factors influencing infarct development

| Factor | Explanation |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Anatomy of the vascular supply** | **Alternative blood supply** protects: **lung (pulmonary + bronchial), liver (hepatic artery + portal vein), hand/forearm (radial + ulnar)** resist infarction; **kidney and spleen** (end-arterial) infarct |
| **Rate of occlusion** | **Slow occlusion allows collaterals** (e.g. coronary inter-arteriolar anastomoses) |
| **Tissue vulnerability to hypoxia** | **Neurons die in 3-4 min; myocardial cells in 20-30 min;** fibroblasts survive hours |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Dual supply = resistant; end-arterial = vulnerable.
- Slow occlusion → collaterals → no infarct.
- Neurons 3-4 min, cardiomyocytes 20-30 min.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the three variables that determine whether occlusion causes infarction.</summary>

**Answer:** The anatomy of the vascular supply, the rate of occlusion and the tissue's vulnerability to hypoxia.

**Why:** Slide "Factors that influence infarct development".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why are the lung and liver resistant to infarction while the kidney and spleen are not?</summary>

**Answer:** Lung and liver have dual blood supplies, whereas the kidney and spleen have end-arterial circulations.

**Why:** Same slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare the ischemic tolerance of neurons and cardiomyocytes.</summary>

**Answer:** Neurons suffer irreversible damage after 3-4 minutes, myocardial cells after 20-30 minutes.

**Why:** Same slide.
</details>

## 5. Shock: types and septic shock

- ⭐ **Shock** = reduced cardiac output or effective circulating volume → **tissue hypoperfusion and cellular hypoxia**; reversible at first, **irreversible if prolonged.** Complicates hemorrhage, trauma, burns, MI, PE, sepsis.

| Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Cardiogenic** | **Pump failure**: MI, arrhythmia, **tamponade**, outflow obstruction |
| **Hypovolemic** | **Blood or plasma loss** (hemorrhage, fluid loss, burns) |
| **Septic** | **Microbial infection + SIRS**: **vasodilation, leakage, venous pooling** |
| **Neurogenic** | Loss of vascular tone (**anesthesia, spinal cord injury**) |
| **Anaphylactic** | **IgE-mediated** vasodilation and permeability |

- **Septic shock triggers:** Gram-positive, Gram-negative bacteria, fungi; products activate **macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, endothelium, complement and factor XII (DIC)** → cytokines (**TNF, IL-1, IFN-γ, IL-12, IL-18, HMGB1**), CRP, procalcitonin, ROS, prostaglandins, **PAF**, **C3a/C5a/C3b**.
- **Four contributors:** (1) **inflammatory and counter-inflammatory** responses (TH1 → TH2 shift, lymphocyte apoptosis, anergy → superinfections); (2) **endothelial activation** (leaky tight junctions, **NO** → hypotension); (3) **procoagulant state/DIC** (up to half of patients); (4) **metabolic** (insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, adrenal insufficiency including **Waterhouse-Friderichsen**, **lactic acidosis**) → **organ dysfunction (ARDS, kidney, liver, heart failure).**
- **Outcome depends on** virulence/extent, host immunity, co-morbidities, mediator pattern. **Superantigens → toxic shock syndrome.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Cardiogenic = pump; hypovolemic = volume; septic = vasodilation + leak; plus neurogenic and anaphylactic.
- Septic shock: cytokines + endothelial injury + DIC + metabolic → multi-organ failure.
- Superantigens cause toxic shock syndrome.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify shock and give the mechanism of each type.</summary>

**Answer:** Cardiogenic (pump failure), hypovolemic (volume loss), septic (infection with SIRS: vasodilation, leakage, pooling), neurogenic (loss of vascular tone) and anaphylactic (IgE-mediated vasodilation).

**Why:** Slides "Shock" and "Table 4.3".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Outline the pathogenesis of septic shock.</summary>

**Answer:** Microbial products activate immune and endothelial cells, complement and coagulation; mediators cause vasodilation, vascular leakage, DIC and metabolic derangements, leading to hypoperfusion and multi-organ failure.

**Why:** Slides "Pathogenesis of Septic Shock".
</details>

**Answer:** Cytokines loosen endothelial tight junctions, causing protein-rich edema and impaired perfusion, and upregulate NO and vasoactive mediators that relax smooth muscle and cause hypotension.

## 6. Stages, morphology and clinical course of shock

- **Stage I – non-progressive:** compensation (**baroreceptors, catecholamines, renin-angiotensin, ADH, sympathetic stimulation**) → **tachycardia, vasoconstriction, fluid conservation; cool pale skin.**
- **Stage II – progressive:** hypoperfusion, **anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acidosis** → arteriolar dilation, **blood pooling**, endothelial anoxia, **DIC**, organ failure.
- **Stage III – irreversible:** cell injury (lysosomal leakage), worse myocardial function, gut bacteria → **bacteremia**, renal failure, death even if hemodynamics are corrected.
- **Morphology:** hypoxic injury of **brain, heart, kidneys, adrenals, GI tract**; **fibrin thrombi in glomeruli**; **"shock lung"** (diffuse alveolar damage) after sepsis or trauma.
- **Clinical course:** hypovolemic and cardiogenic = **hypotension, weak rapid pulse, tachypnea, cool clammy cyanotic skin**; **septic = warm, flushed skin** (peripheral vasodilation).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Three stages: non-progressive, progressive, irreversible.
- Lactic acidosis drives the progressive stage; DIC and organ failure follow.
- Septic shock skin may be warm; the others cool and clammy.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the three stages of shock.</summary>

**Answer:** Non-progressive (compensation maintains vital organ perfusion), progressive (hypoperfusion, lactic acidosis, pooling, DIC) and irreversible (severe cell injury, organ failure, death despite correction).

**Why:** Slide "Stages of Shock".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which compensatory mechanisms operate in early shock and what are their effects?</summary>

**Answer:** Baroreceptor reflexes, catecholamines, renin-angiotensin activation, ADH and sympathetic stimulation produce tachycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction and renal conservation of fluid; skin becomes cool and pale.

**Why:** Slide "In the early non-progressive phase of shock".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How does the clinical picture of septic shock differ from hypovolemic and cardiogenic shock?</summary>

**Answer:** Hypovolemic and cardiogenic shock give cool, clammy, cyanotic skin; in septic shock the skin may be warm and flushed due to peripheral vasodilation.

**Why:** Slide "Clinical course".
</details>
