## 1. Receptor blockade and alpha-blocker effects

- ⭐ **Antagonists occupy adrenoceptors without activating them.**
- **α blockade:** ↓ vasoconstriction, ↓ BP and urinary tone. **β blockade:** ↓ HR and contractility; **β2 → bronchospasm risk.**
- **Alpha-blocker effects:** **α1 (vessels):** relaxation → **↓ BP**; **α1A (prostate, bladder neck):** relaxation → **↑ urine flow**; **α2 (presynaptic):** blockade → **↑ NE release.** **Clue:** hypotension ± **reflex tachycardia.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- α1 vessels = ↓ BP; α1A prostate = ↑ flow.
- Presynaptic α2 blockade increases NE release.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What are the effects of α and β blockade?</summary>

**Answer:** α blockade lowers vasoconstriction, BP and urinary tone; β blockade lowers HR and contractility, and β2 blockade risks bronchospasm.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe α blockade at target organs.</summary>

**Answer:** α1 in vessels: relaxation and lower BP; α1A in prostate and bladder neck: relaxation and better urine flow; presynaptic α2: increased norepinephrine release; clinically hypotension with possible reflex tachycardia.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the clinical clue of α blockade?</summary>

**Answer:** Hypotension with or without reflex tachycardia.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

## 2. Nonselective α-blockers

| Drug | Features | Use | Adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phenoxybenzamine** | **Irreversible, noncompetitive α1 and α2**; **long-lasting** (new receptors needed); ↓ resistance → **reflex tachycardia** | **Preoperative blockade in selected pheochromocytoma** (only use) | **Postural hypotension, dizziness, nasal congestion; reflex tachycardia, palpitations, angina; impaired ejaculation**; caution in coronary/cerebrovascular disease |
| **Phentolamine** | **Reversible, competitive, short-acting α1 and α2** | **Catecholamine-related hypertensive emergencies**; **local infiltration for vasopressor extravasation** | **Hypotension, reflex tachycardia, arrhythmias** |

- **Pheochromocytoma:** **alpha-blocker FIRST, then beta-blocker**; beta-blocker first → **unopposed α vasoconstriction → hypertensive crisis.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Phenoxybenzamine irreversible; phentolamine reversible.
- Always α-block before β-block in pheochromocytoma.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare phenoxybenzamine and phentolamine.</summary>

**Answer:** Phenoxybenzamine is an irreversible noncompetitive α1/α2 antagonist with a prolonged effect used preoperatively in pheochromocytoma; phentolamine is a reversible, competitive, short-acting α1/α2 antagonist used in catecholamine hypertensive emergencies and vasopressor extravasation.

**Why:** Slides 6-8.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the alpha-blocker given first in pheochromocytoma?</summary>

**Answer:** A beta-blocker first would leave alpha vasoconstriction unopposed and risk a hypertensive crisis.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. List the adverse effects of phenoxybenzamine.</summary>

**Answer:** Postural hypotension, dizziness, nasal congestion, reflex tachycardia, palpitations, angina and impaired ejaculation.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

## 3. Selective α1 blockers and BPH

- ⭐ **Prazosin, terazosin, doxazosin:** ↓ peripheral resistance by relaxing **arterial and venous** smooth muscle; **less tachycardia** than nonselective; uses: **hypertension** and **BPH.**
- **Tamsulosin and silodosin** favor **prostatic α1A**: relax prostate and bladder neck → **↑ urine stream**; **improve symptoms, do NOT shrink the prostate.**
- **Adverse effects and precautions:** **orthostatic hypotension and first-dose syncope** (**start low; bedtime; first dose 1/3 or 1/4**; rise slowly), **dizziness, headache, nasal congestion, reflex tachycardia (esp. nonselective), impaired ejaculation**; **caution with nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors.**
- **Comparison:** phenoxybenzamine (irreversible), phentolamine (reversible, short), **prazosin/doxazosin (hypertension)**, **tamsulosin/silodosin (BPH).**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- First-dose phenomenon: start at bedtime with a low dose.
- BPH drugs relieve symptoms, not prostate size.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the first-dose phenomenon and how is it managed?</summary>

**Answer:** Marked orthostatic hypotension or syncope after the first dose of an α1 blocker; manage by starting with a low dose (1/3 or 1/4), giving it at bedtime, rising slowly and monitoring standing BP.

**Why:** Slide 14.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How do tamsulosin and silodosin help in BPH?</summary>

**Answer:** They block prostatic α1A receptors, relaxing the prostate and bladder neck smooth muscle and increasing urine flow; they improve symptoms but do not shrink the prostate.

**Why:** Slide 11.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which drugs interact with α-blockers?</summary>

**Answer:** Nitrates, PDE-5 inhibitors and other hypotensive drugs.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

## 4. Beta-blockers: principles and classification

- ⭐ All are **competitive antagonists**; **nonselective** (β1 + β2) vs **cardioselective** (mainly β1).
- **β1 (heart, kidney):** ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, ↑ renin → **β1 blockade ↓ HR, ↓ AV conduction, ↓ renin.** **β2 (bronchi, skeletal-muscle vessels):** bronchodilation, vasodilation → **β2 blockade = bronchoconstriction, ↑ resistance.**

| Class | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Nonselective β1/β2** | **Propranolol, nadolol, timolol** |
| **β1-selective** | **Atenolol, metoprolol, bisoprolol, nebivolol** |
| **Mixed α1 + β** | **Carvedilol, labetalol** |

- **ECG:** β1 blockade **slows SA and AV nodes** → **prolonged PR interval.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- β1 = heart and kidney; β2 = bronchi and vessels.
- β-blockers prolong the PR interval.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Classify the beta-blockers.</summary>

**Answer:** Nonselective (propranolol, nadolol, timolol), β1-selective (atenolol, metoprolol, bisoprolol, nebivolol) and mixed α1 + β (carvedilol, labetalol).

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Compare β1 and β2 blockade.</summary>

**Answer:** β1 blockade lowers HR, AV conduction and renin; β2 blockade causes bronchoconstriction and increased vascular resistance.

**Why:** Slide 18.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What ECG change do beta-blockers cause?</summary>

**Answer:** Slowing of the SA and AV nodes with a prolonged PR interval.

**Why:** Slide 28.
</details>

## 5. Propranolol, nadolol and timolol

- **Propranolol actions:** **heart (β1)** ↓ HR, AV conduction, contractility; **kidney (β1)** ↓ renin/RAAS; **bronchi (β2)** bronchospasm risk; **metabolic (β2)** ↓ glycogenolysis and **masks hypoglycemia signs.**
- **Uses:** **rate control in SVT, angina, thyrotoxicosis symptoms (tremor, palpitations, anxiety), essential tremor, migraine prevention, portal hypertension (variceal bleeding prevention).**
- **Pharmacokinetics:** **highly lipophilic, crosses BBB**; **extensive first-pass**; **shorter half-life than nadolol**; hepatic impairment and interactions matter.
- **Adverse effects:** **bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, bronchospasm, cold extremities, sexual dysfunction, vivid dreams; abrupt withdrawal → rebound tachycardia or angina.**
- **Nadolol:** nonselective, **long-acting, renal elimination**, portal hypertension protocols. **Timolol:** **eye drops lower IOP in glaucoma**; systemic absorption → **bradycardia or bronchospasm.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Propranolol: lipophilic, first-pass, masks hypoglycemia, rebound on withdrawal.
- Timolol drops treat glaucoma but can be absorbed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the uses of propranolol.</summary>

**Answer:** SVT rate control, angina, thyrotoxicosis symptoms, essential tremor, migraine prevention and prevention of variceal bleeding in portal hypertension.

**Why:** Slide 22.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the adverse effects of propranolol?</summary>

**Answer:** Bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, exercise intolerance, bronchospasm, cold extremities, sexual dysfunction, vivid dreams, and rebound tachycardia or angina on abrupt withdrawal.

**Why:** Slide 24.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the pharmacokinetics of propranolol.</summary>

**Answer:** Highly lipophilic with CNS penetration, extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism and variable oral bioavailability, shorter half-life than nadolol.

**Why:** Slide 23.
</details>

## 6. β1-selective and mixed α/β blockers

- **Atenolol:** relatively **hydrophilic, renal.** **Metoprolol:** **hepatic**; formulations not interchangeable (**succinate** has HFrEF evidence). **Bisoprolol:** **long-acting β1.** **Nebivolol:** β1 with **NO-linked vasodilation.**
- **Uses:** angina, rate control, selected hypertension; **bisoprolol and metoprolol succinate: HFrEF outcome evidence.** **Adverse effects:** bradycardia, fatigue, hypotension (less than nonselective), **AV block**; **selectivity is lost at higher doses.**
- **Mixed α/β (carvedilol, labetalol):** **α1 blockade** (vasodilation, ↓ resistance) + **β1** (↓ HR, contractility, renin) + **β2** (possible bronchoconstriction) → **↓ BP with less reflex tachycardia.**
- **Labetalol:** **IV hypertensive emergencies, hypertension in pregnancy, oral chronic hypertension.** **Carvedilol:** **HFrEF (↑ survival, ↓ hospitalizations)**, hypertension, **LV dysfunction after MI.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- β1 selectivity fades at high doses.
- Labetalol: pregnancy and emergencies; carvedilol: HFrEF.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the mixed α/β blockers.</summary>

**Answer:** They block α1 (vasodilation), β1 (lower HR, contractility and renin) and β2 (possible bronchoconstriction), lowering BP with less reflex tachycardia than pure α blockade.

**Why:** Slide 29.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are the uses of labetalol and carvedilol?</summary>

**Answer:** Labetalol: IV hypertensive emergencies, hypertension in pregnancy, chronic oral hypertension. Carvedilol: HFrEF (better survival, fewer admissions), hypertension and LV dysfunction after MI.

**Why:** Slide 30.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which β1-selective agents have HFrEF evidence?</summary>

**Answer:** Bisoprolol and metoprolol succinate (and carvedilol among mixed blockers).

**Why:** Slides 26-27 and 32.
</details>

## 7. Beta-blockers in heart failure

- **Why avoided (acute decompensated HF):** acute β1 blockade **↓ contractility and cardiac output** → worse **hypotension and congestion**; **delay initiation.**
- **Why recommended now (stable chronic HFrEF):** chronic **sympathetic activation and β-receptor toxicity**; long-term β blockade **↓ arrhythmias, remodelling and harmful signalling**; **↓ mortality and HF admissions.**
- **Evidence-based drugs:** **bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate.** **Start low and up-titrate when stable.**
- **Takeaways:** α1 blockers lower vascular tone and relieve BPH obstruction; **nonselective β-blockers can provoke bronchospasm**; **never stop chronic β-blockade abruptly.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Acute negative inotropy vs long-term neurohormonal protection.
- Start low, go slow, only when stable.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why were beta-blockers historically avoided in HF?</summary>

**Answer:** Acute β1 blockade reduces contractility and cardiac output and can worsen hypotension and congestion in decompensated HF.

**Why:** Slide 31.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why are they now recommended in stable HFrEF?</summary>

**Answer:** Chronic HF causes sustained sympathetic activation and β-receptor toxicity; long-term β blockade reduces arrhythmias, remodelling and harmful signalling and lowers mortality and hospitalization.

**Why:** Slide 32.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How should they be started?</summary>

**Answer:** In a stable patient, using a proven agent (bisoprolol, carvedilol or metoprolol succinate) at a low dose and up-titrating.

**Why:** Slides 32 and 34.
</details>
