## 1. What is a "high-energy compound"?

- ⭐ It is **not** a "high-energy bond": the term is a **misnomer**. It refers to the **large negative free energy change (ΔG°′) of hydrolysis**.
- **Phosphoryl group-transfer potential** = the **tendency of a molecule to transfer its phosphoryl group (~P)** to an acceptor such as **ADP**.
- Transfer potential is measured **relative to ATP hydrolysis: ΔG°′ = −7.3 kcal·mol⁻¹**.
- A compound with a **more negative ΔG°′ of hydrolysis** than ATP has a **higher** transfer potential than ATP.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- High-energy = large negative ΔG°′ of hydrolysis, not a special bond.
- Reference = ATP hydrolysis (−7.3 kcal/mol).
- More negative than −7.3 → higher transfer potential than ATP.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** The energy is not stored in the bond itself; the term really describes the large negative free energy change (ΔG°′) released when the compound is hydrolysed.

**Why:** Key definition from the first slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define phosphoryl group-transfer potential.</summary>

## 2. ATP: the central energy carrier

- **Structure:** **adenine + ribose + triphosphate** (adenosine = adenine + ribose).
  - The bond ribose–**α-phosphate** is a **phosphoester**; the bonds between phosphates are **two phosphoanhydride bonds**.
- **Hydrolysis:** **ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ**, **ΔG°′ = −7.3 kcal·mol⁻¹** (the **γ-phosphate** is removed).
- **Why so negative?**
  - **Closely spaced negative charges** on the phosphate groups at physiological pH repel each other (about 4 negative charges); hydrolysis relieves the strain.
  - The **products (ADP and Pᵢ) are better stabilized by resonance** than ATP.
- The reverse reaction (**ADP + Pᵢ → ATP + H₂O**) has **ΔG°′ = +7.3 kcal·mol⁻¹**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- ATP = adenine + ribose + 3 phosphates; 1 phosphoester + 2 phosphoanhydride bonds.
- ΔG°′ = −7.3 because of charge repulsion + resonance stabilization of products.
- γ-phosphate is the one removed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the structure of ATP and its bonds.</summary>

**Answer:** Adenine and ribose (adenosine) with a triphosphate group. The ribose is joined to the α-phosphate by a phosphoester bond, and the three phosphates are linked by two phosphoanhydride bonds.

**Why:** Fig. 1 of the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why is the ΔG°′ of ATP hydrolysis so negative?</summary>

**Answer:** At physiological pH the closely spaced negative charges of the phosphate groups repel one another, and the products ADP and Pᵢ are stabilized by resonance more than the reactant ATP.

**Why:** Two reasons, always asked together.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Write the ATP hydrolysis reaction and its ΔG°′.</summary>

**Answer:** ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ, ΔG°′ = −7.3 kcal·mol⁻¹.

**Why:** The reference reaction of the whole lecture.
</details>

## 3. ATP in coupled reactions

- **Principle:** exergonic **ATP hydrolysis** is **coupled** to an **endergonic** reaction so that the overall process becomes **spontaneous**.
- **Mechanism:** the **~P of ATP is transferred to a reactant**, forming a **more reactive high-energy intermediate**.
- **ΔG°′ overall = ΔG°′ endergonic + ΔG°′ ATP hydrolysis**; if the sum is **negative**, the reaction proceeds.

### Example: phosphorylation of glucose
| Reaction | ΔG°′ (kcal/mol) |
|---|---|
| Glucose + Pᵢ → Glucose-6-phosphate + H₂O | **+3.3** (non-spontaneous) |
| ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ | **−7.3** |
| **ATP + Glucose → ADP + Glucose-6-phosphate** | **−4.0** (spontaneous) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Add the ΔG°′ values; negative sum = spontaneous.
- Glucose phosphorylation: +3.3 − 7.3 = −4.0 kcal/mol.
- ATP gives its ~P to the reactant (high-energy intermediate).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain energy coupling with ATP.</summary>

**Answer:** The exergonic hydrolysis of ATP is coupled to an endergonic reaction. ATP transfers its phosphoryl group to the reactant, producing a more reactive intermediate, and the overall ΔG°′ (the sum of both) is negative, so the process is spontaneous.

**Why:** Core principle of the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Calculate the overall ΔG°′ of the coupled phosphorylation of glucose.</summary>

**Answer:** +3.3 + (−7.3) = −4.0 kcal·mol⁻¹, so ATP + glucose → ADP + glucose-6-phosphate is spontaneous.

**Why:** The worked example on the slides.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What sign must the overall ΔG°′ have for a coupled reaction to proceed?</summary>

**Answer:** Negative.

**Why:** Spontaneity depends on the sum, not on the endergonic step alone.
</details>

## 4. Phosphoryl group-transfer potential scale

- Compounds can be ranked by their tendency to donate ~P (a **thermodynamic hierarchy**).
- **High-transfer donors:** **PEP, 1,3-BPG, creatine phosphate** — they can **phosphorylate ADP to make ATP**.
- **ATP is in the middle:** it **accepts ~P from high-potential compounds** and **donates ~P to low-potential compounds** (e.g. glucose-6-phosphate, glycerol-3-phosphate).
- Phosphoryl groups flow **high-energy donors → ATP–ADP system → low-energy acceptors**.

| Compound | ΔG°′ of hydrolysis (kcal/mol) |
|---|---|
| **PEP** | **−14.8** |
| **1,3-BPG** | **−11.8** |
| **Creatine phosphate** | **−10.3** |
| **ATP** | **−7.3** |
| Glucose-6-phosphate / glycerol-3-phosphate | less negative (low-energy) |

### Worked question: creatine phosphate in the sprint
Creatine phosphate + ADP → Creatine + ATP: **−10.3 + 7.3 = −3.0 kcal/mol** → creatine phosphate has a **higher transfer potential than ATP**, so it can phosphorylate ADP (answer **A**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PEP > 1,3-BPG > creatine phosphate > ATP > glucose-6-phosphate.
- ATP's middle position makes it the universal carrier.
- Creatine phosphate + ADP: ΔG°′ = −3.0 (spontaneous).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is ATP a good universal energy carrier?</summary>

**Answer:** It occupies an intermediate position on the transfer potential scale, so it can receive ~P from high-potential donors (PEP, 1,3-BPG, creatine phosphate) and give ~P to low-potential acceptors (glucose-6-phosphate, glycerol-3-phosphate).

**Why:** Direct from the slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Rank PEP, 1,3-BPG, creatine phosphate and ATP by transfer potential.</summary>

**Answer:** PEP (−14.8) > 1,3-BPG (−11.8) > creatine phosphate (−10.3) > ATP (−7.3).

**Why:** Rank by how negative the ΔG°′ is.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Calculate the ΔG°′ of creatine phosphate + ADP → creatine + ATP and interpret it.</summary>

**Answer:** −10.3 + 7.3 = −3.0 kcal/mol; negative, so creatine phosphate has a higher transfer potential than ATP and can regenerate ATP from ADP.

**Why:** The sprint question (option A).
</details>

## 5. High-energy phosphorylated compounds

### Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)
- **Enol phosphate.** PEP + H₂O → **pyruvate** + Pᵢ, **ΔG°′ = −14.8**.
- The **enol** form of pyruvate is **unstable** and spontaneously converts to the very stable **keto** form — this drives the large negative ΔG°′.

### 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (1,3-BPG)
- **Acyl phosphate.** 1,3-BPG + H₂O → **3-phosphoglycerate** + Pᵢ, **ΔG°′ = −11.8**.
- The acyl phosphate has a **highly polarized carbonyl carbon**, susceptible to **nucleophilic attack**, and the products are **resonance-stabilized**.

### Creatine phosphate (molecular energy reservoir)
- A **rapidly mobilizable reserve of high-energy phosphate** in **muscle and nerve** tissue (the "energy buffer").
- Creatine phosphate + H₂O → creatine + Pᵢ, **ΔG°′ = −10.3**.
- **Recharging:** when ATP is plentiful, its ~P is used to re-make creatine phosphate by ⭐ **creatine kinase**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PEP = enol phosphate (−14.8); enol → keto drives it.
- 1,3-BPG = acyl phosphate (−11.8).
- Creatine phosphate = muscle/nerve buffer (−10.3); creatine kinase.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why does PEP have such a high phosphoryl group-transfer potential?</summary>

**Answer:** Hydrolysis of PEP releases the enol form of pyruvate, which is unstable and spontaneously converts to the highly stable keto form, giving ΔG°′ = −14.8 kcal/mol.

**Why:** Tautomerization pulls the reaction forward.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the structural reason for the high energy of 1,3-BPG?</summary>

**Answer:** It is an acyl phosphate with a strongly polarized carbonyl carbon that is easily attacked by nucleophiles, and its hydrolysis products are stabilized by resonance (ΔG°′ = −11.8).

**Why:** The slide's "high-energy rationale".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the role of creatine phosphate and how is it recharged?</summary>

**Answer:** It is a quickly available reserve of high-energy phosphate in muscle and nerve that regenerates ATP from ADP. When ATP is plentiful, creatine kinase transfers ~P from ATP back to creatine.

**Why:** Energy buffer during a sprint.
</details>

## 6. Thioesters: acetyl-CoA

- **Structure:** an **acyl group (e.g. acetyl)** linked to the **thiol (–SH)** of **Coenzyme A** by a **thioester bond** (drawn with ~).
- **Hydrolysis:** acetyl-CoA + H₂O → **acetate + CoA-SH**, **ΔG°′ = −7.5 kcal/mol** (**similar to ATP**).
- **Why high-energy?** The **carbon–sulfur bond is weak**, and the products (acetate and thiol) are **more stable by resonance** than the thioester reactant.
- **Coenzyme A** contains **adenosine-3′-phosphate, a pantothenic acid residue (vitamin B₅) and a β-mercaptoethylamine residue**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Acetyl-CoA = thioester; ΔG°′ = −7.5 (≈ ATP).
- C–S bond weak; products resonance-stabilized.
- CoA = adenosine-3′-phosphate + pantothenic acid + β-mercaptoethylamine.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is a thioester and why is it energy-rich?</summary>

**Answer:** An acyl group linked to a thiol (–SH) group; as in acetyl-CoA. The C–S bond is weak and the hydrolysis products are more resonance-stabilized than the reactant, so hydrolysis releases about −7.5 kcal/mol.

**Why:** Third class of energy-rich compounds.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Write the hydrolysis of acetyl-CoA and compare its ΔG°′ with ATP.</summary>

**Answer:** Acetyl-CoA + H₂O → acetate + CoA-SH, ΔG°′ = −7.5 kcal/mol, almost the same as ATP (−7.3).

**Why:** Shows acetyl-CoA is as energy-rich as ATP.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Name the components of Coenzyme A.</summary>

**Answer:** Adenosine-3′-phosphate, a pantothenic acid residue and a β-mercaptoethylamine residue (which carries the –SH group).

**Why:** Fig. 3 of the lecture.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- It is a **high-energy compound**, not a **high-energy bond** — the energy comes from the **ΔG°′ of hydrolysis**.
- **−7.3 kcal/mol** is the reference; **more negative = higher transfer potential** than ATP.
- **Sum the ΔG°′ values**: +3.3 − 7.3 = **−4.0** (glucose); −10.3 + 7.3 = **−3.0** (creatine phosphate).
- **PEP (−14.8) > 1,3-BPG (−11.8) > creatine phosphate (−10.3) > ATP (−7.3)** — don't put ATP at the top.
- **PEP = enol phosphate; 1,3-BPG = acyl phosphate; acetyl-CoA = thioester.**
- Creatine phosphate is a **short-term buffer** recharged by **creatine kinase**, not a long-term store.
- The ΔG°′ of ATP **synthesis** (ADP + Pᵢ → ATP) is **+7.3**.
