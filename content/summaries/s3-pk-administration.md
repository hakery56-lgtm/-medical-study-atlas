## 1. Pharmacokinetics and the choice of route

- **Pharmacokinetics** = **what the body does to a drug**. Four properties determine the **speed of onset**, the **intensity** and the **duration** of drug effect:

| # | Property | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Absorption** (input) | drug at the site of administration → drug in plasma |
| 2 | **Distribution** | drug in plasma ⇄ drug in tissues |
| 3 | **Metabolism** | metabolite(s) in tissues |
| 4 | **Elimination** (output) | drug and/or metabolites in **urine, bile or feces** |

### What decides the route?
- **Properties of the drug:** water/lipid solubility, ionization.
- **Therapeutic objectives:** rapid onset, long-term treatment, or **restriction of delivery to a local site**.
- Routes: **parenteral (IV, IM, SC), sublingual, inhalation, oral, transdermal patch, topical, rectal.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pharmacokinetics = body → drug; ADME controls onset, intensity and duration of effect.
- Route is chosen by drug properties (solubility, ionization) and therapeutic aims (rapid onset, long-term, local delivery).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Why:** They determine onset, intensity and duration of drug effect.
</details>

## 2. Enteral route (drug given by mouth)

- ⭐ **The safest, most common, convenient and economic method.**

### Oral route (swallowed drug)
| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Easily self-administered** | **Absorption is more complicated** |
| **Low risk of infection** (no injection) | **Low gastric pH may inactivate some drugs** |
| **Toxicity/overdose can be managed** with **activated charcoal** (adsorbent) or **GIT decontamination** (emesis, gastric lavage) | |

- **Dosage forms:** liquids (syrup, suspension, elixir) or solids (tablet, capsule).

### Special oral preparations
- **Enteric-coated:** a chemical envelope that **resists gastric juice but dissolves in the upper intestine**. Used for **acid-unstable drugs (omeprazole)** and to **avoid the irritant effect** (e.g. **aspirin**).
- **Extended-release:** **gradual release** → longer duration, **better compliance** (less frequent dosing), keeps concentration **within the therapeutic range** for a long time; useful for **short half-life drugs like morphine**.

### Sublingual route
- Drug placed **under the tongue** → direct absorption to the systemic circulation.
- Advantages: **rapid absorption/onset**, **avoids first-pass metabolism** (↑ bioavailability), convenient, **bypasses the harsh GI environment**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Oral = safest, most common, cheap; downsides: complex absorption, stomach acid inactivates some drugs.
- Overdose managed by activated charcoal, emesis or gastric lavage.
- Enteric coating (omeprazole, aspirin); extended release (morphine) improves compliance.
- Sublingual: rapid, avoids first-pass, bypasses GI tract.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Mention the advantages and disadvantages of the oral route.</summary>

**Answer:** Advantages: easily self-administered, low risk of infection, and overdose can be countered with activated charcoal or GIT decontamination (emesis, gastric lavage). Disadvantages: absorption is more complicated and the low pH of the stomach may inactivate some drugs.

**Why:** Slide "Oral route".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What are enteric-coated and extended-release preparations and why are they used?</summary>

**Answer:** Enteric coating resists gastric juice and dissolves in the upper intestine, protecting acid-unstable drugs (omeprazole) and avoiding gastric irritation (aspirin). Extended-release forms release the drug gradually, giving a longer duration, fewer doses (better compliance) and stable therapeutic levels, especially useful for short-half-life drugs such as morphine.

**Why:** Specific preparations slide.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give the advantages of the sublingual route.</summary>

**Answer:** Rapid absorption and onset, avoidance of first-pass metabolism (higher bioavailability), convenience, and bypass of the harsh GI environment.

**Why:** Drug goes straight into systemic circulation.
</details>

## 3. Parenteral route (injection)

- **Indications:** drugs **poorly absorbed from the GI tract (heparin)** or **unstable in the GIT (insulin)**; **unconscious patients**; when a **rapid onset** is needed.
- **Advantages:** **highest bioavailability**; **no first-pass metabolism**; avoids the harsh GI environment.
- **Disadvantages:** **pain, fear, local tissue damage, infections**.

### Intravenous (IV)
- **Rapid effect and maximum control** of circulating drug levels.
- **Bolus dose:** the full initial amount is delivered almost immediately → **peak plasma concentration**.
- **IV infusion:** slower rate over a longer time → **lower peak** and **longer time in circulation**.
- Disadvantages: **toxicity is not easily reversed** (unlike oral), needs **equipment and experienced staff**, may introduce **bacteria/viruses**, may **precipitate blood constituents or cause hemolysis**.

### Intramuscular (IM)
- **Aqueous solutions** (e.g. **diclofenac ampule**) and **powders needing reconstitution** (e.g. **ampicillin vial**) are **absorbed rapidly**.
- **Depot preparations** are **absorbed slowly** → sustained dose (e.g. **methylprednisolone**).

### Subcutaneous (SC)
- **Slower than IV**; **less risk of hemolysis or thrombosis**; constant, slow, sustained effect (e.g. **insulin**).
- **Irritant drugs** can cause **severe pain and necrosis**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Parenteral: for heparin/insulin, unconscious patients, rapid onset; highest bioavailability, no first-pass; but pain, infection, tissue damage.
- IV: bolus = peak; infusion = lower peak, longer duration; toxicity hard to reverse.
- IM: aqueous/powder = fast, depot = slow. SC: slower than IV, e.g. insulin; irritants cause necrosis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. When is the parenteral route indicated and what are its pros and cons?</summary>

**Answer:** For drugs poorly absorbed (heparin) or unstable (insulin) in the GIT, for unconscious patients and when rapid onset is needed. It gives the highest bioavailability with no first-pass metabolism, but causes pain, fear, local tissue damage and infection.

**Why:** Lecture summary of the parenteral route.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate between IV bolus and IV infusion.</summary>

**Answer:** A bolus delivers the full initial dose almost immediately, giving a peak plasma concentration; an infusion gives the drug slowly over a longer time, which lowers the peak concentration and prolongs the time the drug stays in the circulation.

**Why:** Different rate of delivery.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Compare IM and SC injection with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** IM: aqueous solutions (diclofenac) and reconstituted powders (ampicillin) are absorbed rapidly; depot preparations (methylprednisolone) are absorbed slowly and sustained. SC: slower than IV, reduces the risk of hemolysis or thrombosis, gives sustained effect (insulin), but irritant drugs cause severe pain and necrosis.

**Why:** Two different parenteral routes.
</details>

## 4. Other routes

### Inhalation
- **Rapid delivery** across the **large surface area** of the respiratory mucosa/pulmonary epithelium.
- For **gases (nitrous oxide, a general anesthetic)** and **aerosols (salbutamol)**.
- Effective and convenient in **asthma and COPD**.

### Intrathecal
- Drug introduced **directly into the CSF**; used when the **BBB limits drug entry**, e.g. **intrathecal amphotericin B for cryptococcal meningitis**.

### Topical
- For a **local effect**: creams, nasal drops, eye drops, scalp lotion/shampoo, antiseptics, powders.

### Transdermal
- **Systemic effect via a transdermal patch**; rate depends on **skin characteristics** and **lipid solubility** of the drug; **sustained delivery** — e.g. **nitroglycerin (antianginal)** and **nicotine patches** for smoking cessation.

### Rectal
- **50 % of rectal drainage bypasses the portal circulation** → **less hepatic biotransformation**.
- Avoids destruction by **intestinal enzymes** and **gastric low pH**.
- Useful if the drug **induces vomiting** or the patient is **already vomiting** (e.g. **paracetamol suppositories**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Inhalation: gases (N₂O) and aerosols (salbutamol); asthma/COPD.
- Intrathecal: direct to CSF (amphotericin B in cryptococcal meningitis, BBB limits entry).
- Topical = local; transdermal = systemic, sustained (nitroglycerin, nicotine).
- Rectal: 50 % bypasses the portal circulation; useful when vomiting (paracetamol suppository).

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is the rectal route useful and what does it avoid?</summary>

**Answer:** About 50 % of rectal drainage bypasses the portal circulation, so hepatic metabolism is minimized; it also avoids destruction by intestinal enzymes and gastric acid, and is useful when the patient is vomiting or the drug induces vomiting (e.g. paracetamol suppositories).

**Why:** Partial bypass of first-pass.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate topical from transdermal administration.</summary>

**Answer:** Topical administration gives a local effect (creams, drops, shampoos, antiseptics); transdermal administration gives a systemic effect through a patch, with a sustained delivery that depends on skin characteristics and drug lipid solubility (nitroglycerin, nicotine patches).

**Why:** Local vs systemic target.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is amphotericin B given intrathecally in cryptococcal meningitis?</summary>

**Answer:** The blood–brain barrier limits drug absorption into the CNS, so the drug is introduced directly into the cerebrospinal fluid.

**Why:** Intrathecal route bypasses the BBB.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Oral is the safest and most common**, but **parenteral has the highest bioavailability**.
- **Sublingual and rectal (partly) avoid first-pass**; **oral does not**.
- **Enteric coating protects acid-labile drugs (omeprazole) AND the stomach from irritants (aspirin).**
- **Bolus = peak concentration; infusion = lower peak, longer time.**
- **IV toxicity is harder to reverse than oral** (activated charcoal/lavage only work for oral).
- **Rectal: 50 %** bypasses the portal vein (not 100 %).
- **Inhalation:** nitrous oxide = gas, salbutamol = aerosol.
