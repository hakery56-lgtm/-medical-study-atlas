## 1. Protein reprioritisation, hypermetabolism and muscle wasting

- ⭐ The body **reprioritises protein metabolism away from peripheral tissues (muscle, fat, skin) towards the liver, immune system and wounds**, shuttling amino acids, **especially glutamine and alanine.**
- **Hypermetabolism:** most trauma patients have **energy expenditure 15-25% above resting.**
- **Muscle protein turnover 1-2%/day**; normally synthesis = breakdown. **Feeding and exercise** promote net accretion.
- **Catabolic wasting** = **↑ degradation (enzymatic pathways) + ↓ synthesis.** **Peripheral skeletal muscle** is the **major site** of loss; also **respiratory muscles** (hypoventilation, chest infection) and **gut** (↓ motility); **cardiac muscle mostly spared.**
- **Clinical effects:** **asthenia, fatigue, reduced function, poorer quality of life, ↑ morbidity and mortality.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Protein shifts from muscle, fat, skin to liver, immune cells and wounds (Gln, Ala).
- Energy expenditure +15-25%.
- Wasting = more degradation + less synthesis; respiratory muscles and gut affected, heart spared.

</div>

## 2. Liver, cytokines and the acute phase response

- **Liver + skeletal muscle** = **> 50%** of daily protein turnover. **Muscle:** large mass, **1-2%/day**; **liver:** **1.5 kg**, **10-20%/day.**
- **Hepatic synthesis ~50:50 structural : export proteins**; **albumin** = major export protein, renewed **~10%/day.**
- **Triggers:** **surgery, trauma, sepsis, cancer, autoimmune disease** → blood mononuclear cells release **IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α.**
- **IL-6 → hepatic synthesis of positive acute phase proteins** (**fibrinogen, CRP**). **"Double-edged sword":** repair proteins but **at the expense of lean tissue and energy reserves.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Liver 1.5 kg turns over 10-20%/day; muscle 1-2%/day; together > 50% of turnover.
- IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α; IL-6 drives fibrinogen and CRP.
- Acute phase response costs lean tissue.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** Muscle has a large mass with 1-2% daily turnover; the liver has a small mass (1.5 kg) with 10-20% turnover; together they account for more than 50% of body protein turnover.

**Why:** Slide "The liver and skeletal muscle together".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which cytokines are released and what is the effect of IL-6?</summary>

**Answer:** IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α from mononuclear cells; IL-6 stimulates hepatic synthesis of fibrinogen and CRP.

**Why:** Slides "In response: surgery, trauma..." and "Cytokines, in particular IL-6".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is the acute phase response a "double-edged sword"?</summary>

## 3. Insulin resistance and body composition

- ⭐ **Postoperative hyperglycaemia:** **↑ glucose production + ↓ peripheral glucose uptake** (**insulin resistance**, transient). **Mechanisms:** **proinflammatory cytokines** and **↓ responsiveness of insulin-regulated glucose transporters.**
- **Resistance is proportional to injury**: ~**2 weeks** after routine upper abdominal surgery; patients behave like **type II diabetics**; **mainstay: intravenous insulin infusion.**
- **70 kg male:** **fat 13 kg**; **fat-free mass 57 kg** = **protein 12 kg** (**skeletal muscle 4 kg, visceral 8 kg**) + **water 42 kg** (**intracellular 28 L, extracellular 14 L**) + **minerals 3 kg** (bone).
- **Main labile energy reserve = fat; main labile protein reserve = skeletal muscle.** Fat loss is tolerated; **protein loss causes muscle wasting and visceral protein depletion.** **1 g urinary N = 36 g wet lean tissue**; normal protein intake **70-100 g/day** ≈ **14 g N/day** excreted.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Insulin resistance: ↑ production, ↓ uptake; lasts ~2 weeks after major abdominal surgery; treat with IV insulin.
- 70 kg: fat 13, lean 57 (protein 12, water 42, minerals 3).
- 1 g N lost = 36 g lean tissue.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain postoperative hyperglycaemia and its management.</summary>

**Answer:** Increased glucose production and reduced peripheral uptake due to cytokine-induced insulin resistance and reduced responsiveness of glucose transporters; patients behave like type II diabetics and are managed with intravenous insulin infusion.

**Why:** Slides "Insulin resistance".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Give the body composition of a 70 kg male.</summary>

**Answer:** Fat 13 kg; lean mass 57 kg: protein 12 kg (muscle 4, visceral 8), water 42 kg (28 L intracellular, 14 L extracellular) and minerals 3 kg.

**Why:** Slide "The average 70 kg male".

## 4. Avoidable factors: volume loss, hypothermia and oedema

- ⭐ **Five avoidable factors:** **1 volume loss (continuing haemorrhage), 2 hypothermia, 3 tissue oedema and underperfusion, 4 starvation, 5 immobility.**
- **Volume loss:** **pressor receptors** (carotid, aortic arch) and **left atrial volume receptors** → CNS → **aldosterone and ADH**; **pain also releases ADH**; **ADH → fluid retention.** **↓ pulse pressure** → **juxtaglomerular apparatus → renin-angiotensin → aldosterone** (Na⁺ reabsorption, water conserved); **ACTH augments.** Result: **postoperative oliguria**, **Na⁺/water retention**, **peripheral and visceral oedema** (↓ gastric emptying, delayed feeding, longer stay).
- **Hypothermia:** ↑ **adrenal steroids and catecholamines**; even mild → **2-3× arrhythmias** and ↑ catabolism; **normothermia ↓ wound infection, cardiac complications, bleeding/transfusion.**
- **Tissue oedema:** fluid, proteins, leukocytes leave vessels → ↓ **alveolar O₂ diffusion**, ↓ **renal function**; capillary leak mediated by **cytokines, prostanoids, bradykinin, nitric oxide.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Volume loss → ADH + aldosterone → oliguria and oedema.
- Hypothermia: 2-3× arrhythmias; keep normothermia.
- Capillary leak: cytokines, prostanoids, bradykinin, NO.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** Volume loss, hypothermia, tissue oedema and underperfusion, starvation and immobility.

**Why:** Slide "Avoidable factors".

**Why:** Slide "Hypothermia".

## 5. Starvation, immobility and prevention

- ⭐ **Starvation:** the brain needs **~100 g glucose/day**: **first 24 h from glycogen**, then **hepatic gluconeogenesis** (amino acids, glycerol, lactate). Other tissues use **fat**, mobilised by a **fall in insulin.** Eventually the liver turns **free fatty acids into ketone bodies**, a brain fuel that **reduces lean tissue loss.**
- **Immobility** is a **potent stimulus for muscle wasting** → avoid bed rest, **mobilise early.**
- ⭐ **Preventing unnecessary surgical stress:** **1 minimal access techniques, 2 blockade of afferent pain (epidural, spinal, wound catheters), 3 minimal starvation, 4 early mobilisation.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Brain needs 100 g glucose/day: glycogen 24 h, then gluconeogenesis, then ketones.
- Immobility causes muscle wasting.
- Prevent stress: minimal access, pain blockade, short fasting, early mobilisation.

</div>

**Answer:** Glycogen for the first 24 hours, then hepatic gluconeogenesis from amino acids, glycerol and lactate; later ketone bodies from free fatty acids spare lean tissue.

**Why:** Slides "Starvation".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Why avoid immobility?</summary>

**Answer:** It is a potent stimulus for muscle wasting, so avoid unnecessary bed rest and mobilise early.

**Why:** Slide "Immobility".
</details>
