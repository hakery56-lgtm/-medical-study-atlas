## 1. Calcium balance and functions

- **Calcium** = most abundant mineral: **1–1.5 kg** in adult man; **99% in bones and teeth.**
- **Balance:** adults **in balance**; **infancy/childhood positive** (skeletal growth); **older age negative**, especially **postmenopausal women** (→ **osteoporosis**); **pregnancy and lactation** lose calcium.
- **Functions:** **(1) enzyme activation — calmodulin (17,000 Da) binds 4 Ca²⁺; (2) muscle contraction (SR release, excitation-contraction coupling); (3) nerve transmission; (4) hormone secretion (insulin, PTH, calcitonin, vasopressin); (5) second messenger (with cAMP); (6) ↓ vascular permeability; (7) coagulation = factor IV; (8) bone and teeth.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 99% bone; calmodulin binds 4 Ca²⁺.
- Calcium = factor IV.

</div>

## 2. Plasma calcium and its regulation

| Plasma fraction | % of plasma calcium |
|---|---|
| **Ionized Ca²⁺** (physiologically important) | **50–65%** |
| **Bound to proteins (mainly albumin)** | **30–45%** |
| **Complexed with citrate etc.** | **5–10%** |

- **Raise plasma Ca²⁺:** **PTH** and **1,25-DHCC**; **lower it:** **calcitonin** (↓ osteoclast activity, ↓ renal reabsorption of calcium and phosphate).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Ionized fraction = active fraction.
- PTH and calcitriol raise; calcitonin lowers.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Give the plasma calcium fractions.</summary>

**Answer:** Ionized 50–65%, protein-bound 30–45%, complexed 5–10%.

**Why:** Slide 4.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which fraction is physiologically important?</summary>

**Answer:** The ionized Ca²⁺.

**Why:** Slide 3.
</details>

**Answer:** It lowers plasma calcium by reducing osteoclast activity and renal reabsorption of calcium and phosphate.

## 3. PTH

- **PTH = principal acute regulator**; **secreted when plasma Ca²⁺ falls**, suppressed when it rises (**negative feedback**).
- **Bone:** ↑ **osteoclast resorption** (via osteoblasts); severe hyperparathyroidism → **demineralization, subperiosteal resorption of terminal phalanges, bone cysts, pepper-pot skull.**
- **Kidney:** ↑ **distal Ca²⁺ reabsorption**, ↓ **proximal phosphate reabsorption**, ↑ **1α-hydroxylation**, ↑ **HCO₃⁻ loss** (mild metabolic acidosis).
- **Gut (indirect):** ↑ 1,25-DHCC → ↑ calcium absorption.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PTH: ↑ Ca²⁺, ↓ phosphate.
- Fall in Ca²⁺ → PTH ↑ → calcitriol ↑.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What stimulates PTH secretion?</summary>

**Answer:** A fall in plasma Ca²⁺; a rise suppresses it.

**Why:** Slide 5.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the renal actions of PTH.</summary>

**Answer:** Increases distal calcium reabsorption, reduces proximal phosphate reabsorption, promotes 1α-hydroxylation and increases bicarbonate loss.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe skeletal signs of severe hyperparathyroidism.</summary>

**Answer:** Demineralization, subperiosteal resorption of terminal phalanges, bone cysts and pepper-pot skull.

**Why:** Slide 6.

## 4. Vitamin D (1,25-DHCC)

- **D₃** made in **skin** (UV on **7-dehydrocholesterol**) or from food (**fish oils**).
- **Two hydroxylations:** **25-OH in liver → 25-HCC (calcidiol, main circulating form, highest in summer)**; **1α-OH in kidney → 1,25-DHCC (calcitriol, active).** **24-hydroxylase** → 24,25-DHCC.
- **1α-hydroxylation ↑ by:** **low phosphate, high PTH, hypocalcemia tendency.**
- **Action:** induces **intestinal Ca²⁺-binding protein** → ↑ calcium absorption. **Deficiency → defective mineralization.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Liver 25-OH, kidney 1α-OH.
- Calcitriol ↑ intestinal absorption.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe activation of vitamin D.</summary>

**Answer:** UV converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to D₃ in skin; liver 25-hydroxylation gives calcidiol; kidney 1α-hydroxylation gives calcitriol.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What stimulates renal 1α-hydroxylase?</summary>

**Answer:** Low phosphate, high PTH and a tendency to hypocalcemia.

**Why:** Slides 6–7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is the main action of calcitriol?</summary>

**Answer:** Induces a calcium-binding protein in intestinal cells for calcium absorption; deficiency causes defective mineralization.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

## 5. Laboratory investigation

- **Tests:** serum **calcium, albumin, phosphate, ALP**, ± magnesium, PTH, vitamin D metabolites.
- **Serum calcium 2.10–2.60 mmol/L** (total measured because ionized is technically difficult).
- **Albumin:** ↓ albumin → ↓ total calcium (ionized kept normal by PTH). **Adjusted Ca = measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 − albumin).**
- **pH:** **acidosis → ↑ ionized Ca²⁺; alkalosis → ↓ ionized Ca²⁺** (hyperventilation → **tetany**).
- **Phosphate 0.8–1.4 mmol/L** (fasting; 85% free).
- **Bone ALP ↑ in:** hyperparathyroidism, Paget disease, rickets, osteomalacia, osteoblastic metastases.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Adjusted calcium formula.
- Acidosis ↑ ionized calcium.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How does albumin affect total calcium?</summary>

**Answer:** Low albumin lowers total calcium while ionized calcium stays normal; use adjusted Ca = measured + 0.02 × (40 − albumin).

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does pH affect ionized calcium?</summary>

**Answer:** Acidosis reduces albumin binding so ionized calcium rises; alkalosis lowers it and can cause tetany.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. When is bone ALP raised?</summary>

**Answer:** In increased osteoblastic activity such as hyperparathyroidism, Paget disease, rickets, osteomalacia and osteoblastic metastases.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>
