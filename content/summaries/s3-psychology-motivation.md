## 1. Definition, components and types

- ⭐ **Motivation = biological and psychological processes that initiate, direct and sustain goal-oriented behavior.** It answers: **why start (initiation), why this goal (direction), why continue (persistence).**
- **Cannot be observed directly; inferred from behavior, choices, effort, persistence, reported goals;** the **same behavior may have different motives.**

| Component | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Activation** | **Starting a behavior** | **Student starts studying** |
| **Direction** | **Choosing the goal or action** | **Chooses to study psychiatry** |
| **Persistence** | **Continuing despite difficulties** | **Keeps studying while tired** |
| **Intensity** | **Amount of effort invested** | **Deep concentration, self-testing** |

- ⭐ **Intrinsic motivation:** **from within; the activity itself is rewarding** (curiosity, enjoyment). **Extrinsic motivation:** **to obtain an outcome separate from the activity or avoid a consequence** (score, salary, avoiding complications).
- ⚠️ **The slide's wording for "intrinsic" accidentally describes outside rewards; the examples (curiosity, enjoyment) show the real meaning.**

**Key points**
- Four components: activation, direction, persistence, intensity.
- Intrinsic = inherent enjoyment; extrinsic = separate outcome.

## 2. Drive-reduction and incentive theories

- ⭐ **Drive-reduction (Clark Hull):** **physiological needs → drives (tension) → behavior that reduces the drive.** **Need → drive → goal-directed behavior → drive reduction** (e.g. energy deficit → hunger → eating → satiety).
- ⭐ **Incentive theory:** **anticipated rewards or undesirable consequences influence behavior.** **Anticipated outcome → change in motivation → behavioral choice.** Examples: grade, salary, rehabilitation, avoiding smoking. **Clinical:** a diabetic exercises more when the benefit links to a personal goal (mobility).

| | **Drive-reduction** | **Incentive** |
|---|---|---|
| **Emphasis** | **Internal needs and drives** | **Anticipated outcomes (value)** |
| **Example** | **Hunger motivates eating** | **A dessert tempts eating without hunger** |
| **Limit** | **Doesn't explain all exploratory behavior** | **Rewards alone can't predict every behavior** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Drive-reduction = push from internal need; incentive = pull from anticipated reward.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain drive-reduction theory.</summary>

**Answer:** Clark Hull proposed that physiological needs create tension (drives) that motivate behavior reducing the drive, such as hunger leading to eating and satiety.

**Why:** Slide "Drive-reduction theory".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain incentive theory with a clinical application.</summary>

**Why:** Slide "Incentive theory".
</details>

## 3. Maslow, arousal and expectancy-value

- ⭐ **Maslow (5 levels, low → high):** **1 Physiological (food, water, sleep, oxygen); 2 Safety (security, stability); 3 Belonging (love, social connection); 4 Esteem (respect, competence); 5 Self-actualization (personal growth).** Example: a patient with housing problems struggles to prioritize long-term health.
- ⭐ **Arousal theory / Yerkes-Dodson:** **inverted-U**: **low arousal = poor alertness; moderate = supports concentration; excessive = impairs attention, working memory, decisions.** Mild exam anxiety helps; severe anxiety hurts recall.
- ⭐ **Expectancy-value:** **expectancy (likelihood of success) × value (importance of the outcome).** **Pearl: motivation depends on both expected success and outcome value.** Clinical: patient values mobility but believes exercise won't help → **realistic goals, explain benefits, review progress.**
- **Strong vs weak motivation:** strong = believes effort leads to success + values outcome; weak = success seems impossible + outcome unimportant.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Maslow: physiological → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualization.
- Yerkes-Dodson: moderate arousal is optimal.
- Expectancy-value: both expected success and value matter.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

**Answer:** Physiological, safety, belonging, esteem and self-actualization.

**Why:** Slide "Maslow's hierarchy".

## 4. Self-determination theory

- ⭐ **Three basic psychological needs:** **Autonomy** (behavior is voluntary and consistent with values; e.g. choosing a smoking-cessation strategy), **Competence** (can perform an activity effectively; e.g. completing a manageable exercise program), **Relatedness** (connection and belonging; e.g. supported by healthcare team and family).

| Theory | Main concept | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Drive-reduction** | **Needs create drives** | **Hungry person seeks food** |
| **Incentive** | **Anticipated rewards/consequences** | **Studying to pass** |
| **Maslow** | **Categories of needs** | **Housing struggles vs long-term health** |
| **Arousal** | **Arousal affects performance** | **Moderate vs excessive anxiety** |
| **Expectancy-value** | **Expected success × value** | **Studies harder when success is achievable and important** |
| **Self-determination** | **Autonomy, competence, relatedness** | **Patient engaged in decisions** |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- SDT: autonomy, competence, relatedness support high-quality motivation.

</div>

## 5. Neurobiology of motivation

- **Motivation arises from interactions among circuits for reward, learning, emotion, physiological regulation and executive control.**

| Structure | Role |
|---|---|
| **Mesolimbic dopamine pathway (VTA → nucleus accumbens)** | **Reward learning, incentive motivation, allocating effort** |
| **Prefrontal cortex** | **Planning, goal maintenance, decisions, inhibitory control; balances immediate reward vs future consequences** |
| **Hypothalamus** | **Hunger, thirst, energy balance, homeostasis** |
| **Amygdala** | **Emotionally significant stimuli (threats, rewards)** |

- ⭐ **Dopamine is NOT simply the pleasure chemical:** **reward prediction errors, reinforcement learning, incentive salience, motivated action.**
- ⭐ **Wanting = motivational pull; liking = pleasure.** **In addiction, craving (wanting) can persist when pleasure (liking) has faded.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- VTA → nucleus accumbens = reward/incentive motivation.
- Wanting ≠ liking; dopamine ≠ pleasure.

</div>

## 6. Motivation and psychiatric disorders

| Condition | Motivational change |
|---|---|
| **Major depressive disorder** | **Reduced initiative and interest (anhedonia, fatigue, hopelessness, psychomotor change)** |
| **Mania** | **Increased goal-directed activity; elevated or irritable mood, ↑ energy, ↓ need for sleep** |
| **Schizophrenia** | **Avolition** |
| **Substance use disorders** | **Strong substance-related motivation (craving, learned associations)** |
| **Parkinson's disease** | **Apathy** |
| **Hypothyroidism** | **Reduced energy or initiative** |
| **Anxiety / ADHD / OCD / eating / personality disorders** | **Avoidance / preference for immediate rewards / anxiety-reducing rituals / distorted reward processing / unstable goals** |

- ⭐ **Apathy** = broad reduction in motivation, interest, emotional engagement; **avolition** = reduced initiation and persistence of goal-directed activity; **anhedonia** = reduced interest or pleasure; **fatigue** = subjective exhaustion.
- **Clinical implications:** **assess motivation; realistic goals; behavioral activation; treat underlying symptoms; reinforcement strategies; combine drugs and psychosocial care.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Avolition (schizophrenia) ≠ anhedonia (depression).
- Mania ↑ goal-directed activity; depression ↓.

</div>
