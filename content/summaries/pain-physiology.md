## 1. Definition, significance, threshold and tolerance

- ⭐ **Pain** = an **unpleasant sensory and emotional experience** associated with **actual or potential tissue damage**.
- **Nerve fibers:** myelinated **Aα** (thick), **Aβ** (intermediate), **Aδ** (thin); unmyelinated **C**. **All pain receptors are free nerve endings of unmyelinated C fibers and small Aδ fibers.**
- **Protective function:** makes us aware of damage; helps us **avoid noxious stimuli, remove body parts from danger, promote healing** by preventing further damage and **store painful experiences in memory**.
- **Biological function:** immobility and withdrawal give an environment for **healing and restoration of function**.
- Pain is accompanied by **behavioral** (withdrawal, defense) and **emotional** (crying, fear) responses and is **perceived at cortical and thalamic levels**.

| | Threshold | Tolerance |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | **Minimum** stimulus that causes pain | **Maximum** pain a person can handle |
| **Variation** | **Same in each of us** | **Variable**: environment, psychological/emotional factors, age, ongoing pain, placebo effect |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Pain receptors = free nerve endings of C and Aδ fibers.
- Threshold = same in everyone; tolerance = variable.
- Pain protects: avoidance, withdrawal, healing, memory.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define pain and mention its protective functions.</summary>

**Answer:** An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage. It makes us avoid noxious stimuli, remove body parts from danger, promote healing by preventing further damage, and store painful experiences in memory to avoid future harm.

**Why:** Standard opening question.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Differentiate pain threshold from pain tolerance.</summary>

**Answer:** Threshold is the minimum stimulus that causes pain and is the same in each person. Tolerance is the maximum pain a person can handle and varies with environment, psychological and emotional factors, age, ongoing pain and the placebo effect.

**Why:** Threshold is fixed; tolerance is variable.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which nerve fibers form the pain receptors?</summary>

**Answer:** Free nerve endings of unmyelinated type C fibers and small-diameter type Aδ fibers.

**Why:** Aα and Aβ fibers are not pain fibers.
</details>

## 2. Pain receptors (nociceptors)

- **Special receptors that respond only to noxious stimuli**; they are **specific** (pain is not produced by overstimulating other receptors).
- **Distribution:** widespread in the **superficial skin**, fewer in deep tissue, **absent in brain tissue**. Densest in **skin, periosteum, joint surfaces, arterial walls and dura mater**.
- **Stimuli:** **mechanical** and **thermal** → **fast pain**; **chemical** → **slow pain**.
- **High-threshold** receptors: stimulus must be strong; **heat pain > 43 °C**.
- **Do not adapt** (or very little) — so pain keeps warning while damage persists; they can be **sensitized** (e.g. by **prostaglandins**).
- **Types:** mechanoreceptor nociceptors (pinch, pinprick), **polymodal** (most prevalent: pressure, temperature extremes, algogens), thermal, and **silent nociceptors** (respond only with inflammation).
- **Third-degree burn:** usually **no pain** in the burnt area because the receptors are **destroyed** with the dermis.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Specific, high threshold (>43 °C), non-adapting.
- Mechanical/thermal = fast; chemical = slow.
- Absent in the brain; destroyed in 3rd-degree burns.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the properties of nociceptors.</summary>

**Answer:** They respond only to noxious stimuli (specific), have a high threshold (heat pain above 43 °C), adapt very little or not at all, and can be sensitized by agents such as prostaglandins. They are widespread in the superficial skin, fewer in deep tissue and absent in brain tissue.

**Why:** Collects the slide bullets.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What stimuli activate nociceptors and what pain do they produce?</summary>

**Answer:** Mechanical and thermal stimuli produce fast pain; chemical stimuli produce slow pain.

**Why:** Links stimulus type with pain type.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is there no pain in a third-degree burn?</summary>

**Answer:** The burn destroys the full thickness of the skin, so the pain receptors are obliterated along with the dermis.

**Why:** A frequent clinical application.
</details>

## 3. Chemical mediators of pain

Pain receptors are depolarized directly or by **pain-producing substances** from damaged tissue (inflammatory mediators): **bradykinin, histamine, substance P, CGRP, interleukins, prostaglandins, K⁺, ACh, proteolytic enzymes, acids**. ⭐ **Prostaglandins and substance P** enhance the sensitivity of the receptors.

| Substance | Source |
|---|---|
| **K⁺** | Damaged cells |
| **Serotonin** | Platelets |
| **Bradykinin** | Plasma |
| **Histamine** | Mast cells |
| **Prostaglandins, leukotrienes** | Damaged cells |
| **Substance P** | Primary nerve afferents |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- PGs and substance P sensitize nociceptors.
- Source table: K⁺ = damaged cells, serotonin = platelets, bradykinin = plasma, histamine = mast cells.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List five chemical substances released in tissue damage and their sources.</summary>

**Answer:** K⁺ (damaged cells), serotonin (platelets), bradykinin (plasma), histamine (mast cells), prostaglandins and leukotrienes (damaged cells), substance P (primary nerve afferents).

**Why:** Table from the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which mediators increase the sensitivity of pain receptors?</summary>

**Answer:** Prostaglandins and substance P.

**Why:** Explains why NSAIDs (which block PG synthesis) reduce pain.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How are pain receptors stimulated in inflammation?</summary>

**Answer:** They are depolarized either directly or through inflammatory mediators released from damaged tissue (bradykinin, histamine, substance P, CGRP, interleukins, prostaglandins, K⁺, acids…).

**Why:** Transduction mechanism.
</details>

## 4. Nociception: the four stages

**Nociception** = non-conscious neural transmission of signals from nociceptors to the CNS. Its stages:

1. **Transduction** — activation of nociceptors; the stimulus becomes nerve impulses.
2. **Transmission** — relay of action potentials along nociceptive fibers to the CNS.
3. **Modulation** — alteration (inhibition or facilitation) by other peripheral nerves or CNS mechanisms.
4. **Perception** — interpretation by the brain of the input as pain.

*(Latin **noci-** = harm or injury.)*

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Transduction → transmission → modulation → perception.
- Nociception itself is non-conscious; perception is the conscious step.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define nociception and list its stages.</summary>

**Answer:** The non-conscious neural transmission of signals evoked by nociceptor activation from the periphery to the CNS. Stages: transduction, transmission, modulation, perception.

**Why:** Always asked together.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What happens in transduction?</summary>

**Answer:** Nociceptors are activated by a stimulus and convert it into nerve impulses.

**Why:** First step at the receptor.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Differentiate modulation from perception.</summary>

**Answer:** Modulation is the inhibition or facilitation of the pain signal by other nerves or CNS mechanisms; perception is the brain's interpretation of the input as pain.

**Why:** Different levels of the pathway.
</details>

## 5. Pain transmission: fibers, spinal cord and tracts

| | **Aδ fibers** | **C fibers** |
|---|---|---|
| Myelination | **Myelinated** | **Unmyelinated** |
| Diameter | 2–5 μm | 0.4–1.2 μm |
| Velocity | **12–30 m/s** | **0.5–2 m/s** |
| Pain | **Fast, sharp, well localized, acute** | **Slow, dull, diffuse, poorly localized, chronic** |
| Dorsal horn | **Laminae I and V** (marginal zone) | **Laminae II and III** (**substantia gelatinosa**) |
| Transmitter | **Glutamate** | **Substance P** |

- **Projection neurons** of the dorsal horn cross and project to the **contralateral thalamus**.
- ⭐ **Two ascending pathways:**

| | **Neospinothalamic (lateral STT)** | **Paleospinothalamic (ventral STT) / spinoreticular** |
|---|---|---|
| Fibers | Aδ | C (+ some Aδ) |
| Carries | **Fast acute pain, thermal pain** | **Slow chronic pain**, crude touch & pressure, itch & tickle, sexual sensations |
| Ends in | **Ventrobasal complex and posterior nuclei of the thalamus** → somatosensory cortex | **Brain stem reticular formation, intralaminar thalamic nuclei, hypothalamus** |
| Effect | Well localized | **Arousal** from sleep; poorly localized |

- **Somatosensory cortex** = **topognosis** (localization and quality of pain). **Fast pain is better localized** because the neospinothalamic tract reaches the cortex, while only a small part of the paleospinothalamic pathway does.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Aδ = glutamate, laminae I & V, fast; C = substance P, laminae II & III, slow.
- Neospinothalamic = fast, ventrobasal thalamus; paleospinothalamic = slow, reticular formation, arousal.
- Cortex localizes fast pain.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare Aδ and C fibers.</summary>

**Answer:** Aδ fibers are myelinated, 2–5 μm, conduct at 12–30 m/s, end in laminae I and V, use glutamate and carry fast, sharp, well-localized pain. C fibers are unmyelinated, 0.4–1.2 μm, conduct at 0.5–2 m/s, end in laminae II and III (substantia gelatinosa), use substance P and carry slow, dull, diffuse pain.

**Why:** The core comparison table.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the dual pain pathways.</summary>

**Answer:** The neospinothalamic (lateral spinothalamic) tract carries fast Aδ pain and thermal pain to the ventrobasal complex and posterior nuclei of the thalamus. The paleospinothalamic (ventral spinothalamic/spinoreticular) tract carries slow C-fiber pain and crude touch, itch and tickle to the brain stem reticular formation, intralaminar thalamic nuclei and hypothalamus.

**Why:** Fast vs slow tracts.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Why is fast pain better localized than slow pain?</summary>

**Answer:** Fast pain signals travel in the neospinothalamic tract directly to the somatosensory cortex, which localizes the pain; only a small proportion of the paleospinothalamic pathway reaches the cortex.

**Why:** Role of the cortex in topognosis.
</details>

## 6. Fast vs slow pain (double pain)

| | **Fast (first) pain** | **Slow (second) pain** |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Sharp, intense, **pricking** | **Burning, aching, throbbing**, dull |
| Onset | Within **0.1 s** | After **1 s or more** |
| Example | Pinprick, knife cut | Chronic, tissue destruction |
| Localization | **Well localized** | **Poorly localized** |
| Fibers / tract | **Aδ** / neospinothalamic | **C** / paleospinothalamic |
| Receptors | Mechanical and thermal | **Polymodal** |
| Other | Reflex withdrawal; usually **somatic** | Skin or **any organ**; the **emotional aspect** of pain |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Fast = 0.1 s, Aδ, localized, reflex withdrawal.
- Slow = ≥1 s, C, diffuse, emotional, can be visceral.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare fast and slow pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Fast pain is sharp, felt within 0.1 s, well localized, mediated by Aδ fibers and the neospinothalamic tract, and linked to reflex withdrawal. Slow pain is burning, dull, felt after 1 s or more, poorly localized, mediated by C fibers and the paleospinothalamic tract, and carries the emotional aspect.

**Why:** "Phenomenon of double pain."
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which receptors respond for each type?</summary>

**Answer:** Fast pain follows mechanical and thermal nociceptors; slow pain follows polymodal receptors.

**Why:** Links the receptor with the speed of pain.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Which type of pain is associated with tissue destruction and the emotional aspect?</summary>

**Answer:** Slow pain, carried by C fibers.

**Why:** C fibers = "misery".
</details>

## 7. Pain modulation

- Modulation occurs **peripherally** (at the nociceptor), in the **spinal cord**, or **centrally** (supraspinal), and can **inhibit or facilitate** pain.

### Gate control theory (Melzack & Wall, 1965)
Pain depends on the **balance of large-fiber vs small-fiber input** to the cord.

| Situation | Inhibitory interneuron (I) in the substantia gelatinosa | Projection neuron (P) | Gate | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **No stimulation** | Active, blocks P | Blocked | **Closed** | **No pain** |
| **Non-painful (large fibers)** | **Activated** | Activated but blocked by I | **Closed** | **No pain** |
| **Painful (small fibers)** | **Blocked** | Free to fire | **Open** | **PAIN** |

*(Myelinated afferents excite the inhibitory interneurons; unmyelinated nociceptors inhibit them.)*

### Descending modulation and endogenous analgesia
- Inhibition from higher centers comes mainly from the ⭐ **periaqueductal grey (PAG, midbrain)** and the **raphe nucleus (medulla)**, which **excite the inhibitory interneurons** of the substantia gelatinosa.
- **Endogenous opioids:** **enkephalins, endorphins, dynorphins, endomorphins**. They **block neurotransmitter release (inhibit Ca²⁺ influx)** and **open K⁺ channels (hyperpolarization)**.
- **Perception** in the thalamus and cortex depends on circumstances and past experience; thalamocortical projections carry **location, intensity and nature** of pain.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Gate closed by large fibers; opened by small fibers.
- PAG + raphe → excite inhibitory interneurons → less pain.
- Opioid peptides: ↓Ca²⁺ influx, ↑K⁺ conductance.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Explain the gate control theory.</summary>

**Answer:** Pain is determined by the balance of large- and small-fiber input. With no stimulus or with non-painful large-fiber stimulation, the inhibitory interneuron blocks the projection neuron and the gate is closed. With painful small-fiber stimulation, the interneuron is blocked, the projection neuron fires and the gate is open, so pain is felt.

**Why:** Describes the three scenarios on the slides.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the descending pain modulation system?</summary>

**Answer:** The periaqueductal grey (midbrain) and raphe nucleus (medulla) excite the inhibitory interneurons of the substantia gelatinosa, reducing pain transmission. Endogenous opioids (enkephalins, endorphins, dynorphins, endomorphins) act on this system.

**Why:** Endogenous opiate theory.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do opioid peptides inhibit nociception?</summary>

**Answer:** They block neurotransmitter release by inhibiting Ca²⁺ influx into the presynaptic terminal and open potassium channels, which hyperpolarizes neurons and inhibits spike activity.

**Why:** Two cellular mechanisms.
</details>

## 8. Types of pain: neuropathic, phantom, migraine, referred

- **Classification:** by **duration** (acute vs chronic) and by **nature** (nociceptive, non-nociceptive; visceral, somatic; neuropathic; sympathetic).
- ⭐ **Neuropathic pain:** damage or disease of the **somatosensory system**; described as **burning, tingling, electrical, stabbing, "pins and needles"**.
- ⭐ **Phantom pain:** pain felt in a **lost body part** (amputees); a neuropathic pain, **unresponsive to morphine**.
  - **Peripheral:** damaged nociceptive fibers, abnormal discharge from **neuromas** at the cut nerve ends.
  - **Spinal:** sprouting of nociceptive terminals, sensitized second-order neurons.
  - **Higher centers:** abnormal flow through the somatosensory cortex or burst-firing thalamic neurons.
- **Migraine:** a **central** pain, **not sensitive to morphine**; abnormal cerebral vascular function — **vasospasm followed by vasodilatation**.
- ⭐ **Referred pain:** visceral pain felt at a **somatic** site, often far from the origin; **poorly localized**; follows the **dermatomal rule**; common with **visceral and deep somatic** pain, **not cutaneous**.
  - **Convergence theory:** visceral and skin afferents from the same embryonic segment **converge on the same second-order neuron (lamina V)**; the brain assumes the skin is the source.
  - **Facilitation theory:** impulses from the diseased viscus **raise the excitability** of second-order neurons receiving skin pain fibers.

| Visceral source | Referred to |
|---|---|
| **Heart** | Jaw, **left shoulder**, inner **left arm** |
| **Appendix** | **Periumbilical** region |
| **Ureter** | **Testicular** region |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Neuropathic: burning/tingling; phantom + migraine do not respond to morphine.
- Referred pain = convergence (lamina V) + facilitation; follows dermatomes.
- Heart → left arm/jaw; appendix → umbilicus; ureter → testis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is phantom limb pain and what are its mechanisms?</summary>

**Answer:** Pain felt in a part of the body that has been lost, common in amputees; a neuropathic pain unresponsive to morphine. Mechanisms: abnormal discharges from neuromas at the cut nerve ends (peripheral), sprouting of nociceptive terminals and sensitized spinal neurons (spinal), and abnormal signals through the somatosensory cortex or bursting thalamic neurons (higher centers).

**Why:** Three levels of mechanism.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the mechanism of referred pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Convergence theory: afferents from the diseased viscus and from skin of the same embryonic segment converge on the same second-order neuron (lamina V) and the brain interprets the input as coming from the skin. Facilitation theory: impulses from the viscus raise the excitability of second-order neurons that also receive skin pain fibers, so pain is projected to the skin dermatome.

**Why:** Two theories listed in the lecture.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give examples of referred pain.</summary>

**Answer:** Cardiac pain to the jaw, left shoulder and inner side of the left arm; appendicitis pain to the periumbilical region; ureteric pain to the testicular region.

**Why:** Always asked with the mechanism.
</details>

## 9. Chronic and central pain

- ⭐ **Chronic pain** = persistent or recurrent pain **beyond the usual course of acute illness/injury, or lasting more than 3–6 months**; "pain that continues when it should not."
- **Classification:** **nociceptive** (superficial, deep somatic, visceral) vs **neuropathic** (peripheral, central).
  - Nociceptive examples: postoperative pain, mechanical low back pain, sickle cell crisis, arthritis, sports injuries.
  - Neuropathic examples: postherpetic neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, distal polyneuropathy (diabetic, HIV), complex regional pain syndrome.
- **Pathophysiology — wind-up:** persistent nociceptive input to the dorsal horn causes a **frequency-dependent increase in excitability of spinal neurons** (glutamate acting on **NMDA receptors**); it **lowers the threshold** and can make non-nociceptive fibers respond to pain signals; difficult to reverse.
- **Central pain:** damage or malfunction in the CNS (spinothalamic tract, thalamus, somatosensory cortex, lesions of inhibitory pathways) → **sensitization** of the pain system via wind-up; **often insensitive to opioids**, but **anti-epileptic drugs** can help.
- **Dejerine–Roussy (thalamic pain) syndrome:** after a **thalamic stroke**; first loss of sensation and tingling on the **opposite side**, weeks to months later **severe chronic pain** (**dysaesthesia/allodynia**).
- **Chronic pain syndrome:** ≥3–6 months, complex, poor response to therapy, widespread or ≥2 coexisting pain conditions.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Chronic pain >3–6 months; nociceptive vs neuropathic.
- Wind-up = NMDA-mediated spinal hyperexcitability.
- Central pain: opioid-insensitive, helped by anti-epileptics; Dejerine–Roussy after thalamic stroke.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define chronic pain and classify it.</summary>

**Answer:** Persistent or recurrent pain lasting beyond the usual course of an acute illness or injury, or more than 3–6 months. It is classified into nociceptive (superficial, deep somatic, visceral) and neuropathic (peripheral, central).

**Why:** Definition + classification.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain wind-up.</summary>

**Answer:** Continuous C-fiber input to the dorsal horn produces a frequency-dependent rise in the excitability of the projection neurons through glutamate acting on NMDA receptors. This lowers the threshold for pain, may let non-nociceptive fibers signal pain, and is hard to reverse.

**Why:** Key mechanism of chronic pain.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is Dejerine–Roussy syndrome?</summary>

**Answer:** A central (thalamic) pain syndrome after a thalamic stroke. It begins with loss of sensation and tingling on the opposite side of the body; weeks to months later it becomes severe chronic pain disproportionate to the stimulus (dysaesthesia or allodynia).

**Why:** Classic central pain example.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Pain receptors = free nerve endings of C and Aδ fibers** — not Aα or Aβ.
- **Threshold is the same in everyone; tolerance varies.**
- **Aδ = laminae I & V, glutamate, fast; C = laminae II & III, substance P, slow.** Don't swap them.
- **Neospinothalamic = fast/localized (thalamus → cortex); paleospinothalamic = slow/diffuse (reticular formation, arousal).**
- **Gate:** large fibers **close** it; small fibers **open** it.
- **Phantom limb pain, migraine and central pain do not respond well to morphine.**
- **Referred pain** follows the **dermatomal rule** and is **not** seen with cutaneous pain.
- **Third-degree burns are not painful** in the burnt area (receptors destroyed).
- **Nociceptors don't adapt.**
