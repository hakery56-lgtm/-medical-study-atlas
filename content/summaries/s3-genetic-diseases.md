## 1. Basic terms and mutations

- **Gene** = DNA part coding a **polypeptide**; **2% exons, 24% introns, 74% non-coding outside genes**; **~30,000 genes**, unevenly spread (so some deletions or duplications have little effect).
- **Locus** = gene position; **46 chromosomes = 22 autosome pairs + XX or XY**; **genotype** (genetic makeup) vs **phenotype** (manifest feature); **allele** = alternative gene form (2 per gene); **mutation** = permanent DNA change; **germ-cell mutations are transmitted, somatic ones are not.**
- **Behavior:** **dominant** (one mutated allele is enough), **recessive** (both alleles), **sex-linked.**
- **Point mutations:** **silent** (same amino acid), **missense** (another amino acid; **sickle cell**), **nonsense** (**stop codon UAA, UGA, UAG; β-thalassemia**).
- **Frame-shift:** insertion or deletion **not a multiple of 3** → premature termination (**25%** of disease-causing mutations; **Tay-Sachs: 4-base insertion, ↓ hexosaminidase**). **Multiple of 3** = no frameshift: amino acids gained or lost (**cystic fibrosis: ΔPhe, 3-base deletion**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Silent / missense / nonsense.
- Frameshift if not a multiple of 3.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Define genotype, phenotype, allele and locus.</summary>

**Answer:** Genotype: genetic makeup; phenotype: manifest feature; allele: alternative form of a gene; locus: position of the gene on the chromosome.

**Why:** Slide 6.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Classify point mutations.</summary>

**Answer:** Silent (same amino acid), missense (different amino acid, e.g., sickle cell), nonsense (stop codon, e.g., β-thalassemia).

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Contrast frameshift and in-frame deletions with examples.</summary>

**Answer:** Bases not a multiple of 3 shift the reading frame and truncate the protein (Tay-Sachs); multiples of 3 keep the frame but lose or add amino acids (cystic fibrosis ΔPhe).

**Why:** Slides 11-12.
</details>

## 2. Classification of genetic disorders and AD / AR inheritance

- **Classification:** **single-gene (Mendelian)**: AD, AR, XD, XR; **multifactorial (gene + environment):** diabetes, hypertension, gout, coronary heart disease; **chromosomal:** monosomy (**Turner**), trisomy (**Down**).

| Feature | **Autosomal dominant (AD)** | **Autosomal recessive (AR)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Expression** | **Heterozygous** state | **Homozygous** state only |
| **Protein** | **Structural or non-enzyme** | **Enzyme** (inborn errors of metabolism) |
| **Onset** | **Variable**, may be adult | **Early, uniform** (childhood) |
| **Penetrance** | **Reduced**; **variable expressivity** | **Complete** |
| **Clue** | **Male-to-male transmission** | Both parents carry; if one carrier + one normal parent with affected child → **uniparental disomy** |
| **Examples** | **Marfan (fibrillin, 15q21), neurofibromatosis, EDS, osteogenesis imperfecta, achondroplasia, tuberous sclerosis, hereditary spherocytosis, von Willebrand, myotonic dystrophy, familial hypercholesterolemia** | **PKU, alkaptonuria, glycogen and lysosomal storage diseases, hemochromatosis, 21-hydroxylase deficiency, Wilson, thalassemia, sickle cell, cystic fibrosis** |

- **Uniparental disomy** = two copies of a chromosome (or part) from **one parent**, none from the other.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- AR is the most common Mendelian pattern.
- Reduced penetrance and variable expressivity are AD features.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare AD and AR inheritance.</summary>

**Answer:** AD: heterozygous expression, structural proteins, variable late onset, reduced penetrance; AR: homozygous, enzyme defects, early uniform onset, complete penetrance.

**Why:** Slides 17-20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Define reduced penetrance and variable expressivity.</summary>

**Answer:** Reduced penetrance: carriers may be phenotypically normal; variable expressivity: carriers show the trait differently (e.g., neurofibromatosis).

**Why:** Slide 17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is uniparental disomy?</summary>

**Answer:** Receiving two copies of a chromosome from one parent and none from the other; suspected when an AR disease appears with only one carrier parent.

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

## 3. X-linked inheritance

- **X-linked recessive:** Y has no matching locus → **males affected**, **females carriers**; **carrier mother → 50% of sons affected**; **affected father → all daughters carriers**; example **fragile X.**
- **X-linked dominant:** **males and females** affected; **affected father → all daughters affected, no sons**; **affected mother → half of sons and daughters**; example **vitamin D-resistant rickets** (↓ renal/GI phosphate reabsorption → **hypophosphatemia, osteomalacia**).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- XR: carrier mother, 50% of sons; father passes to all daughters.
- XD: affected father → all daughters, no sons.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe X-linked recessive inheritance.</summary>

**Answer:** Males express the mutation (no Y locus), females with a normal second X are carriers; carrier mothers pass to 50% of sons, affected fathers to all daughters.

**Why:** Slide 21.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe X-linked dominant inheritance.</summary>

**Answer:** Both sexes affected; all daughters but no sons of an affected father are affected; an affected mother transmits to half of her children.

**Why:** Slide 22.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is vitamin D-resistant rickets?</summary>

**Answer:** An X-linked dominant disease with impaired phosphate reabsorption (hypophosphatemia) causing defective bone mineralization (osteomalacia).

**Why:** Slide 22.
</details>

## 4. Non-Mendelian inheritance

- **Mitochondrial:** **maternal** (the ovum has abundant mitochondria); **all offspring of an affected mother are affected; only daughters transmit; affected sons do not**; follows **population genetics**.
- **Genomic imprinting:** **differential expression depending on parent of origin** (**methylation/silencing**); **deletion of 15q11-13:** **paternal → Prader-Willi** (hyperphagia, obesity, short stature, hypogonadism, hypotonia, mental retardation), **maternal → Angelman** (inappropriate laughter "happy puppets", seizures, ataxia, speech delay, mental retardation). In the **maternal** chromosome the **PW genes are imprinted** and the **Angelman gene active**; in the **paternal** chromosome the **PW genes are active** and the **Angelman gene imprinted.**
- **Triple repeat mutations:** **long repeats of three nucleotides**, **dynamic** (amplification **increases during gametogenesis**).
- **Germline (gonadal) mosaicism:** **postzygotic mutation in gonadal precursor cells**; gametes carry it, **somatic cells normal**; a **normal parent can transmit** the mutation.
- **Mosaicism:** **two or more cell populations in one individual** (e.g., 47,XXX and 45,X).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Paternal 15q deletion = PWS; maternal = Angelman.
- Triple repeats are dynamic; mitochondrial inheritance is maternal.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe mitochondrial inheritance.</summary>

**Answer:** Maternal transmission: all children of an affected woman are affected, only her daughters transmit it further, affected men do not.

**Why:** Slide 24.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain genomic imprinting with Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes.</summary>

**Answer:** Expression depends on parental origin (methylation silences one copy); paternal 15q11-13 deletion causes Prader-Willi, maternal deletion causes Angelman.

**Why:** Slides 25-28.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is germline mosaicism?</summary>

**Answer:** A postzygotic mutation limited to cells forming the gonads; gametes carry it though the parent is normal and can transmit the disease.

**Why:** Slide 31.
</details>

## 5. Chromosomal disorders: general

- **Diploid** somatic cells: **46 chromosomes** (22 autosome pairs + 1 sex pair, XX or XY); **gametes haploid (23).**
- **Karyotype:** chromosomes arranged by **size and centromere position.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- 46 in somatic cells; 23 in gametes.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. State the normal chromosome numbers.</summary>

**Answer:** Somatic cells 46 (22 autosome pairs and one sex pair); gametes 23.

**Why:** Slide 33.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What does a karyotype show?</summary>

**Answer:** The chromosomes arranged by size and centromere position, with sex chromosomes last.

**Why:** Slides 34-35.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Give examples of chromosomal disorders.</summary>

**Answer:** Monosomy such as Turner syndrome and trisomy such as Down syndrome.

**Why:** Slide 14.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Missense (sickle cell) vs nonsense (β-thalassemia).**
- **Frameshift only if the change is not a multiple of 3.**
- **AR is the most common Mendelian type; male-to-male transmission means AD.**
- **XR: father → daughters carriers; XD: father → all daughters affected.**
- **Paternal deletion = Prader-Willi; maternal deletion = Angelman.**
- **Mitochondrial inheritance is maternal only.**
