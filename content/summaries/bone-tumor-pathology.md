## 1. Normal bone and overview of bone tumors

- The skeleton gives **mechanical support, mineral homeostasis, hematopoiesis, protection of viscera** and determines **body size and shape**; it has **206 bones** (tubular, flat, cuboid).
- **Normal bone:** **organic matrix** = cells, **type I collagen**, **osteocalcin**, glycoproteins and proteoglycans; **inorganic matrix** = **calcium hydroxyapatite** plus magnesium, potassium, chloride, sodium, fluoride. Diagrams: **epiphysis, physis, metaphysis, diaphysis**, compact and cancellous bone, **periosteum**, osteon with **Haversian and Volkmann canals**; histology: **woven vs lamellar bone**, osteocytes, osteoblasts.
- ⭐ **Primary bone tumors** are classified by their **normal cell of origin / line of differentiation**.
- **Most frequent benign lesions:** **osteochondroma** and **fibrous cortical defect**. **Most common primary bone cancer:** **osteosarcoma**, followed by **chondrosarcoma** and **Ewing sarcoma**. Tumors in the **elderly** are much more likely **malignant**.
- Benign lesions are often **asymptomatic (incidental)**, or cause **pain or a slow mass**; occasionally a **pathologic fracture** is first. **Imaging is important but biopsy and microscopy give the final diagnosis.**
- **Metastatic tumors are much more common than primary bone malignancy.**
- Note: slide 5 ranks osteosarcoma, then chondrosarcoma, then Ewing sarcoma, while slide 14 calls chondrosarcoma third after myeloma and osteosarcoma; know the slide wording.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Classification by cell of origin; metastases outnumber primary bone cancers.
- Osteochondroma and fibrous cortical defect = commonest benign; osteosarcoma = commonest primary malignant.
- Imaging suggests; biopsy confirms.

</div>

## 2. Bone-forming tumors: osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma, osteosarcoma

| Feature | **Osteoid osteoma** | **Osteoblastoma** |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior | **Benign**, identical histology | **Benign**, identical histology |
| Size | **< 2 cm**, thick rim of reactive cortical bone | **> 2 cm** |
| Patient | **Male > female**, age **10–30 yr** | — |
| Site | **Extremities: femur or tibia** | **Spine** |
| Pain | **Nocturnal, relieved by aspirin** | **Dull, achy, unresponsive to salicylates** |

- **X-ray:** central radiolucency with sclerotic rim (osteoblastoma usually without marked bony reaction). **Microscopy:** **central nidus of osteoid** in dense sclerotic reactive cortical bone; haphazardly interconnecting **woven bone trabeculae** with osteoblasts.

### Osteosarcoma
- ⭐ **Most common primary malignant bone tumor**; a **bone-producing malignant mesenchymal tumor**; **60% male**, ages **10–25** (75% under 20); **second peak in the elderly** (secondary).
- **Etiology:** **familial retinoblastoma**, **Paget disease** (after age 40), **post-radiation** (10–15 years later), **chronic osteomyelitis**. **Not associated with trauma.**
- **Sites:** **metaphysis** of long bones: **distal femur, proximal tibia, proximal humerus**. **Metastasis:** **lung (98%)**, other bones, rarely lymph nodes, GI tract, liver, brain.
- **Gross:** large firm white-tan mass with **necrosis and hemorrhage**. **Micro:** **anaplastic pleomorphic cells** with hyperchromatic nuclei, bizarre giant cells, mitoses, **producing osteoid and bone**; **direct production of mineralized or unmineralized bone (osteoid) is essential for diagnosis**; bone is **fine, lace-like** (or broad sheets); abundant malignant cartilage = **chondroblastic osteosarcoma**.
- **Pathogenesis:** **RB mutations** (sporadic and hereditary retinoblastoma with germ-line RB mutation); also **p53, cyclins**.
- **Clinical:** **painful enlarging mass**, localized pain and swelling. **X-ray:** large destructive **mixed lytic and blastic** mass with infiltrating margins; **Codman triangle** (triangular shadow between cortex and raised periosteum; characteristic, **not specific**) and **sunburst pattern**.
- **Spread** hematogenously; **10–20%** have **lung metastases at diagnosis**; **prognosis poor**.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Osteoid osteoma: < 2 cm, night pain relieved by aspirin; osteoblastoma: > 2 cm, spine, no response to salicylates.
- Osteosarcoma: ages 10–25, metaphysis (knee), osteoid by malignant cells, Codman triangle and sunburst, lung metastasis.
- Risks: familial retinoblastoma (RB), Paget disease, radiation, chronic osteomyelitis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare osteoid osteoma with osteoblastoma.</summary>

**Answer:** Both are benign with identical histology. Osteoid osteoma is < 2 cm, occurs in the femur or tibia and causes nocturnal pain relieved by aspirin; osteoblastoma is > 2 cm, involves the spine and causes dull pain not relieved by salicylates.

**Why:** Slide 7.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the pathology and diagnosis of osteosarcoma.</summary>

**Answer:** A large white-tan metaphyseal mass with necrosis and hemorrhage; anaplastic pleomorphic cells with bizarre giant cells and mitoses that directly produce osteoid or bone, which is essential for diagnosis.

**Why:** Slide 9.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the radiologic and clinical features of osteosarcoma?</summary>

**Answer:** Painful enlarging mass; X-ray shows a destructive mixed lytic-blastic mass with Codman triangle and sunburst pattern; spreads hematogenously to the lung (10–20% at diagnosis); poor prognosis.

**Why:** Slide 10.
</details>

## 3. Cartilage-forming tumors

| Tumor | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Osteochondroma (exostosis)** | **Most common benign bone tumor**; **M:F 3:1**; **metaphysis of long bones near the knee**; **cartilage-capped** growth from the **epiphyseal growth plate**; slow-growing, painful if it **impinges on nerves** or the **stalk fractures**; **rarely → chondrosarcoma**; **mushroom-shaped**, 1–20 cm; **solitary** (late adolescence/early adulthood) vs **multiple hereditary exostosis** (childhood, **autosomal dominant**) |
| **Chondroma** | **Benign hyaline cartilage** tumor of endochondral bones; **enchondroma** (medullary) or **juxtacortical** (surface); age **20–50**; **< 3 cm**, **gray-blue translucent**; well-circumscribed hyaline nodules with benign chondrocytes; X-ray: **lucency with central calcification, sclerotic rim, intact cortex** |
| **Chondrosarcoma** | **Malignant cartilage-forming**, **no osteoid**; **third** most common bone malignancy (slide: after myeloma and osteosarcoma); **older patients, peak sixth decade, 75% male**; **pelvis, spine, shoulder girdle**; painful enlarging mass; **conventional vs variants**; **central (medullary), peripheral (cortical), juxtacortical (periosteal)**; **gross:** medullary mass eroding the cortex; **micro:** **atypical chondrocytes/chondroblasts with multiple nuclei in a lacuna**, hyaline and myxoid lobules growing through cortex into a soft tissue mass |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Osteochondroma: commonest benign, cartilage cap, near the knee, hereditary multiple exostosis (AD).
- Chondroma: hyaline cartilage, hands and feet, central calcification.
- Chondrosarcoma: older, pelvis/shoulder, no osteoid, atypical chondrocytes.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe osteochondroma.</summary>

**Answer:** The most common benign bone tumor; metaphyseal cartilage-capped mushroom-shaped growth from the epiphyseal growth plate near the knee, M:F 3:1; painful if it impinges on nerves or the stalk fractures; rarely becomes chondrosarcoma; multiple lesions occur in autosomal dominant hereditary exostosis.

**Why:** Slides 11-12.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the pathology of a chondroma.</summary>

**Answer:** A benign hyaline cartilage tumor (enchondroma or juxtacortical), < 3 cm, gray-blue and translucent, composed of circumscribed nodules of hyaline cartilage with benign chondrocytes; X-ray shows lucency with central calcification and an intact cortex.

**Why:** Slide 13.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe chondrosarcoma.</summary>

**Answer:** A malignant cartilage-forming tumor without osteoid, peak in the sixth decade, mostly male, in pelvis, spine and shoulder girdle; atypical chondrocytes with multiple nuclei per lacuna; grows through the cortex into soft tissue.

**Why:** Slides 14-15.
</details>

## 4. Fibrous and fibro-osseous lesions

- **Fibrous cortical defect / non-ossifying fibroma:** **benign, likely reactive**, probably **developmental**; common in children (slide: **30–50% of children older than 2**; elsewhere "> 50% of ages 2–25"); **cortex only** = fibrous cortical defect, **medulla** = metaphyseal fibrous defect; **eccentric in the metaphysis of the distal femur and proximal tibia**, **half bilateral/multiple**, usually **< 0.5 cm**; enlarging to **5–6 cm** = **non-ossifying fibroma**. **X-ray:** **sharply demarcated oval radiolucency with long axis parallel to the cortex (most important)** with a thin sclerotic zone. **Micro:** bland fibroblasts in a **storiform (pinwheel)** pattern with macrophages and multinucleated giant cells. **Asymptomatic; most differentiate spontaneously into normal cortical bone.**
- **Fibrous dysplasia:** benign, **localized developmental arrest** (all bone components present but **fail to mature**); **monostotic** vs **polyostotic**; **Mazabraud syndrome** = fibrous dysplasia + **soft tissue myxoma**; **McCune-Albright syndrome** = polyostotic + **café-au-lait pigmentation** + endocrine abnormalities (**precocious puberty**); **somatic gain-of-function mutation in GNAS1**; well-circumscribed intramedullary lesion; **curvilinear woven bone trabeculae ("Chinese letters")** in a fibroblastic stroma **without prominent osteoblastic rimming**.
- Note: slide 18 contains a stray line of Chinese text that is not part of the lecture content.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Fibrous cortical defect: metaphysis of femur/tibia, oval lucency parallel to the cortex, storiform fibroblasts, regresses.
- Fibrous dysplasia: failure of maturation, GNAS1, Chinese-letter trabeculae.
- McCune-Albright = polyostotic + café-au-lait + precocious puberty; Mazabraud = + myxoma.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the fibrous cortical defect and its X-ray.</summary>

**Answer:** A benign developmental/reactive lesion eccentric in the metaphysis of the distal femur or proximal tibia, usually < 0.5 cm; X-ray shows a sharply demarcated oval radiolucency with long axis parallel to the cortex and a thin sclerotic rim; it usually regresses spontaneously.

**Why:** Slides 16-17.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is fibrous dysplasia and what are its syndromes?</summary>

**Answer:** A benign localized developmental arrest where all bone components fail to mature; monostotic or polyostotic; Mazabraud (with soft tissue myxoma) and McCune-Albright (polyostotic, café-au-lait spots, precocious puberty); caused by somatic GNAS1 gain-of-function mutation.

**Why:** Slide 18.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the microscopy of fibrous dysplasia.</summary>

**Answer:** Curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone ("Chinese letters") surrounded by a moderately cellular fibroblastic proliferation, without prominent osteoblastic rimming.

**Why:** Slide 18.
</details>

## 5. Ewing sarcoma

- ⭐ **Malignant undifferentiated cell** neoplasm arising in the **marrow cavity**, later invading **cortex and periosteum** to form a soft tissue mass; member of the **PNET** family; **second most common bone tumor in children** after osteosarcoma.
- **Genetics:** **t(11;22)** producing the **EWS-FLI1 fusion gene** in **95%**.
- **Age 5–20**; **diaphysis of long bones** (femur, pelvis, tibia).
- **Clinical:** pain, **fever, weight loss, leukocytosis, raised ESR** → **mimics osteomyelitis**.
- **X-ray:** destructive **lytic** tumor with reactive periosteal bone, **onion skin**.
- **Gross:** tan-white, hemorrhage and necrosis, ill-defined, medulla and cortex involved, periosteal elevation. **Micro:** **sheets of small round uniform cells** (larger than lymphocytes) with scant clear cytoplasm; **Homer-Wright pseudorosettes** (cells around a central fibrillary space).

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Ewing: t(11;22), EWS-FLI1 (95%), ages 5–20, diaphysis, onion skin.
- Mimics osteomyelitis (fever, leukocytosis, high ESR).
- Small round blue-type cells with Homer-Wright pseudorosettes.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. What is the genetic hallmark of Ewing sarcoma?</summary>

**Answer:** The t(11;22) translocation producing the EWS-FLI1 fusion gene, present in 95% of cases; it belongs to the PNET family.

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the clinical and radiologic features of Ewing sarcoma.</summary>

**Answer:** Ages 5–20, diaphysis of femur, pelvis or tibia; pain, fever, weight loss, leukocytosis and high ESR mimicking osteomyelitis; destructive lytic lesion with onion-skin periosteal reaction.

**Why:** Slides 19-20.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the microscopy of Ewing sarcoma.</summary>

**Answer:** Sheets of small round uniform cells larger than lymphocytes with scant clear cytoplasm and Homer-Wright pseudorosettes.

**Why:** Slide 19.
</details>

## 6. Giant cell tumor and metastatic bone tumors

- **Giant cell tumor:** **locally aggressive**, **almost exclusively adults**, **epiphysis of long bones** (distal femur, proximal tibia), near joints; **micro: lacks bone or cartilage**, numerous **osteoclast-type giant cells with 100 or more nuclei** among uniform oval mononuclear cells.
- ⭐ **Metastatic tumors are the most common malignant bone tumors.** **Spread:** direct extension, lymphatic/hematogenous, **intraspinal seeding**.
- **Adults (> 75%):** **prostate, breast, kidney, lung**. **Children:** **neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma**.
- **Pattern:** **multifocal, axial skeleton, especially vertebral column**; X-ray **lytic, blastic or mixed**; **poor prognosis**. **Metastases slide:** **male → prostate, female → breast; renal and thyroid also seek bone early.**
- *Standard teaching, not on the slides:* prostate metastases are typically **blastic**; breast mixed/lytic; renal and thyroid lytic.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Giant cell tumor: adults, epiphysis, osteoclast-type giant cells, no bone or cartilage.
- Bone metastases are the commonest bone malignancy: prostate, breast, kidney, lung; axial skeleton; lytic, blastic or mixed.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe giant cell tumor of bone.</summary>

**Answer:** A locally aggressive tumor of adults arising in the epiphysis of long bones (distal femur, proximal tibia); sheets of osteoclast-type giant cells with 100 or more nuclei among oval mononuclear cells, lacking bone or cartilage.

**Why:** Slide 21.
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Which tumors metastasize to bone and how?</summary>

**Answer:** In adults prostate, breast, kidney and lung (> 75%); in children neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma; by direct extension, lymphatic/hematogenous spread and intraspinal seeding.

**Why:** Slide 22.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How do bone metastases look and behave?</summary>

**Answer:** Multifocal, in the axial skeleton especially the vertebral column; lytic, blastic or mixed on X-ray; poor prognosis.

**Why:** Slide 22.
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps
- **Night pain relieved by aspirin = osteoid osteoma (< 2 cm); osteoblastoma = spine, > 2 cm, no response to salicylates.**
- **Codman triangle is characteristic but NOT specific for osteosarcoma; osteoid production by malignant cells is essential for diagnosis.**
- **Onion skin + t(11;22) + fever/high ESR = Ewing sarcoma (mimics osteomyelitis).**
- **Osteochondroma: most common benign bone tumor; chondrosarcoma produces NO osteoid.**
- **Giant cell tumor = epiphysis, adults; Ewing = diaphysis; osteosarcoma = metaphysis.**
- **Metastases (prostate, breast, kidney, lung) are the commonest bone malignancy; vertebral column favored.**
- **Fibrous cortical defect: long axis parallel to the cortex; fibrous dysplasia: GNAS1, "Chinese letters".**
