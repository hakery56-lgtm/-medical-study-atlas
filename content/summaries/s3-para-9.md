## 1. Haemoflagellates: introduction and stages

- **Haemoflagellates** = blood and tissue protozoal flagellates of the family **Trypanosomatidae**; major genera: **Leishmania and Trypanosoma.**
- **Binary fission** in both hosts; **no sexual cycle**; **insect vector = intermediate host**, **human = definitive host** (or animal = reservoir host).
- All stages: **central spherical nucleus + kinetoplast** (**parabasal body + blepharoplast**), a **single free flagellum** (from the blepharoplast) and an **undulating membrane.**

| Stage | Key features | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Amastigote** (Leishman bodies) | **Non-motile**, round/oval, **obligate intracellular** | Human/animal cells (Leishmania, T. cruzi) |
| **Promastigote** (Leptomonas) | **Spindle**, **motile** with free flagellum; ⭐ **infective stage of all Leishmania** | Sand fly (saliva, feces), culture |
| **Epimastigote** (Crithidia) | Longer; kinetoplast near nucleus, undulating membrane | Tsetse fly, T. cruzi in reduviid bug; **not in Leishmania** |
| **Trypomastigote** | Long; kinetoplast **posterior**; **extracellular** | Blood/fluids of host, insect gut (Trypanosoma) |
| **Metacyclic trypomastigote (MCT)** | Smaller; **infective stage of Trypanosoma** | Salivary gland (salivary station) / midgut stool (posterior station) |

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Leishmania has only two stages: amastigote (host) and promastigote (sand fly, culture).
- Promastigote = infective; amastigote = intracellular and diagnostic.
- Epimastigote and trypomastigote are not part of the Leishmania cycle.

</div>

## 2. Leishmania: overview and transmission

| Species | Disease |
|---|---|
| **L. donovani** | **Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar, black fever)** |
| **L. tropica** | **Cutaneous leishmaniasis (oriental sore)** |
| **L. braziliensis** | **Muco-cutaneous leishmaniasis (Espundia)** |

- ⭐ **Infective stage: promastigote**; **entry: bite of infected female sand fly**; **diagnostic stage: amastigote**; **definitive host: human**; **intermediate host: female sand fly**; **source: animals and humans.**
- Named for **William Leishman** (discovered L. donovani, 1900-1903; Donovan, 1903); reservoirs: **dogs, foxes, rodents.**
- **Zoonotic; not contagious person to person.** **Lasting immunity** after cure.
- **Transmission:** sand fly bite; **blood transfusion**; **placenta**; contamination of wounds with sand fly **saliva (L. donovani, L. tropica)** or **feces (L. braziliensis).**
- **Tissue preference:** L. tropica = **cutaneous/subcutaneous**; L. braziliensis = **muco-cutaneous**; L. donovani = **RES (liver, spleen, bone marrow, lymph nodes).**
- Dies at **45 °C**; survives years at **-70 °C.** Promastigote in **sand fly and culture**; amastigote in **human and animals.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Three species, three diseases; promastigote infects, amastigote is diagnostic.
- Zoonosis; reservoirs are dogs, foxes and rodents.
- Saliva spreads L. donovani and L. tropica; feces spread L. braziliensis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. List the Leishmania species and the disease each causes.</summary>

**Answer:** L. donovani causes visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar), L. tropica causes cutaneous leishmaniasis, and L. braziliensis causes muco-cutaneous leishmaniasis.

**Why:** Slide "Leishmania: Objective".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How is Leishmania transmitted?</summary>

**Answer:** By the bite of the female sand fly (saliva for L. donovani and L. tropica, feces for L. braziliensis), and also by blood transfusion and through the placenta. It is zoonotic, not contagious from person to person.

**Why:** Slides "Introduction" and "Leishmania" (transmission).
</details>

**Answer:** Infective stage: promastigote; diagnostic stage: amastigote; definitive host: human; intermediate host: female sand fly; source of infection: animals and humans.

**Why:** Slide "Leishmania: Objective".
</details>

## 3. Life cycle

**In humans (definitive host)**
1. Infected female sand fly bites and injects **promastigotes** (saliva) — **L. braziliensis**: promastigotes in **feces** placed on the skin.
2. Promastigotes are **engulfed by macrophages** → become **amastigotes within 24 hours.**
3. They fuse with **lysosomes → phagolysosomes** that **inhibit macrophage function** (and/or secrete protective acids).
4. Amastigotes **multiply by binary fission** until the cell holds **more than 200** and **ruptures.**
5. Released amastigotes **reinfect macrophages (internal autoinfection)** or reach **peripheral blood** and are **sucked up by a sand fly.** Takes **1-3 months.**

**In the female sand fly**
1. Amastigotes reach the **midgut** → become **promastigotes**, enclosed by a **membrane/fluid** secreted by midgut epithelium.
2. They multiply by **binary fission**; the membrane **ruptures** and promastigotes swim in the fluid.
3. **L. donovani and L. tropica:** reach the **mouth cavity (salivary gland)**; **L. braziliensis:** reach the **end of the midgut (feces).**
4. **10 days** to the **active promastigote**; whole sand fly cycle **about 20 days.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Promastigote → amastigote in macrophages within 24 hours; phagolysosome protects the parasite.
- More than 200 amastigotes fill the macrophage before it ruptures.
- Sand fly: 10 days to infectivity; about 20 days for the cycle.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the life cycle of Leishmania in humans.</summary>

**Answer:** Promastigotes inoculated by the sand fly are engulfed by macrophages and become amastigotes within 24 hours. They multiply by binary fission until the cell ruptures (more than 200 amastigotes), then reinfect other macrophages or enter the peripheral blood to be taken up by a sand fly. The cycle takes 1-3 months.

**Why:** Slides "Life cycle in human body".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. How does Leishmania avoid destruction inside the macrophage?</summary>

**Answer:** It fuses with lysosomes to form phagolysosomes that inhibit the macrophage's function, and the promastigote may secrete special acids that cover and protect it from cellular immunity.

**Why:** Slide "Life cycle" (phagolysosomes).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. Describe the life cycle in the sand fly and the differences between species.</summary>

**Answer:** Ingested amastigotes become promastigotes in the midgut, multiply inside a membrane, are released, and within 10 days become infective; L. donovani and L. tropica move to the mouth cavity/salivary gland, L. braziliensis to the end of the midgut (feces). The cycle takes about 20 days.

**Why:** Slides "Life cycle in female sand flies".
</details>

## 4. Leishmania donovani — kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis)

- **Found by Leishman (1900) and Donovan (1903)** in spleen smears; **India, Sudan, eastern Africa, China, Red Sea littorals**; **endemic in young children** with **malnutrition and immune suppression.**
- **Kala-azar** = skin becomes **dry, rough, dark.**
- **Multiplies in macrophages** (liver, spleen, bone marrow) → **RES blockage.** **Incubation ~1-3 months.**
- **Onset insidious:** low-grade intermittent fever and sweating → **high intermittent fever**, sometimes **double rise in 24 hours.**
- ⭐ **Hepatosplenomegaly:** **Kupffer and endothelial cells parasitized, hepatocytes spared**; spleen may reach the **iliac fossa** (solid, painless).
- **Anemia:** **complement-mediated hemolysis (immune complexes)**, **bone marrow infection**, **hypersplenism.**
- **Spleen:** **thickened capsule (perisplenitis)**, **soft and friable, no fibrosis**, **red/chocolate** cut section. **Bone marrow:** infiltrated by parasitized macrophages.
- **Liver:** **prothrombin decreased**, dilated sinusoids, fatty degeneration, **nutmeg** appearance.
- **Complications:** septicemia, secondary bacterial infection (pneumonia), **splenic rupture**, late edema, cachexia, hyperpigmentation; **lymphadenopathy common in Africa.**
- **PKDL (post kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis):** after cure; **hypopigmented/erythematous macules or nodules**, especially around **mouth and nose**, **non-ulcerative.**
- **Note:** the slide says hemoglobin levels were "significantly increased" in severe kala-azar anemia; this is a slip (anemia means decreased hemoglobin). No exam question is built on it.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Kala-azar = hepatosplenomegaly, fever, anemia, wasting, dark skin.
- Hepatocytes spared; Kupffer cells parasitized; spleen is soft, no fibrosis.
- PKDL appears after cure around the mouth and nose.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Describe the clinical features of kala-azar.</summary>

**Answer:** After an incubation of about 1-3 months, insidious low-grade intermittent fever and sweating progress to high intermittent fever (sometimes a double rise in 24 hours), with hepatosplenomegaly, anemia, wasting and increased pigmentation if untreated.

**Why:** Slides "Pathogenicity and clinical features" (L. donovani).
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Explain the causes of anemia and the organ changes in kala-azar.</summary>

**Answer:** Anemia results from complement-mediated hemolysis by antigen-antibody complexes, bone marrow infection and hypersplenism. The spleen is enlarged, soft, friable with thickened capsule and chocolate-colored cut surface; the liver has nutmeg appearance and reduced prothrombin; the marrow is infiltrated by parasitized macrophages.

**Why:** Slides on spleen, bone marrow and liver.
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What is post kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis?</summary>

**Answer:** After treatment and recovery from kala-azar some patients develop hypopigmented or erythematous macules or nodular, non-ulcerative lesions, especially on the face around the mouth and nose, usually without systemic symptoms.

**Why:** Slide "Post kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis".
</details>

## 5. Leishmania tropica — cutaneous leishmaniasis (oriental sore)

- **Discovered** by **Cunningham (1885), Borovsky (1898), Wright (1903, named it).**
- Names: **oriental sore, Baghdad boil, Hallab boil, Delhi boil, Tehran boil**; **urban cutaneous leishmaniasis.**

| | **L. tropica major** | **L. tropica minor** |
|---|---|---|
| Lesion | **Wet** oriental sore | **Dry** oriental sore |
| Setting | **Rural** | **Urban** |
| Incubation | **1-6 weeks** | **2-6 months** |
| Size | **3-5 cm** | **1-3 cm** |
| Reservoir | **Gerbil (small rodent)** | **Dog** |

- **Vector:** female sand fly (**Phlebotomus**); life cycle like L. donovani.
- **Lesion:** after **2-8 months**, on an **exposed area**: **itching small red painless papule** with erythema, growing to **1-2 cm** (strong immune and cellular response).
- **Wet type:** shallow **ulcer healing in 3-12 months** with a **depressed mottled scar**; **secondary bacterial infection** → pus (center), **bad odor**, pain, **lymphangitis and lymphadenitis.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Major = wet, rural, gerbil, 1-6 weeks; minor = dry, urban, dog, 2-6 months.
- Painless papule becomes an ulcer healing with a scar.
- Secondary infection causes pain, odor, lymphangitis and lymphadenitis.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Compare L. tropica major and minor.</summary>

**Answer:** Major causes wet oriental sore in rural areas, incubation 1-6 weeks, lesion 3-5 cm, reservoir gerbil; minor causes dry oriental sore in urban areas, incubation 2-6 months, lesion 1-3 cm, reservoir dog.

**Why:** Slides "Leishmania tropica major / minor".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the course of a cutaneous leishmaniasis lesion.</summary>

**Answer:** After an incubation of weeks to months an itching, small, red, painless papule with erythema appears on an exposed area and grows to 1-2 cm; in the wet type it becomes a shallow ulcer that heals in 3-12 months leaving a depressed mottled scar.

**Why:** Slides "Pathogenicity and clinical features" (L. tropica).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What does secondary bacterial infection add?</summary>

**Answer:** The lesion fills with pus (mainly centrally), has a bad odor and becomes painful, with lymphangitis and lymphadenitis along the lymphatic channels.

**Why:** Slide on wet cutaneous leishmaniasis.
</details>

## 6. Leishmania braziliensis — muco-cutaneous leishmaniasis (Espundia)

- **Isolated in Brazil** by **Lindenberg (1909)**; **Carini (1911)** named it. **Espundia** = **spongy** lesion around **mouth, nose, pharynx, external ear.**
- **Vector:** sand fly **Lutzomyia**; **transmitted via feces.** **Reservoir:** **rodents and domestic animals.**
- **Metastatic mucocutaneous lesions 1-4 years** after the cutaneous lesion heals.
- **Amastigotes** in macrophages of **skin and mucous membranes** (nose, mouth), **RE cells and lymphoid tissue of skin only — not internal organs.**
- **Lesion:** small **spongy swelling → ulcer** spreading sideways and deeper, **open at the center** with **semi-liquid necrotic discharge**; **destroys skin, muscle, cartilage (nose, ear) and bone**; may leave a **depressed circular scar.**

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- L. braziliensis = Espundia; spongy mucosal lesions; Brazil; Lutzomyia; feces.
- Mucocutaneous metastasis 1-4 years after skin lesion heals.
- Destroys cartilage and bone of nose and ear; no visceral involvement.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. Why is muco-cutaneous leishmaniasis called Espundia?</summary>

**Answer:** Because the lesion is spongy in texture around the mouth, nose, pharynx and external ear.

**Why:** Slide "Leishmania braziliensis: Introduction".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. Describe the pathology and course of L. braziliensis infection.</summary>

**Answer:** A small spongy swelling breaks into an ulcer that extends sideways and deeper and stays open at the center discharging necrotic material, destroying skin, muscle, cartilage of the nose and ear and bone; metastatic lesions appear one to four years after the skin lesion heals.

**Why:** Slides "Pathogenicity and clinical features" (L. braziliensis).
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. What are the vector, source and tissue distribution of L. braziliensis?</summary>

**Answer:** The vector is the sand fly Lutzomyia; the source and reservoir are rodents and domestic animals; amastigotes are found in macrophages of skin and mucous membranes and lymphoid tissue of skin only, not in internal organs.

**Why:** Slides "Introduction" and the following slide.

## 7. Laboratory diagnosis, treatment and prophylaxis

| Method | Details |
|---|---|
| **Direct smear** | **Amastigotes** in **Leishman/Giemsa** smears of **spleen, liver, lymph node, bone marrow aspirates** (kala-azar); **scrape the EDGE of the ulcer (not the center)** for L. tropica / L. braziliensis |
| **Skin test (Montenegro)** | Helps diagnosis; **negative in diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis** (appears after 3 months of symptoms) |
| **Culture** | **NNN medium, 24 °C, 7 days** → **promastigotes** |
| **Serology** | **IFT, ELISA**; **limited value in L. tropica** (no detectable antibodies) |
| **Formol-gel (aldehyde) test** | **Kala-azar**: **jellification** from binding of **serum globulins to formaldehyde** |
| **Molecular amplification** | **Most sensitive** test available |

- **Treatment:** ⭐ **pentavalent antimonials** = drug of choice, **20 mg/kg IM or IV for 20-30 days**; **pentamidine** if L. tropica is resistant; topical **10% charcoal in sulfuric acid** (as written on the slide) or **liquid nitrogen**; **sodium stibogluconate** also treats mucocutaneous disease; **L. braziliensis:** same as kala-azar **+ amphotericin B** (best alternative).
- **Prophylaxis:** **early detection and treatment**, **kill the female sand fly**, **protective clothing and repellents**; **no vaccine** against kala-azar.

<div class="key-points">

**Key points**
- Diagnose by amastigotes in smears (edge of ulcer); culture shows promastigotes.
- Pentavalent antimonials first; pentamidine or amphotericin B alternatives.
- No vaccine: prevent by sand fly control and personal protection.

</div>

**Short-essay questions**

<details><summary>Q1. How is leishmaniasis diagnosed in the laboratory?</summary>

**Answer:** Direct demonstration of amastigotes in Leishman or Giemsa stained smears (aspirates of spleen, liver, lymph node, bone marrow; scraping from the edge of an ulcer), culture on NNN medium at 24 °C for 7 days showing promastigotes, the Montenegro skin test, serology (IFT, ELISA), the formol-gel test for kala-azar and molecular assays (most sensitive).

**Why:** Slides "Laboratory diagnosis".
</details>

<details><summary>Q2. What is the treatment of leishmaniasis?</summary>

**Answer:** Pentavalent antimonials (20 mg/kg IM or IV for 20-30 days) are the drug of choice; pentamidine for resistant L. tropica; topical agents or liquid nitrogen for skin lesions; sodium stibogluconate for mucocutaneous disease; amphotericin B is the best alternative for L. braziliensis.

**Why:** Slide "Treatment".
</details>

<details><summary>Q3. How is leishmaniasis prevented?</summary>

**Answer:** Early detection and treatment of all cases, killing the female sand fly, protective clothing and insect repellents. There is no vaccine against kala-azar.

**Why:** Slide "Prophylaxis".
</details>

## ⚠️ Exam traps

- **Promastigote = infective; amastigote = diagnostic and intracellular.**
- **Epimastigote is NOT present in Leishmania.**
- **L. donovani/L. tropica: saliva; L. braziliensis: feces (and promastigotes end in the midgut).**
- **Hepatocytes are NOT parasitized in kala-azar; Kupffer cells are.**
- **Oriental sore specimen: scrape the EDGE, not the center.**
- **Major = wet, rural, gerbil; minor = dry, urban, dog.**
- **L. braziliensis amastigotes are in skin/mucosa only, not internal organs.**
